# Clinician's Quick Guide to Interpersonal Psychotherapy — digest

## What this book is for the lens

IPT's mechanism thesis: depression (and much relational distress) arises in a current interpersonal context, and solving ONE current life problem — grief, role dispute, role transition, or isolation — both lifts symptoms and builds social skills that lower future vulnerability. For the attachment lens this is the canonical procedure manual: it converts attachment disruption into a bounded, solvable, here-and-now problem with session-by-session mechanics (mood↔event linking, communication analysis, role-play, a fixed time limit). It beats other lens sources on three things: per-adaptation evidence honesty (a 0–4 star scale with negative trials reported plainly), concrete named worksheets and handouts as navigation anchors, and a strictly state-based stance — it never types people, so it cannot reproduce the categorical-typology error for which the lens excludes Attached (Levine/Heller).

## Core model

- Depression has three parts — symptoms, social/interpersonal life, personality — and IPT deliberately treats only the first two; apparent personality (dependency, irritability, passivity) is often the depressive state itself (Ch. 1, Ch. 9).
- Four focal problem areas: grief (complicated bereavement after an actual death only), interpersonal disputes (Ch. 4, Handout 4.1), role transitions (Ch. 5), interpersonal deficits/isolation (Ch. 6, Handouts 6.1–6.2 — the residual, weakest-fitting category). Choose exactly ONE.
- Initial phase ≤3 visits, seven tasks (Ch. 2): diagnose with a scale; psychoeducation; medication evaluation; interpersonal inventory (Handout 2.3); formulation tying illness to one focal area; treatment contract with exact session count; grant the sick role. Physical exam (esp. over 50) rules out somatic causes.
- Session engine (middle phase): two-question opener → one recent affect-laden incident → communication analysis / decision analysis / affect work → role-play → real-life attempt → debrief → summary linking work to the depression.
- IPT is defined by its strategies (problem areas, medical model, outside-the-office focus), not its techniques — the techniques are generic tools (Ch. 8). Therapist stance: warm, active, non-neutral advocate; no dream/transference interpretation, no childhood excavation, no formal homework.
- Termination is itself a role transition (Ch. 7); high-risk responders get a new explicit maintenance contract (Ch. 10–11).
- Adaptation framework (Ch. 10): keep a fixed time limit, respect clinician experience, check the star rating — 4 = ≥2 RCTs superior to control; 3 = ≥1 RCT or equivalence to an established treatment; 2 = open/pilot; 1 = untested; 0 = negative findings.
- Adaptations (Ch. 12–24): perinatal, adolescents (Mufson), older adults, medical illness, dysthymia (IPT-D), bipolar (IPSRT + Social Rhythm Metric), bulimia, anxiety/PTSD, BPD (experimental), cross-cultural (Uganda group IPT), group/conjoint/telephone formats, IPC (simplified ≤6-session counseling).

## Moves

Deduplicated across all chunks; ordered by tier, then evidence strength. "Method-level" = supported only as part of the full IPT package in RCTs.
### [ATT:MOODLINK] Mood–event linking
**Move:** Open every review of a period with two questions: how did the week go — if a mood is reported, ask what happened around it; if an event is reported, ask how it felt. Anchor all work to one concrete, affect-laden incident from the past week (demand "for example?" whenever talk goes abstract); a daily mood–event diary extends the same link between sessions.
**APPLY WHEN:** Every session after the first; any mid-week mood swing with no obvious cause; jargon, generalities, or theory-reading replacing lived material.
**AVOID WHEN:** Letting sessions drift into unstructured talk — the mood↔interpersonal-event link IS the intervention; treating the link as proof of causation (IPT claims connection, not cause).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Heart of the IPT session engine; method-level across IPT RCTs.
### [ATT:SCALE-TRACK] Serial symptom scale
**Move:** Pick one depression scale (Ham-D clinician-rated or Beck Depression Inventory self-report), administer at baseline and every 3–4 weeks until remission (Ham-D < 8); explain what the score means and mark milestones aloud (score halved, remission crossed). Seeing symptoms itemized reframes them as illness, not personal flaws.
**APPLY WHEN:** Intake and throughout any depression treatment, or structured self-monitoring alongside one.
**AVOID WHEN:** Treating Ham-D as diagnostic (it measures severity only); score-watching as a reassurance ritual; suicide items endorsed → clinician involvement now, not continued self-tracking.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Standard measurement practice across all IPT trials; method-level.
### [ATT:SICKROLE] Sick role and the two-illness frame
**Move:** Grant the sick role: depression is a treatable medical illness, not the person's fault or will — what it blocks is excused (nobody blames themselves for the flu), while the patient takes on an active duty to work at recovery. With comorbid medical illness, frame depression as a second, independent, treatable illness — both need care, either can kill, neither is the person's fault.
**APPLY WHEN:** Self-blame, guilt over reduced functioning, hopelessness read as truth; depression discounted as an understandable reaction to diabetes/cancer/HIV (treating depression in HIV relieved fatigue/insomnia both parties had blamed on HIV).
**AVOID WHEN:** It slides into passivity (the role explicitly includes working as a patient); delusional guilt themes; new late-life or somatically suspicious onset — medical workup first.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core IPT component; HIV adaptation (Markowitz 1998); IPT in medical patients rated 4 stars; method-level.
### [ATT:INVENTORY] Onset timeline + interpersonal inventory
**Move:** Build a timeline tying symptom onset to interpersonal events (Ch. 1 nine-question set: what was changing — death, dispute, move, job, illness — who is involved, supports, estrangements, remaining strengths). Then the interpersonal inventory (Handout 2.3): list key people; per relationship — interaction quality, each side's expectations and whether met, satisfying vs unsatisfying aspects, wanted changes, confidants, repeating patterns. Graphic variant: closeness circle — self at center, markers at distances matching felt closeness (Mufson adolescent manual).
**APPLY WHEN:** Start of any episode review; before choosing a focus; when the presenting complaint may be the tip of the iceberg (children named first, spouse's affair surfacing later).
**AVOID WHEN:** Rushed — a shallow inventory produces mid-course surprises and a wrong focus; no precipitant found is fine (consequences of the depression — strained ties, lost jobs — also qualify as a focus).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Standard IPT intake; therapists reliably agree on focus choice (Markowitz et al., 2000); method-level.
### [ATT:FORMULATE] One-focus contract with an exact endpoint
**Move:** One plain statement linking the problem to a single focal area ("the depression seems tied to X; spend the next N weeks solving X; solving it should lift the depression"), explicit agreement, and an exact session count — precisely 12, 14, or 16, never a range; the exact number matters less than that there is one. Consecutive weekly sessions for momentum; plan vacations upfront, make up cancellations. Tie each week's events back to the agreed focus; tolerate digressions briefly, return; change focus only explicitly.
**APPLY WHEN:** End of the initial phase (≤3 visits); starting any bounded change effort; harnessing deadline pressure.
**AVOID WHEN:** Choosing 2+ foci — dilution means no focus at all; pushing your formulation over the person's alternative; saying "interpersonal deficits" to a patient (say loneliness or isolation); mechanically suppressing genuinely new material (a fresh mid-course loss justifies an explicit, bounded extension).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Formulation method (Markowitz & Swartz, 1997, 2006); the time limit itself never tested against open-ended IPT; method-level.
### [ATT:COMM-ANALYSIS] Communication analysis
**Move:** Reconstruct one important conversation utterance by utterance — what was said, felt, intended at each step. Flag disjunctions between feeling and action (felt angry, said nothing — did the silence shape the bad outcome?), indirect substitutes for speech (sulking, going mute instead of stating grievances), mind-reading assumptions ("they should just know"), depression-distorted readings of others, and mismatches between words and affect (flat retelling of a betrayal, smiling through an argument story). Let the person draw conclusions before offering alternatives, then role-play the better version.
**APPLY WHEN:** Recurring fights; messages landing wrong; any dispute or disappointing exchange; a silence/sulking style replacing voiced complaints.
**AVOID WHEN:** Skipping validation — anger and sadness must first be normalized as legitimate signals; wielding contradictions as gotchas; imposing one's own cultural communication norms as the standard.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Signature IPT technique; method-level, no dismantling trial.
### [ATT:AFFECT-ELICIT] Elicit, normalize, then decide about expression
**Move:** Draw out feelings around key events in a safe setting, normalize them ("most people would feel this; the anger makes sense"), and stay with strong emotion rather than rushing past — until it becomes tolerable and informative. Feelings are powerful but only feelings: data about interpersonal events one can learn to express and steer. Only then weigh whether/how/to whom to express them outside, and rehearse first. Also practice expressing warmth and gratitude, not just anger.
**APPLY WHEN:** Stuck decisions with an unacknowledged feeling layer; guilt over negative feelings; habitual affect avoidance; emotion arising over current relationships.
**AVOID WHEN:** Raw venting into fragile relationships (in-session expression is not a template for outside); repetitive angry/sad outbursts — break the pattern, explore other channels; overwhelming trauma-linked affect or dissociation — grounding first, therapist territory.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core across IPT protocols; method-level.
### [ATT:ASSERT-VALID] Needs into words, channel chosen to fit
**Move:** Validate needs and anger as normal interpersonal signals, then convert feeling into a deliverable statement: unvoiced needs go unmet, others expect self-advocacy, unexpressed annoyance guarantees the behavior continues. Draft actual words ("what might you say? does that say what you want? how comfortable saying no?"), fine-tune tone, start in lower-stakes settings (defining work hours) before riskier ones (dates). Choose the expression channel acceptable in context: direct statement (Western default), indirect signals, trusted intermediaries (relatives, a doctor, a healer), or reframing the plea around what the other party values (Ch. 22).
**APPLY WHEN:** Puts everyone's needs first; sees anger as bad or selfish; answers conflict with silence; self-erasing accommodation → helplessness → symptomatic soothing; cross-cultural or hierarchical disputes where direct confrontation reads as disrespect.
**AVOID WHEN:** Overcorrecting into constant self-assertion — that spoils relationships too; realistic retaliation risk — safety planning first; indirection becoming chronic avoidance of a resolvable dispute; depression makes expression harder — acknowledge that.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level; channel tactics are field adaptations from the Uganda group RCT (Bolton 2003).
### [ATT:ROLEPLAY] Rehearse → try live → debrief
**Move:** Rehearse the encounter aloud, tone of voice included; play the other party to hear how one comes across; iterate until words match intent. Separately rehearse contingencies — what to say to yourself/others if anxiety symptoms hit mid-performance, labeling them symptoms, not personal defects. Then run it for real and debrief four-part: congratulate the courage to risk the encounter; link the mood shift to how it went; reinforce the adaptive maneuvers used; if it went badly, commiserate and explore alternatives ("what did you like about your handling? what next?").
**APPLY WHEN:** Assertions, confrontations, dating/social approaches, unfamiliar situations; before any transgression confrontation; after every real-life attempt.
**AVOID WHEN:** Rehearsal loops replacing the real conversation; mistaking a polished script for control over the other's response; stealing credit — out-of-session work is what builds mastery and independence.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Used across IPT trials; method-level. Fairburn's ban on role-play in bulimia trials was a design artifact to keep IPT distinct from CBT — the authors say do NOT drop it in practice.
### [ATT:DECISION-ANALYSIS] Options with the person in the lead
**Move:** Name the wanted outcome, generate the full option list, walk each option's consequences before acting — via gentle questioning until the person names feasible alternatives themselves, so they get the credit and the sense of competence. Explicitly teachable as a life skill.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any interpersonal problem with choices; passivity defaulting into non-decision; claims of "tried everything / nothing works" — rarely true; prior attempts were often half-hearted or options overlooked through discouragement.
**AVOID WHEN:** Supplying the answers yourself — it makes the helper look good and the person feel incompetent; analysis becoming a substitute for acting.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Generic technique within the method-level package; authors' clinical guidance.
### [ATT:DISPUTE-STAGE] Stage the dispute, map the expectations
**Move:** A role dispute = nonreciprocal expectations with a significant other, escalated into open or silent struggle. Stage it and let strategy follow stage: renegotiation (parties actively in contact) → improve communication; impasse (talk stopped, silent treatment, smoldering resignation) → deliberately reopen the conflict; dissolution (relationship irretrievable) → treat the ending as a role transition with mourning. Map the expectations (Handout 4.1): what each side wants, how each has disappointed the other, what has been tried, remaining options for both, realistic likelihood of change, resources, similar past disputes.
**APPLY WHEN:** Dispute chosen as focus; dispute presented as impossible and impassible (some solution usually exists); unclear why nothing moves.
**AVOID WHEN:** Expecting reopened impasses to feel good — open disagreement may increase first, by design; assuming the fault is all one side's. Even failed renegotiation teaches communication skill and relocates blame realistically — sometimes the problem is the partner's unwillingness to change.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level; role dispute is the most common focus in depressed outpatients.
### [ATT:TRANS-NAME] Name the role transition
**Move:** Define which role ended and which began; the label itself reassures — a transition, unlike chaos or free fall, is something one adjusts to and controls.
**APPLY WHEN:** Depression follows any life change, including positive, sought-after ones (dream house, promotion, retirement, new baby, illness, financial shift); "disputes" that are really shifts (a boss-conflict that was actually a changing company; a "marriage problem" that was already over).
**AVOID WHEN:** A death caused the loss (grief focus); a live struggle with one specific person dominates (dispute — choose by the key issue: change vs struggle; if either frame fits, pick the one less threatening to the person).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level.
### [ATT:TRANS-WORK] Mourn the old role, build the new
**Move:** Five tasks (Ch. 5): give up the old role; mourn it (let sadness, guilt, anger, fear be spoken); acquire new skills; build new attachments and supports; recognize what the new role offers. Correct the depressed memory split (idyllic-before / traumatic-change / dreadful-now — it reflects mood more than reality) with a balanced audit: what was good AND bad about the old role, hard AND promising about the new; hunt for genuine positives even in bad events (survivor identity, sharpened value of time). Sequence confidence-building: the structured work arena (built-in job description, clear roles) before the uncharted social/dating arena.
**APPLY WHEN:** Nostalgia ("things were fine then"); guilt about grieving a "positive" change; gratitude used to silence complaint; feeling unprepared or incompetent in the new role; new rewards seeming worthless at first.
**AVOID WHEN:** The transition itself is traumatic (violence, life-threatening diagnosis) and detailed revisiting floods — therapist territory; drifting into vocational counseling — target the feelings blocking adjustment, not job placement; work itself being the acute threat (abusive environment — no safe first arena there).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level; the five-task map is the authors' framework.
### [ATT:GRIEF-SCREEN] Screen complicated grief, then staged reengagement
**Move:** Screen (Handout 3.1): death timing vs symptom onset, ability to talk about the deceased, avoidance of funeral/grave, preserved possessions, fear of having the same illness, self-blame about the death, supports available at the time. Telltales: the person never mentions the deceased or the circumstances; anniversary worsening; delayed/absent mourning; somatic presentations; adopting the deceased's illness. As mood lifts: staged reengagement — resume old pleasurable activities, contact one friend, test new relationships; debrief each attempt (what was done, what was enjoyable, what was hard, would you repeat it).
**APPLY WHEN:** Any significant death in history plus current depression; later-phase grief work after affect has moved.
**AVOID WHEN:** Pathologizing normal mourning — sadness, insomnia, reduced functioning resolving over a few months is adaptive and must not be discouraged; unaddressed guilt about improving ("if I really loved them I could never recover") or abandonment fears about new relationships — discuss the fear before the attempt; excessive guilt and suicidal ideation are NOT normal grief — they signal complication.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical heuristics within method-level grief-focus trials.
### [ATT:WHY-BETTER] Close the effort: name the gain, hand over the credit
**Move:** At the contracted end, re-administer the symptom scale, name the gain (remission = Ham-D < 8), then ask the person to explain why they improved — steering credit to their own out-of-session work — and rehearse applying the new skills to upcoming situations. Label end-of-therapy or end-of-relationship sadness as the normal bittersweet feeling of a role transition, categorically distinct from returning depression.
**APPLY WHEN:** Closing any bounded change effort with measurable improvement; endings of therapy, mentorship, collaborations; wobbly weeks right after improvement.
**AVOID WHEN:** No real improvement — forced credit rings hollow (use ATT:NONRESPONSE-REFRAME); the scale shows actual symptom return — treat as relapse risk, not normal sadness; reluctance to end — prescribe a several-month waiting period, not automatic continuation.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Termination phase of IPT RCT protocols; method-level.
### [ATT:NONRESPONSE-REFRAME] Blame the treatment, not the person
**Move:** When treatment failed, apply the medical model to the failure itself: the treatment failed, not the person. Cite that only ~2/3 respond to a first medication course and most nonresponders respond to a later one; pivot to concrete alternatives — add/switch medication, another therapy or therapist, a renegotiated contract.
**APPLY WHEN:** <50% symptom reduction after ~12 weeks/sessions; self-labeling as a treatment failure.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used to console instead of actually changing course; drifting on with a non-working treatment past the contracted end.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical guidance + pharmacotherapy response literature. Documented failure mode it prevents: nonresponders self-blame and quit all treatment.
### [ATT:STATE-NOT-TRAIT] Illness, not personality
**Move:** Acute branch: attribute apparent character flaws mid-episode (passivity, touchiness, avoidance, self-described defectiveness, lateness) to the depressive state — acute depression mimics Cluster C personality disorders; defer any verdict on "real personality" until mood remits. Chronic branch: years-long low mood or social anxiety fused with identity is a treatable illness so chronic it masquerades as personality (dysthymia; "role insecurity" replaces the pejorative "interpersonal deficits"); define starting treatment as an iatrogenic role transition into health — learn what the illness is vs who one is, and practice handling situations the way the healthy version would (a martyr's total selflessness guarantees unmet needs).
**APPLY WHEN:** Self-condemnation mid-episode; tempted to self-diagnose a personality problem while depressed; "I've always been like this"; chronic demoralization about social functioning; success feeling fraudulent, intimacy avoided for fear of being found defective.
**AVOID WHEN:** Dismissing stable patterns clearly visible outside episodes; dismissing real external problems as "just the illness"; selling the reframe as a stand-alone cure for dysthymia — IPT monotherapy earned 0 stars, medication is first-line (combination 2 stars); plan months of continuation for the euthymic identity to consolidate.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Personality did not predict short-term IPT outcome (Zuckerman 1980); PD patients improved equally (Markowitz 1998); apparent traits recede over maintenance (Cyranowski 2004); social phobia: open trial (Lipsitz 1999), group RCT IPT ≈ CBT (Hoffart 2005).
### [ATT:BINGE-CONTEXT] Interpersonal context of the symptom urge
**Move:** When the urge/symptom topic comes up, redirect from symptom talk to its interpersonal trigger: what was I feeling right before, what had just happened or was about to happen interpersonally? Normalize the feeling (disappointment, anger), then look for the recurring feeling→context pattern. In the bulimia adaptation, food/eating/body-image talk is deliberately off-limits — bulimic patients need no reminding they're ill, and symptom talk is their default avoidance channel.
**APPLY WHEN:** A repetitive symptom or habit obscures its own interpersonal triggers; the person labels their feelings "weird" or abnormal.
**AVOID WHEN:** Anorexia nervosa as the primary condition (no benefit, McIntosh 2005); acute medical risk from the eating disorder needing direct management; conditions where the person already over-identifies with the diagnosis (don't keep telling a bulimic patient they're ill — that psychoeducation move is for depression).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Bulimia rated 4 stars (≥2 RCTs, individual + group; Fairburn trials); IPT slower than CBT but caught up in 2 studies, inferior in a third (Agras 2000).
### [ATT:SRM] Social rhythm regularity
**Move:** Keep a daily Social Rhythm Metric grid — wake time, first human contact, meals, bedtime, how stimulating each anchor is; review weekly for regularity, identify disruptors; protect sleep above all (evening wind-down, no late caffeine/alcohol, fixed sleep-wake hours, no all-nighters). Sleep loss commonly triggers mania.
**APPLY WHEN:** Bipolar spectrum on a mood stabilizer; schedule-driven mood instability.
**AVOID WHEN:** As bipolar monotherapy — untested without medication; once mania has begun — IPSRT prevents mania, it does not treat it.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** IPSRT RCT (Frank 2005, n=175 bipolar I; 3 stars, adjunct to mood stabilizers): greater rhythm regularization in the acute phase predicted less recurrence; benefit appeared as longer maintenance-phase euthymia, not faster stabilization.
### [ATT:TRANSGRESSION] Name the transgression
**Move:** When a significant other broke a clear social code (affair, cruelty, physical harm), name it a transgression entitling one to redress — at minimum an apology. Then run: validate the feeling → scan options for expressing it → role-play the encounter with the right tone.
**APPLY WHEN:** A passive, guilt-prone person cannot mobilize legitimate anger in a role dispute.
**AVOID WHEN:** Ongoing danger or an abusive partner — confrontation can escalate and needs professional safety planning; when it feeds grievance rumination instead of one bounded action.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical heuristic; patients reported feeling liberated/vindicated (clinical observation only).
### [ATT:DEFICIT-REBUILD] Isolation: pattern review + graduated contact
**Move:** Review past significant relationships good and bad, extract recurring strengths and breakdown patterns, discuss feelings about all current contacts (Handouts 6.1–6.2: contact-frequency counts, what makes closeness hard, the party thought-experiment — a room of strangers, what exactly would you do). Then graduated contact: recontact an old friend or one trusted person, enter low-stakes social settings; extensive role-play beforehand (these patients need much more of it); debrief every encounter step by step. Uniquely for IPT, past relationships and the helping relationship itself may serve as material.
**APPLY WHEN:** Isolation/loneliness focus — chosen only when no death, no dispute, no transition exists (residual category; 4 subtypes: long-standing isolates, many-but-shallow relationships, chronically depressed, social phobia).
**AVOID WHEN:** Reading a personality disorder off an episode snapshot — apparent traits recede when depression lifts; framing practice as formal homework (non-compliance makes people feel like "bad patients" and predicts dropout — keep it an invitation); using the label "interpersonal deficits" with the person — it insults.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Weakest IPT category by the book's own admission; no controlled data specific to this focus; the "such patients do better in CBT or long-term treatment" claim is flagged by the book itself as unsupported. A "deficits" case can often be recast as a role transition (college→adulthood) — more palatable, equally workable.
### [ATT:MAINT-CONTRACT] Maintenance contract
**Move:** After acute response in a high-risk patient (multiple prior episodes, high residual symptoms), negotiate a new explicit, time-limited maintenance contract — monthly sessions are the best-tested dose, frequency adjustable — with freedom to shift focal area as new life events arrive, renegotiated at each term's end. Goals shift from symptom reduction to relapse prevention; social skills keep growing during the year after acute treatment (Weissman 1981).
**APPLY WHEN:** Recurrent depression; responder with residual symptoms; pregnancy/lactation; medication intolerance or refusal.
**AVOID WHEN:** Single episode with few residual symptoms — advice-and-discharge may suffice; age >70 — monthly IPT alone was inferior to medication (Reynolds 2006); open-ended drift — maintenance is still a contract. Post-response euthymia feels fragile for weeks–months (Markowitz 1993) — fragility is not failure.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** 4 stars — Frank 1990/1991; Reynolds 1999. Tested doses: weekly ×6 months (continuation), monthly up to 3 years.
### [ATT:SUICIDE-INQUIRY] Graded suicide inquiry
**Move:** Ask directly, in graded sequence: life not worth living → death wishes → thoughts of self-harm → plan, means, past attempts, ability to stay safe and to alert someone. Weigh lethality, supports, alliance; escalate (second opinion, notify a minor's parents, ER/hospitalization) when a plan is clear or safety cannot be guaranteed. Frame suicidal thoughts as symptoms of a good-prognosis illness: stay alive long enough for treatment to work.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any depression evaluation, especially adolescents (elevated attempt risk); suicide-scale items endorsed.
**AVOID WHEN:** As solo self-assessment — risk triage is clinical work.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Standard protocol across IPT trials; suicide is depression's greatest risk; primary-care IPT for SSRI-refusers reduced suicidal ideation over 1 year, significant by 4 months (Bruce 2004).
### [ATT:MED-EVAL] Medication decision
**Move:** Evaluate medication need by symptom severity, patient preference, treatment history, and medical contraindications; combine treatments for severe presentations; IPT can be added to ongoing medication as augmentation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Severe vegetative symptoms; Ham-D in the high 20s–30s; high suicide risk (medication acts faster); psychotic features (no psychotherapy alone suffices — medication or ECT); non-response to therapy alone; dysthymia (medication first-line).
**AVOID WHEN:** Assuming a life-stress trigger rules out medication (it doesn't); pregnancy/lactation are relative contraindications — severely recurrent cases were maintained through pregnancy on monthly IPT without medication (Frank 1990), while stopping medication in pregnancy carries substantial relapse risk (Cohen 2006).
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Method-level combination literature; per-patient superiority of one modality over another is empirically unresolved.
### [ATT:GRIEF-RECON] Facilitated mourning
**Move:** Reconstruct the relationship and the death in detail (how the news came, circumstances, before/during/after); deliberately invite both the loved and the disliked sides of the deceased; allow catharsis — when crying starts, do not interrupt; strong feelings are powerful but not dangerous and subside once accepted. Staged imaginary conversation with the deceased to voice what was left unsaid. Aids: photo albums, meaningful places, talking with friends and family about the dead person. Bipolar variant: mourning the "lost healthy self" — what episodes destroyed (relationships, career, self-image) — as a special role transition.
**APPLY WHEN:** Complicated-bereavement focus agreed; post-episode life in shambles with a shattered sense of self.
**AVOID WHEN:** Solo — this deliberately opens loss material; expect irritation at ambivalence probes when the deceased was the only secure attachment; cultures forbidding direct negatives about the dead — ask "were there times you felt disappointed?" instead.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Grief-focus component of IPT trials (method-level); prolonged-grief adaptation adding PTSD-treatment elements trialed (Shear, Frank, Houck & Reynolds, 2005); lost-healthy-self focus is an IPSRT component (Frank 2005), untested in isolation.
### [ATT:PTSD-SOCIAL-REBUILD] Non-exposure PTSD track
**Move:** Treat the traumatic event as a role transition and work exclusively on current interactions — expressing feelings, setting limits, reclaiming closeness with partner and coworkers — without exposure to trauma memories or reminders. Patients often expose themselves spontaneously as they improve (one resumed subways unprompted) — an observation, not a promise.
**APPLY WHEN:** PTSD patient refuses or cannot tolerate exposure-based treatment; comorbid depression present.
**AVOID WHEN:** Attempted solo — a formal treatment protocol for a trauma disorder with suicide-relevant comorbidity; blocking spontaneous self-exposure when it emerges.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Open trial (Bleiberg & Markowitz 2005); Krupnick group RCT vs treatment-as-usual in low-income women reported positive; 2 stars, preliminary.
### [ATT:DYAD-FEEDBACK] Therapy relationship as live sample
**Move:** Use the dyad as a here-and-now lab for the person's interpersonal style — invite complaints about therapist and treatment, correct distortions, apologize for real mistakes. On an alliance rupture, name it as an instance of the presenting problem (e.g., anger), explore expression options in the here-and-now, then pivot the same skill to outside relationships (sponsor, neighbors, job leads). Never framed as transference or childhood reenactment. Discuss termination early and often with abandonment-sensitive patients.
**APPLY WHEN:** In-treatment friction; deficits patients needing a model relationship; BPD-spectrum in-session reactions disrupting treatment; fragile alliance.
**AVOID WHEN:** Outside a therapy dyad; when it displaces the outside-life focus; as a psychodynamic interpretation exercise — the model explicitly avoids interpretations.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical guidance; BPD variant from an ongoing open Columbia trial (Markowitz, Skodol & Bleiberg 2006) — 1 star, do not oversell; DBT and mentalization-based treatment have stronger BPD evidence per the book.
### [ATT:ANGER-GRADED] Graded anger expression
**Move:** For the suppress-then-explode cycle (chronic suppression punctuated by explosions, which reinforce further suppression): link each anger surge to its interpersonal situation, validate the legitimate portion, then role-play progressively muted expressions — from silently staying in the room, to voicing feelings, to delivering measured versions in the real relationship.
**APPLY WHEN:** Suppress-then-explode anger wrecking relationships (AA sponsors, neighbors, employment); BPD-spectrum anger, framed to the patient as a poorly named syndrome with a large depressive component.
**AVOID WHEN:** Solo in a BPD context — suicide risk requires close monitoring; active crisis needing containment first.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Case-level within an unfinished open trial (Markowitz, Skodol & Bleiberg 2006) + one small unpublished trial (Angus & Gillies 1994); 1 star.

## What the book debunks

- State/trait confusion — the classic error: reading personality from a depressed snapshot; treating Axis I may dissolve the apparent Axis II (Ch. 1, 9).
- Multiple foci: choosing 2+ problem areas dilutes treatment into nothing; hopelessness is a symptom of a good-prognosis illness, not a forecast.
- "I've tried everything" is rarely accurate — attempts were half-hearted or options overlooked through discouragement.
- Grief myths: fear of being overwhelmed by grief is near-universal and near-always wrong (expressed affect loses force); treatment is not disrespect to the deceased; the goal is easing mourning, not ending it (mourning continues for years).
- Expecting others to read one's mind destabilizes relationships; in-session venting is not a template for outside use.
- "IPT is just supportive therapy" — half-true: it shares common factors but adds systematic interpersonal strategies no supportive therapist uses as consistently; hundreds of psychotherapies were never efficacy-tested, and technique overlap ≠ equivalence.
- Biological-vs-psychological is a false dichotomy — psychotherapy changes brain chemistry (Brody 2001, Martin 2001); alcohol is a mood depressant masquerading as self-treatment and raises suicide risk.
- Documented failure mode of the method itself: IPT is NOT a substance-abuse treatment — both drug RCTs negative (Rounsaville 1983, Carroll 1991); the authors say so plainly and warn against presenting it as one.
- Fairburn's role-play ban in bulimia trials was a design artifact, not clinical advice — keep role-play in real practice.
- Ageism ("the old can't change") contradicted by trial evidence; "who wouldn't be depressed with cancer" — most medically ill people are not depressed; "pregnancy = unconditional well-being" is a myth (10% of pregnant women have major depression, and it does not spontaneously remit).
- Didactic lectures and conference workshops alone do not change clinician behavior (Davis 1999) — supervised taped cases are the active ingredient; no official IPT certification exists, so "certified" claims exceed what the field offers.
- Delusional (psychotic) depression is not treatable by any psychotherapy alone; bipolar disorder requires medication (manic patients won't collaborate with talk therapy); response ≠ remission — Ham-D 30→13 is a response but still near relapse threshold.

## Numbers that matter

- Acute course: 12–16 weekly ~50-minute sessions (3–4 months); initial phase ≤3 visits; studies span 6 weekly to 36 monthly sessions.
- Ham-D bands: ≤8 normal (remission < 8); 9–12 mild; 13–19 moderate; ≥20 moderate-to-severe; ≥30 clearly severe; high 20s–30s → medication likely needed alongside therapy. Repeat every 3–4 weeks.
- Course-correction benchmarks: <50% symptom reduction by ~12 weeks → switch/augment; ~2/3 respond to a first pharmacotherapy course; ~50% remit within 4–6 weeks of proper treatment; 30–40% of people with one episode never have another (Judd 1998).
- Prevalence and risk: MDD point prevalence 3–4% of adults; children of depressed parents carry 2–3× risk (6–9% vs ~3% base) — most never become depressed; parental remission measurably improves children's symptoms (Weissman, Pilowsky 2006).
- Star scale (Ch. 10): 4 = ≥2 RCTs superior to control; 3 = ≥1 RCT or equivalence to established treatment; 2 = open/pilot; 1 = untested; 0 = negative findings.
- Ratings by adaptation: maintenance 4★; adolescents 4★; older adults 4★; medical patients 4★; bulimia 4★; cross-cultural MDD 4★; pregnancy/postpartum 3★ (miscarriage 2★); IPSRT 3★; anxiety disorders 2★; conjoint and telephone 2★; BPD 1★; prepubertal children 1★; dysthymia monotherapy 0; substance abuse 0; anorexia 0.
- Dysthymia trials: Browne 2002 (n=707) — response 47% IPT vs 60% sertraline vs 57.5% combination, but IPT lowered health/social-service costs, making combination most cost-effective (dose-unbalanced: 12 sessions vs 2+ years of medication); Markowitz 2005 (n=94) — sertraline beat both IPT and brief supportive therapy; change in the focal interpersonal problem correlated with symptom improvement.
- Grief thresholds: normal grief resolves over a few months; complication markers — severe phase >2 months, or not functioning at work/family 2–3 months post-death; DSM bereavement exclusion ends at 2 months.
- Uganda group IPT (Bolton 2003): local depression prevalence ≈21%; groups of 8–10, sex-segregated; 2 individual + 16 weekly 90-minute sessions; dropout 7.8%; "interpersonal deficits" dropped as culturally irrelevant.
- Elderly: first onset in late life is rare (medical/neurovascular workup first); suicide rates highest in late life, and most victims saw a primary-care physician within 6 months.
- Training: reading + workshop + 2–3 supervised recorded cases recommended; experienced therapists reached competence after as little as 1 supervised case (Rounsaville 1986).

## Blind spots of this book

- Everything move-level rests on package RCTs: no dismantling trials exist, so every "Evidence: method-level" line means the full protocol worked, not the individual technique — the book is honest about this but the limitation is structural.
- Attachment-theory-light: despite the interpersonal name, it never measures attachment style at all — no dimensions, no instruments. It avoids the categorical-types error by omission rather than by design; the lens must source dimensional attachment measurement elsewhere.
- 2007 vintage: DSM-IV frame throughout; prolonged grief disorder, DSM-5 changes, and post-2007 trial evidence (including updated star ratings in the 2018 Guide) postdate it.
- Deliberately excludes trauma processing, cognitive work, and childhood origins — nothing for people who need exposure, schema-level, or developmental work; PTSD track is explicitly non-exposure and preliminary.
- Its weakest category is chronic isolation ("interpersonal deficits") — precisely the population an attachment lens most often serves; the book admits IPT fits them worst and its referral-to-CBT suggestion is itself unsupported.
- Assumes a therapist-delivered dyad: self-application of SELF-tier moves is an extrapolation the book never tests (IPC, its simplified form, was never head-to-head tested against IPT).
- Comparative claims lean on dose-unbalanced designs (Browne: 12 IPT sessions vs 2+ years of sertraline), and the fixed time limit — presented as itself therapeutic — was never tested against open-ended IPT.
- Cross-cultural universality of the problem areas rests on 1–2 trials per culture, with technique adaptations authored by local trainees rather than trialed separately.
