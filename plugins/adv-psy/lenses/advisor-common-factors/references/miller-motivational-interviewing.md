# Motivational Interviewing: Helping People Change and Grow — digest

## What this book is for the lens

People talk themselves into and out of change, so the lever is *whose voice says the reasons*. Ambivalence is normal; the change-talk/sustain-talk ratio predicts change, and a helper can shift that ratio with observable, learnable skills. Where other common-factors sources describe the relationship, MI specifies what to say next — which question, which reflection, which summary, in which direction.
It also names the standing failure mode (the fixing reflex: argue the pro-change side, and the other person rehearses the anti-change side) and supplies graded repairs for pushback that never refute.
It is unusually honest about limits: ~25% null trials, adverse effects at high initial readiness, workshops alone barely moving coded skill, and public retraction of the authors' own earlier positions.

## Core model

- **Spirit (4):** partnership (they are the expert on themselves), acceptance (being accepted as-you-are is what makes change possible), compassion (benevolent *intent*, not sympathy), empowerment (naming free choice lowers defensiveness). Spirit grows out of practising the skills; it is not a prerequisite.
- **Three styles:** directing / following / **guiding** — MI's home style, walking alongside.
- **Four tasks, recursive (Ch. 4–7):** engaging "Can we walk together?" → focusing "Where are we going?" → evoking "Why would you go there?" → planning "How will you get there?". Their speech tells you which task you are in. A goal is not a goal until shared; a plan is not a plan until owned. MI is *directional* — neither directive nor nondirective; evoking is what separates it from generic empathic listening.
- **Language (Ch. 6):** change talk = preparatory **DARN** (desire, ability, reasons, need) + mobilizing **CATs** (commitment, activation, taking steps); each has a sustain-talk mirror, tangled together in ambivalence. "Resistance" is retired and split into **sustain talk** (about the topic) and **discord** (about you — usually contains "you").
- **Base skills: OARS** — open question, affirm, reflect, summarize. None is neutral; each carries direction whether you notice or not.
- **Part III depth:** Ch. 8 deeper listening; Ch. 9 focusing, values, neutrality; Ch. 10 cultivating change talk; Ch. 11 information/advice (ask–offer–ask); Ch. 12 persistence; Ch. 13 planting seeds when there is no change talk; Ch. 14 sustain talk and discord; Ch. 15 practising well; Ch. 16–17 learning and fidelity (MISC/MITI); Ch. 18 evidence.
- **Anchors:** Box 6.1 (decisional-balance debunk), Box 7.1 (100 characteristics of successful changers), Box 9.1 (Values Card Sort), Fig. 4.1/4.2 (four-step listening chain), Fig. 5.1 (diabetes bubble sheet: 12 topics + blank), MITI 4.2.1.

## Moves

SELF tier first (method-level core skills before author-craft moves); REQUIRES-THERAPIST at the end.

### [CF:GUIDE] Take the guiding stance on purpose
**Move:** Name the style you are about to use — directing, following, guiding — and default to guiding: listen well, offer direction only when it fits, hold beginner's-mind curiosity, restrain the urge to solve, stay the calm centre when anger is aimed at you.
**APPLY WHEN:** any conversation about someone's change where the choices are theirs; you feel the pull to tell them what to do.
**AVOID WHEN:** acute risk where directing is correct; they asked for instruction; faking calm while denying what you feel — people detect phoniness.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level — the guiding style is the foundational stance across MI trials.

### [CF:FIXREFLEX] Catch the fixing reflex and the four traps
**Move:** Notice the urge to convince, warn, prescribe; on hearing "Don't you see…"/"Yes, but…", stop arguing and ask instead of tell. Audit for the four traps — expert (solving *for* them), persuasion (you take the pro side), time (rushing, which slows everything), wandering (following with no direction). Countermoves: state collaborative intent, ask-not-tell, allow more engaging time, clarify focus.
**APPLY WHEN:** you feel urgency about what someone should do; they counterargue you; the exchange left them defensive or you did most of the talking.
**AVOID WHEN:** emergencies where directive action is the job; using the audit to self-flagellate instead of building one alternative response.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** MI process research on interviewer→client language plus authors' training experience.

### [CF:LAYDOWN] Drop responsibility for the outcome, keep it for your conduct
**Move:** Restate your job beforehand: create conditions that make change possible, not produce it. Nobody can force another's choice; carrying that assumption is a route to burnout and is detected as coercive intent. Pair with beginner's mind — set aside diagnosis and what you already "know".
**APPLY WHEN:** you feel "I'm doing my best and they still don't change"; you catch yourself running remedies while they are still speaking.
**AVOID WHEN:** it excuses passivity or skips a real duty of care; acute risk assessment cannot be deferred.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' experience plus early studies linking the mindset shift to lower burnout.

### [CF:RAPID-ENGAGE] Spend the first fifth of the time engaging only
**Move:** For ~20% of available time do nothing but engage: one open opener, then listening and reflection only — no investigative questions, no problem-hunting, notes afterwards. Under time pressure this makes the rest faster; all four tasks fit inside a 5–10-minute consultation.
**APPLY WHEN:** the start of any consultation or hard conversation, especially when you feel you have no time.
**AVOID WHEN:** extended into following-only for the whole conversation (wandering trap); triage needing fast information.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' training experience; alliance is among the most consistent outcome predictors across orientations.

### [CF:MIRROR] Reflect as a statement, not a question
**Move:** Strip question markers and inflect down at the end. Simple reflection stays close to what was said; complex reflection guesses at meaning. Keep it no longer than their utterance; shift simple→complex as understanding grows. A wrong guess costs nothing — "no, it's more…" teaches as much as "yes, and…".
**APPLY WHEN:** any task, any time you want them to keep exploring and hear themselves; whenever you were about to ask another question.
**AVOID WHEN:** parroting or light paraphrase (the misconception that hollows the method out); upward inflection, which reads as doubting their right to feel it; technique without real intent to understand.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level core skill, operationalized in MISC/MITI; empathy→outcome link correlational but sturdy.

### [CF:CONTPARA] Continue the paragraph
**Move:** Voice the plausible *next* sentence instead of restating the last — one small increment ahead, sometimes a single word, as a statement. Variant, lending change talk: tentatively say the change talk that follows from their story and let them confirm or correct. Let silences stand.
**APPLY WHEN:** the person is terse or low-verbal; the conversation circles; change talk sits just below the surface.
**AVOID WHEN:** you would leap several steps ahead — the standard failure mode; the unsaid material is trauma, abuse or self-harm with no clinical container.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level support for complex reflection as a class; the specific move is authors' clinical method.

### [CF:INTENSITY-DIAL] Choose the intensity of the reflection deliberately
**Move:** Reflect one notch below or above what you heard. Understate ("a little annoyed" for "upset") to invite more; overstate — amplified reflection — so an absolute claim gets walked back by them ("Drinking has never caused you any trouble"). Track the correction and adjust.
**APPLY WHEN:** you want elaboration (understate); an all-or-nothing claim would soften if stated fully (overstate).
**AVOID WHEN:** any sarcasm — amplification collapses into mockery instantly; rapport is thin; the person is flooded; neutrality is the aim, since intensity is directional.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical experience inside method-level MI; no trial isolates over/understating.

### [CF:DOUBLESIDE] Double-sided reflection, change side last, joined by "and"
**Move:** Both horns in one sentence — sustain talk first, their own change talk last, connected by "and"/"at the same time"; "but" cancels what precedes it. People elaborate whatever you said last. Softer variant: "there's a part of you that…".
**APPLY WHEN:** both sides are voiced; single-sided reflections keep producing "yes, but"; you want the whole picture held without taking sides.
**AVOID WHEN:** the change side is your invention; the decision is made and needs consolidating; it becomes a trick to bury the sustain side — the manipulation is heard.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level research on change/sustain-talk sequencing; ordering effect illustrated, not measured.

### [CF:OPENQ] Open questions, two reflections per question
**Move:** Ask questions leaving room to choose what to say ("In what ways is this important to you?"); hold ~2 reflections per question, minimum 1:1; speak less than half the time.
**APPLY WHEN:** opening conversations, building momentum, learning their frame.
**AVOID WHEN:** strings of closed intake questions — they render the person passive and imply your questions will produce the solution; closed chains read as cross-examination.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level (OARS; question/reflection counts are MITI categories); 2:1 is an author guideline.

### [CF:AFFIRM] Affirm something real, simple or complex
**Move:** Comment appreciatively on something true. Simple affirmation targets a specific act ("You saw the warning signs and acted"); complex names an enduring strength ("That took real courage"). Leave "I" out. Switch to "strengths glasses" when you are turning into a deficit detective.
**APPLY WHEN:** defensiveness is present (affirmation lowers it and raises openness to threatening information); strengths visible but unremarked.
**AVOID WHEN:** insincere or overused; affirming maladaptive content — people produce more of whatever gets affirmed, even when it leads to worse outcomes; premature reassurance.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level — positive regard is one of eight skills of more-effective therapists; affirmation raises change talk.

### [CF:BOUQUET] Collect their change talk, hand it back, then ask the key question
**Move:** Gather accumulated change talk — without the sustain-talk weeds — into one summary, so they hear their own arguments a third time; 2–3 statements make a first posy. Use mini-summaries mid-conversation ("So far you've mentioned X and Y — what else?") and an end summary inviting correction. Then ask: "So what do you think you'll do?"
**APPLY WHEN:** change talk has accumulated; transitions and session close; readiness signs appear but the shift to planning is not explicit.
**AVOID WHEN:** prosecutor mode, assembling admissions as evidence (backfires); summaries ending on the status quo, since the last thing lingers; all-problem summaries, which demoralize; asking "Are you ready?" head-on.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level change-talk research plus OARS trials; four contrasting summaries of one interview are an author demonstration.

### [CF:EAR] Train the ear for change talk, including embedded and hedged
**Move:** Attend to, recognize and *remember* every fragment of change talk, classified against the current focus and sorted by type (DARN/CATs). Mark fragments inside a mostly-sustain-talk paragraph; count hedged forms ("maybe", "I guess") as real. Self-use: label a journal entry sentence by sentence and count what you have.
**APPLY WHEN:** always, once a focus exists; when a stance feels uniformly stuck; before concluding you are "not ready".
**AVOID WHEN:** counting talk about a different goal as change talk for yours; the target is trauma or loss rather than a behaviour choice — coding then substitutes for feeling.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level — change-talk coding is the measurement backbone of 40 years of MI process research.

### [CF:DIRQ] Ask questions whose natural answer is change talk
**Move:** Build directional questions from DARN: "Why would you want this?", "If you decided, how could you succeed?", "What are the best reasons for you?", "How important is this?" The point is that they hear their own answer.
**APPLY WHEN:** focus is set and you are evoking the why; you want depth and momentum, not more balance.
**AVOID WHEN:** phrasing invites sustain talk ("Why haven't you changed?", "What keeps you from it?") — too early for obstacles while they weigh *whether*; no direction agreed, or you hold a conflict of interest, which makes it manipulation.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level trials — evoking skills raise the change-talk ratio, and the ratio predicts change.

### [CF:RULER] Importance and confidence rulers, asked upward
**Move:** "0–10, how important is this?" and separately "how confident are you that you could do it if you decided?" Then the follow-up that makes it work: "Why a 4 and not 0?" — the answer is change talk. Also "What would move it one point up?"
**APPLY WHEN:** telling a motivation problem apart from a self-efficacy problem; step 3 of brief action planning.
**AVOID WHEN:** asking "why not an 8?" — that elicits sustain talk; ritual automatic use; treating the number as a measurement rather than a prompt for speech.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Standard in MI and MET protocols; follow-up direction comes from change-talk research.

### [CF:OARS-CT] When change talk appears, do not just sit there
**Move:** Ask an open question for elaboration or a concrete example ("When was your last hangover?"), affirm the strength it implies, reflect it, summarize it later — then ask "What else?" until the theme is exhausted. Decide in advance what you will do the moment change talk appears, then watch how they respond to your response.
**APPLY WHEN:** any change talk or confidence talk surfaces, including the first thin reason.
**AVOID WHEN:** it becomes interrogation, or fatigue/discord appears — reflect instead; affirming unhealthy content; mechanical repetition that sounds like technique.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level — response to change talk influences its later frequency; the commonest trainee failure is walking past offered change talk.

### [CF:DIRREF] Reflect selectively, toward the change side
**Move:** In a mixed statement ("I don't want to quit… I know I should… it's hard"), mirror the change strand ("It's pretty clear to you that you ought to quit"). What you reflect is what the next utterance continues; every candidate must be a legitimate reflection — selection, not distortion.
**APPLY WHEN:** change and sustain talk are tangled and you have an agreed direction.
**AVOID WHEN:** the intent is deliberate neutrality; the reflection distorts; reflecting sustain talk by default — legitimate early for engagement, not as a standing habit.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level sequential studies — what is reflected shapes subsequent change/sustain talk.

### [CF:ONE-STEP-AHEAD] Predict the next utterance, then check
**Move:** Before each question or reflection, silently predict what they will most likely say next. If the prediction is sustain talk, rephrase until the natural answer would be change talk; then compare the actual reply and adjust. One move of look-ahead, not chess.
**APPLY WHEN:** conversations keep circling into reasons-why-not; you are deliberately practising across many exchanges.
**AVOID WHEN:** engagement is not established or they feel steered; neutrality is the goal; monitoring gets so heavy you stop listening.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Sequential-analysis studies of therapist→client language plus authors' training practice.

### [CF:VALUES] Explore values as the internal frame of reference
**Move:** Ask open questions about guiding values, following each answer with a reflection rather than another question: what matters most; how you hope life differs in a few years; the rules you live by; five values in one word each; what close friends would say you live for. Structured version — Values Card Sort (Box 9.1, 100 public-domain items): five piles, blanks allowed, rank the top 5 or 10, then elaborate each (how you express it, examples, why it matters).
**APPLY WHEN:** engagement stage; goal unclear; motivation seems absent ("no one is unmotivated"); you need leverage that is theirs.
**AVOID WHEN:** rapid-fire questioning without reflections; scoring the card sort like an instrument; stopping at slogan labels; confusing understanding with endorsement.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level MI research plus self-affirmation literature; no RCT of the card sort itself.

### [CF:VALUE-GAP] Let them notice the value–behaviour gap themselves
**Move:** Put stated long-term values and current behaviour in one frame with curiosity, not challenge — for each top value ask whether the behaviour helps it, is neutral, or interferes — and let the discrepancy be theirs to see. Do not announce it for them.
**APPLY WHEN:** values are articulated in their own words; ambivalence is live; you are developing ambivalence where there is none.
**AVOID WHEN:** run as a gotcha to prove hypocrisy — that converts discrepancy into discord, the main misuse of values work.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Cited discrepancy research; a values-based single-session MI trial cut alcohol use at 3 and 6 months, mediated by self-esteem.

### [CF:TIMELENS] Look back, look forward, imagine the extremes
**Move:** Shift the time frame: recall life before the problem; envision success, including the miracle question ("you wake up and life is as you'd like it — what's different?"); ask the extremes ("best that could come of changing?" / "worst if nothing changes?"). When obstacles dominate, invite envisioning past them first, then return to the route.
**APPLY WHEN:** motivation is abstract; hope has drained; they cannot see past current troubles or discuss the how at all.
**AVOID WHEN:** there is no better past to recall — the better-days strategy backfires; forcing rosy futures onto acute despair; using extremes to frighten; never returning to concrete steps.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' practice inside method-level MI; the miracle question is borrowed from solution-focused therapy.

### [CF:AGENDA-MAP] Map the agenda visually, and treat focus as renegotiable
**Move:** Lay candidate topics out as a menu — spoken, or a bubble sheet of circles with at least one blank "?" (Fig. 5.1: 12 diabetes topics + blank) — and let them pick 2–3. Then keep re-clarifying: hold the agreed horizon, attend to the immediate concern, steer back, and let a new horizon replace the old when one genuinely emerges.
**APPLY WHEN:** multiple change targets; new diagnosis or broad topic; limited time; chronic-condition talk has turned blaming; conversation keeps drifting.
**AVOID WHEN:** bubbles pre-filled with your priorities — a fake menu costs trust; they already state a specific goal; no shared focus exists yet, so steering is imposition; the "drift" is a new priority worth re-contracting around.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Rollnick's primary-care development work (early 1990s); bubble sheet from Steinberg & Miller (2015).

### [CF:WILLING-STEP] When their goal differs from yours, ask what they *are* willing to do
**Move:** Stop asking what they will not do. Start from a broad life goal they hold (health, staying out of prison, a strong relationship), list steps toward it, and pick the first one they would actually try. Keep their interest, not the institution's, at the centre; state your hopes openly but do not impose them.
**APPLY WHEN:** mandated or pressured entry (courts, probation, family); you and they want different things; they sound "unmotivated", which usually means argued at.
**AVOID WHEN:** it is a disguised route back to your agenda — reframing an institutional goal as "theirs" is misuse; genuinely no part of them wants the change, so go to planting seeds or declared neutrality.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level MI trials in addiction and mandated settings; matching data suggest larger benefit at lower initial motivation or higher anger.

### [CF:ASK-PERMISSION] Gate every piece of advice on permission
**Move:** Before offering information, opinion or suggestion, secure one of three conditions: they asked, you asked ("May I share an idea about that?"), or you prefaced by emphasizing their freedom to discount it. Accept a no. Without one of the three the response is coded MI-inconsistent.
**APPLY WHEN:** you hold relevant knowledge and no request has been made; any power gradient (clinician/patient, teacher/student, parent/adult child).
**AVOID WHEN:** you will say it regardless — permission as a ritual prefix to pre-decided persuasion is the named misuse; urgent safety-critical information.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** MITI coding — persuasion without permission and confrontation are the two coded MI-inconsistent behaviours; plus reactance literature.

### [CF:AUTONOMY-EMPHASIS] Say the true thing: the choice is theirs
**Move:** Hand the decision back — "It's up to you", "Nobody can decide that for you" — and preface content with autonomy language ("you may already know this", "I wonder what you'll think"). If you must speak unbidden: "you don't need to agree, and that's OK, but I wouldn't feel right saying nothing." Drill: say "It's your choice" warm, flat, cynical, threatening, and hear the difference.
**APPLY WHEN:** any advice-giving, especially where you hold authority or a stake; they bristle at pressure; they are under real constraint and still choose.
**AVOID WHEN:** used as a fig leaf while the tone coerces — the mismatch is heard; dodging honesty about consequences; false control claims ("You can't drink"), which invite the test you fear.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level, supported by reactance research and freedom-of-choice framing studies.

### [CF:OPTION-MENU] Offer several options in one pass, after eliciting theirs
**Move:** Never prescribe one route. Say several exist → ask what they have already considered or tried → brainstorm without evaluating, welcoming unrealistic ideas to unfreeze thinking → ask permission before adding yours → lay the menu out in one continuous pass → ask which seem possible, with explicit veto over any.
**APPLY WHEN:** the goal is agreed and only the method is open; they produce reasons each single suggestion won't work; the option space feels closed.
**AVOID WHEN:** only one option is genuinely appropriate — padding a menu is dishonest; asking "what about this one?" after each item, which turns choosing into serial critiquing; they are overwhelmed and need one small step.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level practice; choice/commitment research — freely chosen options stick, rapid-fire single suggestions manufacture sustain talk.

### [CF:AOA] Ask–offer–ask around every piece of information
**Move:** Ask first (permission, plus what they already know, want to know, or have tried) → offer one small chunk framed by their answer → ask again (reaction, meaning: "what does this mean for you going forward?"). Repeat in cycles. Give just enough to make it intelligible, then stop and wait for them to ask for more rather than finishing the lecture you know.
**APPLY WHEN:** any psychoeducation, feedback, test result or advice; you are tempted to explain, warn or educate about a risk.
**AVOID WHEN:** an emergency instruction is needed in full; chunking becomes a quiz; it is interrogation in disguise or walks them to a pre-scripted conclusion; you skip the closing ask, which turns it back into lecturing.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level trials on information exchange plus group-MI data; the prenatal-clinic case produced most of the intended lecture from the women's own mouths.

### [CF:READY-SIGNS] Read the six signs of readiness instead of scheduling the shift
**Move:** Before moving from why to how, listen for: more change talk; less sustain talk; resolve or quiet; envisioning aloud what change would be like, including its difficulties; questions about change; small steps already taken. The commonest single sign is them raising the topic of *how*.
**APPLY WHEN:** deciding whether to shift from evoking to planning.
**AVOID WHEN:** treating one cue as a green light to prescribe; pushing planning on schedule rather than on signal; misreading imagined obstacles as sustain talk — they are trying the change on.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical observation, consistent with process research on change/sustain ratios.

### [CF:BAP] Brief action planning
**Move:** Five steps, a few minutes: (1) "Is there anything you'd like to do for your health in the next week or two?"; (2) if yes, shape it into a SMART plan; (3) have them restate it in their own words; (4) rate confidence 0–10 and, if under 7, ask what would raise it; (5) offer accountability. Frame the result as an experiment — one option to try, with the message that you will keep working until something fits.
**APPLY WHEN:** they sound engaged and action-ready; brief medical-style contacts; a small near-term step is on the table; they fear another "failure".
**AVOID WHEN:** ambivalence or low readiness — evoke the why first; pressing structure on someone restless with step-by-step plans; using "experiment" to dodge specificity about what, when and how.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** MI-consistent five-step method taught in medical settings; method-level support, no book-specific RCT.

### [CF:COMMIT-LISTEN] Track the verb ladder, never press for "I will"
**Move:** Listen for the modal actually used — want → could → need → willing → will — and treat movement up it as strengthening commitment; check congruence between words and gesture. Support specific stated intentions and let the language strengthen on its own.
**APPLY WHEN:** a plan is forming and mobilizing change talk appears; reviewing your own stated intentions.
**AVOID WHEN:** pressing ("So are you going to do it or not?") — pressure can undo motivational progress; scoring or catching someone out. A declared commitment is not required: a favourable balance predicts action without it.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Psycholinguistic MI process research plus implementation-intention findings; debunks "no commitment language, no change".

### [CF:ABILITY-QUEST] Evoke confidence, then hand the challenges back
**Move:** When importance is set but confidence lags, ask questions whose natural answer is ability talk — "How might you go about it?", "What would be a good first step?", "What gives you some confidence you can do this?" — and reflect the answers. Once confidence talk flows, stress-test by handing problems back: "What might you do if…?"
**APPLY WHEN:** the profile is high importance, low confidence ("I know I should, but I can't"); the plan needs specificity.
**AVOID WHEN:** you answer your own questions; you take a first "I don't know" at face value — usually a thinking placeholder; you offer solutions and they list limitations; challenges before any confidence exists.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level MI trials; self-efficacy predicts who benefits and is modifiable.

### [CF:STRENGTHS] The 100-adjective strengths list
**Move:** Hand over Box 7.1 (100 adjectives describing successful changers), ask them to circle at least five that fit, then interview each choice with open questions and reflections — a concrete example, and the link to the current change. Extend to social supports: who could help, and how.
**APPLY WHEN:** confidence is low; the person is too self-conscious to affirm themselves unprompted.
**AVOID WHEN:** you assign the strengths instead of them choosing; you skip the elaboration interview — circling alone does little.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Structured procedure from the authors' practice; affirmation effects are method-level.

### [CF:PAST-SUCCESS] Mine past changes for transferable skill
**Move:** Ask what difficult changes they have already made, or done when unsure they could; walk one through concretely — how they decided, prepared, started, kept going, what obstacles arose, what they credit — then reflect the skills the story implies and generalize them to the present change.
**APPLY WHEN:** confidence is low but a change history exists.
**AVOID WHEN:** you narrate the lessons instead of letting them voice it; you persist past the point of annoyance.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' practice within MI; mastery-experience rationale from self-efficacy theory.

### [CF:REFRAME-TRIES] Turn "failures" into tries
**Move:** Recast previous attempts as successive steps showing persistence, not proof of inability. When the attribution is stable and internal ("I can't"), reflect toward external/unstable causes: "The time wasn't right", "You haven't done it yet", "You weren't quite ready".
**APPLY WHEN:** they are bogged down in "I've tried several times and failed".
**AVOID WHEN:** it slides into platitudes; it denies real obstacles they are naming.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Attribution theory plus authors' practice; dependent smokers average 3–4 serious quit attempts.

### [CF:WHAT-NEXT-NOW-ELSE] Three replanning questions
**Move:** "What next?" — the next small approximation. "What now?" — after a setback, how to get back on track and whether the plan needs adjusting. "What else?" — when the approach itself is failing, scrap it and build a new plan for the same goal. Close by summarizing the new plan and asking for explicit assent.
**APPLY WHEN:** an agreed plan is under way and stalling, interrupted or ineffective.
**AVOID WHEN:** the goal itself is wrong — refocus instead; you start supplying the steps. The fixing reflex is strongest here, and explaining how to change once someone seems ready can destroy the momentum built.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level MI; chapter-level clinical guidance (Ch. 12), no separate trial.

### [CF:SETBACK-NORMALIZE] Bound the setback before it eats the plan
**Move:** Catch a lapse early, name it as an ordinary event rather than failure, keep it explicitly bounded. Replace absolute perfection rules ("I will never eat sweets") with non-binary targets, reframe imperfection as partial progress, and affirm the ground already gained.
**APPLY WHEN:** a black-or-white rule has just broken and the "nothing left to lose" logic is starting.
**AVOID WHEN:** the lapse involves acute risk — overdose, self-harm, medical emergency; normalizing is not the response there.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Cited work on rule-violation breakdown of self-control and on early support maintaining change.

### [CF:RE-EVOKE-CHECK] Re-evoke rather than remind; check confidence before importance
**Move:** Ask plainly whether this is still what they want, need or choose. If commitment slipped, replay their own earlier reasons non-confrontingly and re-run the importance ruler. If importance seems to have dropped after failed attempts, check the confidence ruler first: repeated failure lowers self-efficacy and people then rationalize the goal as never that important.
**APPLY WHEN:** a sound plan is executed listlessly; commitment sounds hollow; a previously important goal is suddenly downgraded.
**AVOID WHEN:** a "let me remind you" tone — blunt confrontation with discrepancy is contraindicated; the reprioritization is genuine and circumstances changed, so refocus.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level MI plus Ch. 6 rulers; the confidence-first inference is the authors' clinical reasoning.

### [CF:DISCORD-SCAN] Classify sustain talk vs discord; treat the rise as a stop signal
**Move:** Ask what you just heard: about the topic (sustain talk) or about you (discord — usually contains "you")? Discord markers: being challenged or discounted, defending their worth, new interrupting, disengagement. Any rise means stop what you were doing — back off, reflect, reaffirm autonomy, leave the door open, since pushing trains them to defend the status quo.
**APPLY WHEN:** a change conversation turns adversarial or the tone shifts with no content reason; any concern-raising about behaviour someone else wants changed.
**AVOID WHEN:** it becomes silent diagnosis of their character — the framing is interactional, not a trait rating.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** MI session-coding research (Moyers); the amount of sustain talk predicts outcome better than the amount of change talk.

### [CF:REFLECT-SUSTAIN] Answer pushback with a reflection, not a counterargument
**Move:** Meet sustain talk and discord with plain reflection of content or feeling — "It just doesn't feel right to you", "You really want me to hear you" — no stock preamble needed. Sustained reflection de-escalates because the person registers being heard. Track intensity: if negativity rises across exchanges, change approach.
**APPLY WHEN:** they argue for the status quo, are heated, or have just accused you of not listening.
**AVOID WHEN:** the reflection carries sarcasm; discord is about a real grievance needing an actual remedy.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level trials plus coding research; softening reduces resistance, refuting strengthens it.

### [CF:REPAIR-DISCORD] Apologize, or shift attention
**Move:** When the friction is about the two of you, take partial responsibility aloud — "I'm sorry, let me try again; tell me what you want me to understand". When the fight is over a label or who is at fault, step off it: "I don't care much about labels; what I care about is you and what alcohol is doing in your life."
**APPLY WHEN:** the signal is discord rather than sustain talk; winning the sub-argument would change nothing.
**AVOID WHEN:** you would apologize for someone's ambivalence — that is sustain talk; the topic you sidestep is their actual agenda, which reads as evasion and makes more discord.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical experience; defined repairs given without effect sizes.

### [CF:PENDULUM] Ride the swing to the sustain side first
**Move:** When open questions yield no change talk, deliberately reflect the sustain talk for a few turns, briefly summarize what they like about the status quo, then ask about the downside — letting the pendulum reverse on its own. (Renamed from "running head start" in this edition.)
**APPLY WHEN:** repeated attempts to evoke change talk produce only "yes, but".
**AVOID WHEN:** used routinely — the authors call it an occasional tool; a conflict of interest makes the "less good" question loaded.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical experience, illustrated by a diet-change dialogue; no trial.

### [CF:COMING-ALONGSIDE] Voice their own position fully instead of disputing it
**Move:** State the extreme or self-deprecating position back without endorsing it and without sarcasm — "Maybe you'll just need to keep smoking regardless of the consequences" — and let them supply the counterargument. Variant: summarize the whole list of reasons-it-won't-work, count them out neutrally, then say you sense there is another list and ask what is on it.
**APPLY WHEN:** direct reassurance keeps producing more self-discounting; other softening responses failed and the fixing reflex pulls you toward persuasion.
**AVOID WHEN:** delivered as a dare, bluff or reverse psychology — people sense manipulation; the belief concerns safety or self-harm, where amplification reads as agreement with a dangerous conclusion.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Standard MI technique with method-level backing; wordings are authors' clinical experience.

### [CF:REFRAME] Offer another meaning, lightly, once
**Move:** Propose a different plausible reading of a fact they already accept — "she's always nagging me" → "she really cares about you"; high alcohol tolerance → elevated risk, not immunity (smoke-alarm analogy: real damage without the warning most people have). Deliver in passing, as a plain reflection, then let it sit.
**APPLY WHEN:** their own interpretation of a known fact is what blocks concern or fuels a standoff.
**AVOID WHEN:** you would insist, repeat or defend it — an insisted reframe becomes an argument; the reframe is strained, denies a real harm, or their negative reading is accurate.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Long-standing MI element without isolated trial support; authors' clinical experience.

### [CF:SEEDFRAME] With no change talk at all, switch the goal to developing ambivalence
**Move:** Stop evoking motivation that is not there. Aim to create ambivalence over weeks or months while protecting the relationship: keep engaging, hold off on any plan, and treat one raised doubt as a full success. Motivation is not a fixed internal quantity — it is generated inside a relationship.
**APPLY WHEN:** repeated evocation returns only sustain talk; the person has not considered change at all or has given up.
**AVOID WHEN:** they are in fact ambivalent and simply have not been listened to yet — most people who look unmotivated are ambivalent underneath and defending it against people who lecture.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level MI trials plus authors' experience; no trial of seed planting as an isolated procedure.

### [CF:OTHERSEYES] Ask what the worried people around them see
**Move:** With curiosity, not prosecution, ask why the concerned people in their life might be worried, reflect the content, and gather the concerns into a bouquet without adding your verdict — the aim is integration, not extracting an admission. Companion rule: strip fear appeals and moral pressure, keep a flat tone, and pair every uncomfortable fact with an accepting reflection.
**APPLY WHEN:** someone else is worried, the person is not, and they will still talk to you; the content itself is frightening.
**AVOID WHEN:** you are playing "gotcha"; the third party's concern is coercive or abusive; closed questions turn it into cross-examination; calm delivery becomes dishonest softening of facts.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical experience (annotated Peter transcript, self-critiqued for closed questions).

### [CF:PERMCONCERN] State your own concern once, then hand the choice back
**Move:** Say what you observed, ask "Is it OK if I tell you why I'm concerned?", give the concern once with your expertise honestly bounded, name the possible seriousness a single time, then explicitly return the decision ("it's up to you") and anchor it in caring.
**APPLY WHEN:** you hold relevant information or a real concern and have enough credibility to be heard.
**AVOID WHEN:** permission is refused, or you would repeat the concern after it was declined — repetition converts advice into confrontation; the "advice" is pressure in a polite wrapper.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Brief-advice trials showing empathic advice from a credible source can trigger change.

### [CF:CHECKUP] Generate personal data with no strings, then feed it back carefully
**Move:** Propose an assessment framed explicitly as information-gathering, not treatment, with the decision about results reserved entirely to them (the drinker's check-up model). Return results one at a time ask–offer–ask style, benchmarked against real norms, and supply accurate normative data when their sense of "normal" rests on an outlier peer group. When they dispute a number, reflect the disbelief instead of defending it, and affirm their honesty.
**APPLY WHEN:** motivation looks thin but curiosity exists and objective measures do; their main defence is "everyone I know does this".
**AVOID WHEN:** engagement is absent and it would feel like surveillance; you cannot honestly leave the decision with them; no reliable norms exist; the format quietly makes you talk far more than you listen.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Direct trials of the drinker's check-up and its computer version (participants roughly halved drinking, largely without treatment); norm-correction trials; MET is the derivative.

### [CF:ATTRIBCHECK] Convert trait labels back into interactions
**Move:** When you catch a stable internal attribution — "he's in denial", "she's just resistant" — stop and ask what in your own last few responses produced the behaviour you are labelling. In-session resistance turns up and down like a volume knob depending on what the helper does, and so does motivation.
**APPLY WHEN:** you notice yourself diagnosing character rather than describing an interaction.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to dismiss legitimate risk signals or as a substitute for real assessment where diagnosis is warranted.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Attribution research (actor–observer divergence) plus 40 years of MI process observation.

### [CF:SKIPEVOKE] With high readiness, skip evoking and go to the how
**Move:** When readiness and confidence are already high, stop building motivation and move to planning; do not allocate fixed time per task in structured delivery — let readiness decide how much evoking versus planning happens.
**APPLY WHEN:** the person is asking for method, not motivation.
**AVOID WHEN:** readiness is stated but not demonstrated, or ambivalence is still audible in sustain talk; a protocol requires standardization, in which case measure what was actually delivered.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Several reports of adverse effects at high initial readiness — the strongest contraindication signal in the book; causal direction inferred, not tested.

### [CF:PRACTICE-LOOP] Practise deliberately, then count instead of judging
**Move:** Drill: practise one OARS component at a time in ordinary conversations; substitute a reflection every time you were about to ask a question; ask permission to just listen for a long stretch on a topic where you disagree. Measure: record real practice with consent, review a 10-minute segment, and count — who talked more, questions vs simple vs complex reflections, what preceded each change talk / sustain talk / discord, and how you responded.
**APPLY WHEN:** you want data rather than recollection; skill feels stuck; a peer learning community exists (real play beats role play).
**AVOID WHEN:** consent is absent or recordings unprotected; monitoring gets so absorbing you stop listening; covert practice on people who would object; self-rating substitutes for counting — self-reports err upward.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Training research: workshops alone left ≈ one extra reflection per 10 minutes at 4 months with no change in client responses; only feedback *plus* coaching shifted client language.

### [CF:ADMITLIMIT] Name where your competence ends and route out
**Move:** State plainly what you are not qualified to judge, pair it with a reflection of what they actually want, and offer a concrete next step — enquiries, arranging a contact, meeting again — instead of an answer you cannot back. When their aims fall outside your remit, refer rather than bending their goal to fit your service.
**APPLY WHEN:** asked whether to pursue trauma-focused work or a specific therapy; wrong-door situations; requests exceeding scope.
**AVOID WHEN:** the mismatch is really ambivalence about a goal you *can* work on — referral is then avoidance; handing off someone in acute crisis without arranging a real contact.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Professional-ethics norm asserted by the authors; naming the limit is the part a non-clinician can do, the decision about opening traumatic material is not.

### [CF:VALUE-ORIGIN] Ask how a value became a value
**Move:** Ask for the biography of a named value — "how did this get to be a value for you?" — and follow with reflections rather than further questions.
**APPLY WHEN:** a top value has been named, you want depth, and the person is stable and willing.
**AVOID WHEN:** the origins sit in childhood danger, neglect or violence — the book's own demonstration lands on an unsafe childhood and protecting a parent within a few turns.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical demonstration; no trial isolates origin-probing.

### [CF:VERTICAL-AMB] Name a motive outside awareness — once, then wait
**Move:** When behaviour persistently contradicts a stated motive, name the inconsistency a single time as a question ("you say you don't want children, and you're always on the floor with them — what's that about?"), then give patient empathic support and double-sided reflections while they integrate it. Do not press for insight on a schedule.
**APPLY WHEN:** one pole is clearly out of awareness and they are puzzled by their own repeated behaviour; repeated failure to act despite a settled decision.
**AVOID WHEN:** the hidden material is likely trauma or grief — the book's own example resolves into a childhood bereavement, and timing of interpretation is a clinical judgment.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical experience framed as psychodynamic timing of interpretation; no trial, and they admit having no better answer than patient support.

### [CF:EXPERIENCING-SHIFT] Deepen from abstract to present-tense felt experience
**Move:** Notice whether an account is abstract, past-tense and impersonal, and steer it toward first-person, present-tense, emotionally engaged description — "what is that like right now" rather than "why did that happen". Generally: name which in-session response your method treats as the healing signal, and check it predicts outcome before evoking it.
**APPLY WHEN:** self-reflection has gone theoretical and detached and the content is ordinary present-life material.
**AVOID WHEN:** the material is traumatic or grief-laden — deepening immediacy there is exposure work; regulation is already poor; the marker is a proxy you like rather than one with predictive evidence.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Strong observational evidence that in-session experiencing predicts outcome across orientations; not MI-specific.

## What the book debunks

- **"Resistance"/"denial" as client traits.** Never confirmed by research; the authors retract their own editions 1–2 label and split it into sustain talk and discord, both jointly produced and turnable up or down by what the helper does. Confrontational "breaking down defences" exacerbates what it targets and would be malpractice in most other disorders; the immature-defence model of substance use disorders is rejected.
- **The decisional-balance intervention as a motivator (Box 6.1).** Listing all pros and cons has no theoretical or empirical basis as a change aid and *decreases* commitment in ambivalent people. Legitimate only as the tool of deliberate neutrality (Ch. 9) — never as a pre-test of whether to evoke, since measuring ambivalence that way is not inert.
- **"Hit bottom"/"wait until they've suffered enough,"** plus "families are powerless until the person is ready", plus the belief that people change when made to feel bad enough. Guilt and shame rarely fuel change; fear and shame shut people down.
- **Motivation as a trait or fuel gauge.** It is generated in a relationship and is task-specific — "motivated for treatment" is too broad; readiness attaches to a concrete action.
- **"MI is just good listening."** The common misunderstanding; training that under-teaches the directional component reduces efficacy. Equally rejected: MI as nondirective, as Rogers's client-centred counselling, as a quick technique, as a school demanding allegiance, as the transtheoretical model (stages of change belong elsewhere), or as assessment feedback.
- **"MI is the opposite of giving advice."** Unskillful direction is the problem, not advice — MI-trained nurses wrongly thought advising a new mother about spoon-feeding a 2-week-old would violate MI. But unsolicited advice is the junk mail of life, and rapid-fire single suggestions manufacture sustain talk whose refusals get misread as denial or difficulty — the book reattributes that pattern to the helper's technique, not the client's character.
- **"OARS are neutral tools."** All four carry direction whether or not you notice, which is why neutrality must be deliberate and is not achieved by "just not giving advice"; inadvertent nudging is the documented failure mode, invisible to both parties.
- **"I attended the workshop, so I do MI."** Workshops alone barely move coded practice; self-report of MI skill errs upward; certification proves competence at one moment, not current fidelity, and drift is the default. Personality, professional background and degrees do not predict who learns MI; clinical experience can even hurt coding accuracy, because clinicians infer meaning instead of recording what was said.
- **"No commitment language, no change."** All seven change-talk types favour subsequent change; a favourable balance predicts action without a declared intention.
- **"Feedback doesn't work."** A misreading of the check-up, which failed at its designed purpose (pushing people into treatment) while substantially reducing drinking outside treatment.
- **Documented misuse:** Danish "departure centres" ran mandatory police "motivational interviews" to push denied asylum seekers to leave; few signed voluntary-return forms, those who did rarely left, and both sides found the sessions aversive. Without a goal consistent with the person's own values the technique is inert, and coercive framing is not MI. Reframing an unshared institutional goal as "theirs" is the same misuse in miniature; using MI to obtain research consent is explicitly ruled out.
- **Tone inverts every technique.** Autonomy statements said grudgingly communicate one-up judgment; amplified reflection with sarcasm becomes mockery; coming alongside as a bluff becomes reverse psychology. Authority figures who overstate control ("You can't drink") say something untrue and invite the test they fear.

## Numbers that matter

- **Evidence base:** 2,000+ controlled trials; 200+ systematic reviews/meta-analyses; effects typically small-to-medium with wide between-study variance; **~25% of trials null**, and multisite trials show MI working at some sites and not others. Addiction is the strongest domain (25+ reviews examined); little support for MI alone in opioid or stimulant use disorders.
- **Practice ratios:** ~20% of time for pure engaging; reflections to questions at least 1:1, preferably **2:1**; interviewer speaks under half the time; reflection no longer than the person's utterance.
- **Taxonomy:** 7 change-talk types = 4 preparatory (DARN) + 3 mobilizing (CATs); perfect ambivalence = 50/50; 2–3 statements make a first bouquet.
- **Rulers and plans:** 0–10 importance and confidence; brief action planning = 5 steps, confidence threshold 7.
- **Instruments:** Box 7.1 = 100 adjectives, circle ≥5; Values Card Sort = 100 items, 5 piles (3 workable), top 5 or 10 ranked; Fig. 5.1 bubble sheet = 12 diabetes topics + 1 blank; MITI = 4 global 5-point scales (cultivating change talk, softening sustain talk, partnership, empathy) + 10 counted behaviours; 21 different MI rating instruments exist; 10-minute segments are the working unit; 40-item confidence self-check.
- **Training:** post-workshop residual gain ≈ one extra reflection per 10 minutes at 4 months, no change in client responses; on average five 30-minute coaching calls (up to six) reached trial-delivery competence; follow-up to 12 months; client language shifted only with feedback *and* coaching.
- **Timescales:** setbacks with addictive behaviour cluster in the **3–6 months** after initial consultation (the recommended follow-up window); stable weight loss ≈ 1–2 lb/week over months; dependent smokers average 3–4 serious quit attempts (an individual may need 6–7).
- **Case markers:** join-up at ~3 minutes in the Mike interview; change talk outweighs sustain talk ~3 minutes into the smoking transcript; ~7 minutes of pure listening before the presenting issue surfaced in the John transcript; the check-up client's 19 standard drinks/week = 98th percentile among US adult women (94th among women who drink).
- **History:** MI introduced 1983 (origins in 1982 conversations with Norwegian clinicians); editions 1991, 2002, 2013 (resistance split into sustain talk and discord), 2023; used on six continents in 75+ languages; ~40 cultural-adaptation studies; MINT founded 1997, TNT from 1993; MI deliberately never trademarked.

## Blind spots of this book

- **No RCT of the book, and none of most individual moves.** The evidence is method-level: whole-package trials plus process research on language. Many moves rest on a single annotated transcript or an explicit "no trial isolates this" (amplified reflection, reframing, pendulum, coming alongside, analogy). Package-level effects do not transfer automatically to any one micro-move.
- **Self-report is the weakest link in its own quality control.** Having refused to trademark or license MI, the authors concede there is no MI police, that far more people believe they deliver MI well than do, and that drift is expected — so any claim of "doing MI" carries no information without observed practice.
- **Mechanism is under-determined.** Self-regulation, self-determination, Rogers, dissonance and self-perception each explain part; none covers the findings. "Relational or technical, which matters more" is treated as a malformed question rather than answered.
- **Population and cultural limits.** The individual-autonomy framing is Western; in collectivist contexts the relevant "self" may be family or community — flagged, not resolved. Little is known about younger children (possibly a cognitive-development threshold for self-regulation), so the workaround is working through parents. Autonomy support was less prominent among Danish and German therapists than US ones.
- **Contraindication is acknowledged but thinly mapped.** Adverse effects at high initial readiness or confidence; MI alone insufficient for many concerns; a quarter of trials null. Beyond the high-readiness signal, the book does not specify who should not receive it.
- **Trauma, grief and severe psychopathology sit outside the method.** MI's role there is explicitly adjunctive — improving entry into and persistence in trauma treatment — while deep listening reliably opens material (a partner-hitting disclosure after seven minutes; a childhood bereavement behind vertical ambivalence) for which the method offers no protocol. For a lens sitting next to a live therapist, this is the boundary that matters most.
- **Edition and translation gap.** The persistence/growth thesis of Ch. 12, the "empowerment" and "fixing reflex" terminology and the pendulum renaming are 4th-edition-only; the circulating Russian translation is the 3rd edition, so none of it can be cited from there. Page numbers are edition-specific and the e-book's pagination does not match the print edition.
- **Directionality is an ethical burden handed back unresolved.** The book lists contested cases where MI gives no answer — vaccination, organ donation, euthanasia, harm reduction, pregnancy counselling, teen contraception, interrogation — and offers four guidelines (beneficence, autonomy, honesty, conflict of interest) rather than a decision rule. The greater your stake in someone's choice, the more you must shift toward declared neutrality; where your stake crosses that line, it cannot tell you.
