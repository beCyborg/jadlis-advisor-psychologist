# advisor-grief — moves

Citation prefix `[GR:]`. Canon source: Worden, *Grief Counseling and Grief Therapy*, 5th ed. (2018). Calibration source: PG26 digest (Verification corrections binding).

Tiers here are assigned **per operation** against safety-protocol §3, not copied from the source digest — the digest was written for a counsellor working face to face, this lens is read by a person alone next to a live therapist. Where §3 puts loss exposure and unfinished-grief work on the therapist side, the digest's SELF label was overridden and the move split so that no code carries two tiers:

- digest ACTUALIZE → `[GR:ACTUALIZE-RITUAL]` (SELF) + `[GR:RETELL-DEATH]` (REQUIRES-THERAPIST)
- digest LETTER-WRITE → `[GR:JOURNAL-NARRATIVE]` (SELF) + `[GR:LETTER-UNFINISHED]` (REQUIRES-THERAPIST)

Every code below is unique. Entry gate for the whole set: `[GR:MEASURE-FIRST]`.

## A. Is grief work indicated at all — targeting

### [GR:MEASURE-FIRST] Measure before any grief protocol runs
**Move:** Take a prolonged-grief symptom measure plus PHQ-9 before touching any technique below. Elevated symptoms are the entry condition for grief work; unremarkable scores mean no protocol — information, contact and time instead.
**APPLY WHEN:** "Unfinished grief" surfaces as a theme; before proposing any move in sections C–F.
**AVOID WHEN:** Scores unremarkable — running a protocol anyway (near-zero effect, potential harm at uncomplicated grief); treating one measurement as a forecast of the course.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** PG26 [PG26:GRIEF-GATE] — indicated-only effect MODERATE, treatment half is therapist-led; measurement itself is a self-administrable step.
### [GR:RISK-SCREEN] Route help to the indicated minority, not to everyone
**Move:** Around four weeks after the death, score the situation against the known risk profiles — Parkes & Weiss's eight dimensions (young children at home, low income, high anger, high pining, high self-reproach, thin relationships, poor-coping judgement), Raphael's predictors (perceived nonsupport, traumatic circumstances, ambivalent marriage, concurrent crisis) — and act only on a high profile, a high initial distress level, or one's own felt difficulty.
**APPLY WHEN:** Deciding whether help is warranted after a significant death; judging one's own state.
**AVOID WHEN:** Using a composite as a checklist that must fully match; reading an ICG score as a predictor rather than a snapshot; arranging help for every bereaved person by default — no benefit shown (Parkes 1998).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Prospective cohorts (Parkes & Weiss 1983; Raphael 1977; Worden 1996) plus meta-analytic reviews (Schut 2010; Larson & Hoyt 2007); converges with the indicated-only d≈0.53 anchor.
### [GR:MEDIATOR-MAP] Profile the loss against the seven mediators
**Move:** Run this specific loss through all seven: who died, the quality of the attachment (security, ambivalence, dependency, abuse history), how the death happened (sudden, violent, stigmatised, ambiguous, preventable), prior losses and prior low mood, personality and coping style, satisfaction with support (not its availability), concurrent stressors. The same grid explains why grieving is not moving.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any significant loss; deciding whether help is indicated; grief that has stalled.
**AVOID WHEN:** Letting one salient mediator (usually suddenness) carry the whole explanation; reading high pain by itself as a sign of pathology.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Framework synthesised from cohort studies (Harvard bereavement studies; Cleiren 1993; Bonanno 2002) — predictive-observational, the grid as a whole is untested.
### [GR:MOOD-SCREEN] Separate grief from a depressive picture; escalate when it fits
**Move:** Check for a global and persistent collapse of self-worth (grief guilt stays tied to the loss; in grief the world feels empty, in depression the self does), generalised culpability, symptoms still running through the first year, or grief escalating into a clinician-assessable disorder. Any of these goes to a professional evaluation, and the disorder is treated before the separation conflicts — medication can lift mood but does not resolve an attachment conflict.
**APPLY WHEN:** A depression-like state that is not lifting; prior depressive episodes; co-occurring anxiety, which is common (over 40% of bereaved spouses have an anxiety episode in year one, Jacobs 1990).
**AVOID WHEN:** Early transient sadness, broken sleep and lost appetite — that is ordinary grief, do not medicalise it; recommending medication for uncomplicated acute grief.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Worden's differential plus Zisook and Jacobs cohort work — method-level; no label is assigned by this lens, only a routing signal.
### [GR:SUICIDE-ASK] Ask the suicide question plainly
**Move:** Ask directly whether it has been bad enough to think about hurting oneself. Anger turned inward and the wish to rejoin the dead person both occur in ordinary grief; asking the question does not plant it.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any acute grief contact — checking oneself or supporting another person.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never avoid the question; but a yes moves to the crisis circuit and a live human, never to further self-guided work. Keep lethal quantities of medication away from an acute mourner.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Standard clinical safety practice plus Worden's case experience; the escalation branch is governed by safety-protocol §1, not by this lens.

## B. Orientation and psychoeducation

### [GR:NORM-MAP] Map reactions onto the ordinary-grief inventory
**Move:** Place what is happening into the four categories — feelings, bodily sensations, thoughts, behaviours — and name the ordinary items as ordinary: sensing the dead person's presence, hearing or seeing them, numbness, distractibility, dreams, relief, anger. Feeling like one is losing one's mind is not losing one's mind. Pre-load the course: long, wave-shaped, no return to a pre-loss state, support from others thinning out around three months, family members grieving in incompatible styles. Plan for the dates that will hit — three months, holidays, the first anniversary.
**APPLY WHEN:** Fear of going crazy; a first significant loss; a family policing one member's way of grieving; a hard date approaching.
**AVOID WHEN:** Reactions that are extreme in intensity AND still running late — that is a complication signal, not material to normalise; histories where reality testing is fragile.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Lindemann 1944 descriptive symptomatology; Demi & Miles 1987 Delphi consensus; Harvard observations; author's practice — descriptive, not trial evidence.
### [GR:TASK-MAP] Read mourning as tasks, then find the blocked one
**Move:** Replace "phases I have to sit through" with four tasks that can be worked: accepting the reality of the death intellectually and emotionally; going through the pain, which includes anger, guilt, anxiety and loneliness, not only sadness; adjusting to a world without the person, externally, internally and in what one assumed the world to be; finding an enduring place for the dead person while getting on with the rest of life. Then locate which one is jammed — what makes the death unbelievable, which feeling stays unfelt, which adjustment is refused, what blocks remembering while re-investing.
**APPLY WHEN:** Passivity and waiting for time to do the work; any stalled grief, as the organising assessment.
**AVOID WHEN:** Turning the tasks into a timetable ("I should be finished with the second one"); working task-two or task-four conflicts on loaded material alone — that is session work.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** The book's core framework; face-valid and clinically standard, but the model itself has never been trialled.
### [GR:DOSE] Dose the pain — oscillation, not immersion
**Move:** Go toward the painful material as far as is tolerable, then deliberately step back and do something restoration-shaped (logistics, work, the living), then go back. The alternation is the adaptive mechanism, not cheating; there is no requirement to be in the loss at all times.
**APPLY WHEN:** Fear of being swallowed by the feeling; white-knuckle constant confrontation; managing the gap between sessions.
**AVOID WHEN:** The stepping-back becomes permanent with no return trips; pressure — from oneself or others — to do pure grief work continuously.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Dual Process Model (Stroebe & Schut 1999/2010) — the best externally supported regulation principle in this tradition, though the model is descriptive rather than an outcome-trialled protocol.

## C. Self-manageable work in recent, uncomplicated loss

### [GR:ACTUALIZE-RITUAL] Make the death real through words, rituals and objects
**Move:** Use hard words and the past tense — died, not lost. Take up the offered choices rather than avoiding them: seeing the body when it is offered as an informed choice, a service that resembles the actual person, visiting the grave when the timing is right. Where there was no ritual — a miscarriage, a stillbirth, a burial missed — build tangibility instead: name the baby, hold a memorial, gather mementos, read the autopsy report to check the cause against what one imagines.
**APPLY WHEN:** A sense of unreality; a sudden death; the body was never seen; a loss with no ritual attached to it.
**AVOID WHEN:** Treating the ordinary early flicker between believing and not believing as pathology; pushing a viewing or a ritual on someone either way; a fixed conviction that the death did not happen, which is a psychiatric matter, not a ritual one.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Author's clinical experience plus ritual literature (Hoy 2013; Kristensen 2012); perinatal practices method-level (Brier 2008; one RCT, Pruitt Johnson & Langford 2015). Tier split from the digest's combined entry because the retelling half is an exposure operation — see [GR:RETELL-DEATH].
### [GR:JOURNAL-NARRATIVE] Keep a grief journal and build a coherent account
**Move:** Write the events down in a structured way — what happened, in what order, who said what — so the account becomes coherent rather than fragmentary; keep a dated journal with a stop rule. An online memorial page serves the same expressive and bond-keeping function.
**APPLY WHEN:** A fragmentary or scrambled account of the loss; feelings that have no outlet; a loss others do not treat as a real loss.
**AVOID WHEN:** The writing floods rather than settles — stop and route it to the session; unstructured venting while rumination-driven distress is already running; a memorial page standing in for live human contact; sessions longer than 45 minutes with no resolution, or closing tenser than opening.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level writing-therapy trials (O'Connor 2003; Ihrmark 2011); memorial-page use is descriptive only. Bounded by PG26: expressive writing is not unsafe by default, only unstructured venting during active rumination is contraindicated.
### [GR:GUILT-TEST] Reality-test the guilt that will not survive the facts
**Move:** Write the accusation out in full — the specific "I should have" — and check it against what was actually knowable and doable at the time, then enumerate what was in fact done, and what else, until the list runs out. Most grief guilt is not warranted by the facts and collapses under this.
**APPLY WHEN:** If-only loops; guilt fixed on the circumstances of the death; self-blame that repeats in the same words.
**AVOID WHEN:** The guilt survives the reality test because the culpability is real — that goes to [GR:CHAIR] and a therapist; guilt braided into traumatic imagery, which goes to [GR:TRAUMA-FIRST].
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Worden's technique, no trial; the target is well chosen — Stroebe 2014 (longitudinal) finds self-blame, not regret, the strongest determinant of grief outcome, and grief-specific.
### [GR:COPING-AUDIT] Audit coping, and ask about alcohol every time
**Move:** Map how the days are actually being got through, and ask specifically about alcohol and drugs, including the "just to sleep" version that escalates. Move deliberately from avoidant coping — blame, permanent distraction, denial, withdrawal, substances, the worst-outcome cluster — toward active emotional coping: reframing, humour, expression that includes some positive affect, letting support in. Problem-solving can be trained.
**APPLY WHEN:** Drinking is climbing; every reminder has been removed from the house; total withdrawal from people.
**AVOID WHEN:** Confronting the pattern before there is enough trust to survive the confrontation; taking short-term relief as proof that a strategy works.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Systematic review (Stahl & Schulz 2014); avoidant coping predicts posttraumatic and complicated-grief outcomes (Schnider 2007) — observational throughout.
### [GR:RUMINATE-REDIRECT] Break the loop, and give the bond a route other than pain
**Move:** For passive circling on the symptoms of grief: shift toward a solvable problem, rebuild contact in forms that do not exhaust the people offering it, and deliberately anchor the connection to the dead person in something other than suffering — the pain must stop being the last remaining tie.
**APPLY WHEN:** Chronic passive looping; the belief that the pain is what still connects; a meaning-search that runs in circles.
**AVOID WHEN:** The early preoccupation of fresh grief is being mislabelled as rumination — in the first weeks it is the normal state.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** The harms of rumination are solid observational work (Nolen-Hoeksema; Eisma 2015 — ruminators find less meaning, not more); the counter-moves are Worden's recommendations, untested.
### [GR:ROLE-SKILLS] Rebuild the roles the dead person carried; postpone the irreversible
**Move:** Inventory what the person actually did — decisions, paperwork, the emotional weather, the sexual relationship — turn each gap into a concrete problem, and train the missing skill. Counter helplessness by reviewing how things were managed before or without them. Meanwhile hold off on irreversible moves: selling the home, relocating, changing career, a quickly conceived next pregnancy. Frame the delay as a matter of readiness, never as prohibition. With older people, allow a transitional period of leaning on others first.
**APPLY WHEN:** Floundering in the practical adjustment; "I cannot survive on my own"; decisions that are visibly driven by avoiding pain; escalating dependence on adult children.
**AVOID WHEN:** Doing the tasks for the person instead of training them; using problem-solving to replace emotional processing when avoidance is the actual pattern; a delay framing that breeds helplessness or blocks a decision that genuinely cannot wait.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Mastery as an active component (Onrust 2007); a skills intervention in older widowed people (Caserta, Lund & Obray 2004); relocation and replacement-child harms are observational (Lopata 1996; Legg & Sherick 1976).
### [GR:RELOCATE-BOND] Plan remembrance, and license the rest of the life explicitly
**Move:** Address the fear of forgetting head-on and make remembrance concrete — objects kept on purpose, a memory book, a date in the calendar, the inherited traits named out loud. Then license re-investment in as many words: new people occupy the empty space without replacing the dead one and without dishonouring them.
**APPLY WHEN:** New attachments blocked by loyalty; fear that the memory is fading; a life that has been on hold since the death.
**AVOID WHEN:** The opposite pattern is running — fast replacement, a quick remarriage that cuts the grief short and adds a second loss later; pressure to move on before the pain has been worked at all; the excavating version of this move ("what would I lose if I gave up the grief") — that probes resistance and belongs in a session.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Continuing-bonds literature (Klass et al.; Field 1999 — the shift from possessions to memories marks a healthier bond) plus Worden's procedures (Powers & Wampold 1994); observational.
### [GR:MEANING] Work the meaning question only if the search is already running
**Move:** If a meaning search is spontaneously present and stuck, make the questions explicit — what this life looks like now, who one is after this death — and work them through telling the story rather than answering them abstractly. Channels that carry the question outward (a memorial, a scholarship, advocacy tied to how the person died) belong here. Expect to return to it at later life stages.
**APPLY WHEN:** A meaning search is already underway on its own; the death feels senseless; the assumptive world has been broken.
**AVOID WHEN:** No spontaneous search exists — do not start one: 20–30% adapt well without ever making meaning; any growth, silver-lining or "what this taught you" framing, which invalidates the loss and skips the pain (Gamino & Sewell 2004); this lens never offers a positive reframe of a death.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Neimeyer's framework plus Davis 2000 (correlational; those who search and fail adjust worst); reframing linked to better outcomes in the Harvard data — observational, direction of causation unestablished.
### [GR:JOIN-GROUP] Join a group matched to the kind of loss
**Move:** Join a bereavement group that matches the loss type, or at minimum contains one other person with the same loss — a lone widower among widows becomes the group's exception, a lone suicide survivor raises the anxiety of the whole room. The established networks are loss-specific: The Compassionate Friends, SIDS networks, survivors-of-suicide groups, Parents of Murdered Children.
**APPLY WHEN:** At least six weeks after the death; isolation; a loss that others do not recognise as one; the sense that nobody who has not been through it can understand.
**AVOID WHEN:** Under six weeks post-loss; serious co-occurring difficulty that needs individual work first; several stacked losses; using a group as a substitute for indicated treatment at elevated symptoms.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme experience (Hospice of Pasadena); trials of groups for complicated grief are mixed (Piper 2009; Johnsen 2012); one RCT in HIV-related bereavement (Sikkema 2006).

## D. Children, anticipatory loss, and the family

### [GR:CHILD-NEEDS] Cover the child's list; invest in the surviving parent
**Move:** Answer the question the child will not ask — who will take care of me — before it is asked; absolve them of the magical self-blame; give clear age-appropriate facts including that the illness is not catching; involve them; keep routines, and read play as coping rather than indifference; answer the same question as many times as it is asked; build ways to remember, such as a family memory book. Offer the funeral as a coached choice, not a requirement. Above all, put resources into the surviving parent's functioning — it is the strongest predictor of how the child comes out.
**APPLY WHEN:** A child has lost a parent or a sibling; designing what happens in the household after a parental death.
**AVOID WHEN:** Euphemisms and information vacuums, which children fill with worse stories; compelled funeral attendance; intervening with the child while the surviving parent is left unsupported; blanket programmes instead of screening.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Harvard Child Bereavement Study (125 children, 70 families, two-year controlled follow-up): around 80% cope, parent functioning is the strongest predictor, ages 5–7 most vulnerable; parent-targeted programmes trialled (Sandler 2010/2016) — the strongest evidence in the whole lens.
### [GR:WORRY-WORK] Before an expected death, legitimise the work of worry
**Move:** Read rehearsal aloud — where will I live, how will the money work — as adaptive coping, and say so both to the person doing it and to the relatives who hear it as coldness; block the reassurances that shut it down. Prompt unfinished business while the person is still alive: appreciation and disappointment both, with explicit permission. Name the two distance failures — saying goodbye so completely that the patient's survival produces resentment and guilt, and ambivalence that produces smothering closeness.
**APPLY WHEN:** A terminal illness in the family; caregiver guilt; the family judging one member's planning talk.
**AVOID WHEN:** Pushing rehearsal on someone who is not doing it; forcing a confrontation that loads the dying person; promising that forewarning makes the bereavement easier — that is unsettled (Parkes yes, Nielsen 2016 systematic review no effect).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Janis 1958 analogue work; pre-death communication predicts outcomes (Metzger & Gray 2008); clinical observation (Weisman & Hackett 1961).
### [GR:FAMILY-WORK] Treat the family as the grieving unit
**Move:** See survivors individually and at least once together — live interaction is what reveals the vacated role (watch for a teenager being told he is the man of the house now), the alliances, and the scapegoating of displaced anger, often onto the most vulnerable child. Take a two-generation loss history, since unresolved grief transmits. Have the family tell the story of the dead person together, building a shared meaning without forcing agreement; inducing the mourning response in one member during a conjoint session (operational mourning, Paul & Grosser) is a therapist manoeuvre.
**APPLY WHEN:** Friction in the household after a death; the death of a child, where blame and mismatched grieving styles between partners are the standard failure; a family holding a myth or a silence.
**AVOID WHEN:** Visibly siding with the more expressive partner; forcing a single agreed narrative; assigning the dead person's role to a child; attempting operational mourning outside conjoint family therapy.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Bowen systems tradition plus Nadeau 1998; Kissane 2006 — family intervention effective for a screened at-risk subset, which is exactly the indicated-only pattern; Sandler's Family Bereavement Program.

## E. Old, stuck or complicated grief

### [GR:UNRESOLVED-CLUES] Screen old losses against Lazare's twelve clues
**Move:** Take a structured history of losses, then check it against the clue list: fresh intense grief when an old loss comes up; outsized reaction to a minor loss or someone else's; loss as a recurring theme; possessions frozen in place or all discarded at once; physical symptoms resembling the dead person's, timed to an anniversary or to reaching their age; a radical cut-off in lifestyle; a long low-grade depression carrying guilt; compulsive imitation; self-destructive impulses; sadness at the same point every year; a phobia of the illness that killed them; rituals and support missing at the time of the death.
**APPLY WHEN:** Vague bodily complaints or stuck patterns that might be loss-driven; grief surfacing late through a lesser loss or through something in the media.
**AVOID WHEN:** Reading any single clue as diagnostic — one is never enough; judging what has been kept without the cultural and religious context; pressing for disclosure during an acute crisis.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Lazare's clinical taxonomy (1979/1989), endorsed by Worden; never validated as an instrument — a prompt list, not a screen with properties.
### [GR:CONTRACT] Contract explicitly for time-limited grief therapy
**Move:** For grief that is genuinely stuck, contract for roughly 8–10 weekly sessions focused on the loss, with intake screening for co-occurring low mood, anxiety, trauma and personality-level difficulty, and treat off-topic drift as material to be named and returned. The working mechanism is going through the thoughts and feelings that were avoided, with the therapist supplying the social permission that was missing at the time.
**APPLY WHEN:** Focused unresolved grief, chronic or delayed; the person's own verdict that this is not ending, typically two to five years out.
**AVOID WHEN:** Serious underlying difficulty surfaces and needs longer non-grief treatment; dependent structures for which brief work is not enough.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Worden's own practice format; the trialled method-level analogue is Shear's 16-session complicated-grief treatment. PG26 correction applies: at elevated symptoms grief work helps, but no brand wins (Hao 2024 network meta-analysis, 55 studies, N=10,330, five modalities with overlapping intervals).
### [GR:CHAIR] Empty chair and psychodrama with the dead person
**Move:** The high-leverage mechanism of this tradition: speaking to the dead person in the present tense rather than about them. Empty chair or eyes-closed imaginal address; power increases by switching seats and answering as them; a photograph can anchor the address. Where the culpability is real and survived reality testing, the exchange takes the form of asking for and granting forgiveness, ending in something reconciled.
**APPLY WHEN:** Unfinished business, guilt, regret, a finality that will not land; grief stuck for years; genuine culpability that [GR:GUILT-TEST] did not dissolve.
**AVOID WHEN:** Psychosis-spectrum presentations or unstable identity and reality testing — explicitly contraindicated by the author; any solo attempt on a loaded loss; an untrained facilitator; guilt that is not warranted by the facts, which belongs to [GR:GUILT-TEST] instead.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Gestalt tradition (Polster & Polster), paradigm support (Field & Horowitz 1998), extensive case work — no RCT. PG26 reinforces the tier: chair work with vulnerable material is author-contraindicated at risk of retraumatisation, self-harm or suicidality, and the state gate before it is not self-administrable.
### [GR:LETTER-UNFINISHED] Write the letter that says the unsaid
**Move:** Write to the dead person to say what was never said, including a letter of farewell — the version that deliberately opens the unfinished business rather than recording events.
**APPLY WHEN:** Unfinished business with elevated grief symptoms; feelings never expressed to the person while they lived; a loss with no ritual through which to say it.
**AVOID WHEN:** The death was traumatic and the writing floods instead of integrating; done alone with no session in which to land what it opens — this is an unfinished-grief operation, which safety-protocol §3 places on the therapist side even though the digest tiered the combined move as SELF.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Method-level writing-therapy trials (O'Connor 2003; Ihrmark 2011) — the trials were conducted with clinician structure around them; no solo format has been tested. Split from the digest's LETTER-WRITE; the recording half is [GR:JOURNAL-NARRATIVE].
### [GR:AMBIV-GRADE] Graded memory work where the bond was ambivalent
**Move:** Bring memories back in order — plenty of positive ones first as ballast, then the mixed ones, then the hurt and the anger, approached obliquely (what is missed, what is not missed, how they disappointed) rather than by asking about anger head-on. Reverse the order if the person arrives entirely negative. Always rebalance so that one valence does not erase the other. Where the dead person has become larger than life, allow the idealisation first and surface the ambivalence later, establishing that anger does not cancel love. Tailor to attachment style: preoccupied toward internalised proximity, dismissing with an eye on bodily expression, fearful with protection against isolation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Idealisation with anger suspected underneath; ambivalent or dependent bonds; relationship business left open.
**AVOID WHEN:** Jumping to the negative material without the positive groundwork; surfacing anger and leaving it unresolved, which can turn it inward and leave the person worse than before; typing someone's attachment style from a single behaviour.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Worden's method plus attachment-bereavement theory (Mikulincer & Shaver; Field 2006); the tailoring rules have never been tested.
### [GR:LINK-OBJECT] Work the linking object rather than removing it
**Move:** Distinguish an ordinary keepsake from a linking object — an item so invested that its absence triggers panic. Bring the object into the session and examine what conflict it is holding outside the person. Expect it to be put away spontaneously as the work completes; in the Harvard case the boy's cap was worn in year one, hung on the bedpost in year two, and put in the closet after that.
**APPLY WHEN:** The object has to be carried at all times; panic when it cannot be found; a room preserved exactly as it was.
**AVOID WHEN:** Confiscating it or pushing disposal — the movement has to come from the person; judging what is kept without the cultural or religious context.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Volkan's clinical concept plus Worden's cases; Field 1999 on the shift from possessions to memories is observational.
### [GR:MULTI-LOSS] Grieve stacked losses one at a time, easiest first
**Move:** Where several deaths arrived together or in a short window, take them separately and start with the relationship carrying the fewest complications, then move to progressively harder ones. Starting at the easy end is what unfreezes a state where there is too much to feel at all.
**APPLY WHEN:** Multiple deaths; flat affect that will not move; accumulated losses presenting as anxiety.
**AVOID WHEN:** Trying to take them all at once; starting with the most traumatic one; working an overwhelming stack alone.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Worden's clinical practice, case reports only; the overload concept comes from Kastenbaum 1969.
### [GR:RETELL-DEATH] Repeated detailed retelling of the death
**Move:** Tell the death through in detail and repeatedly — where it happened, how, who delivered the news, what the funeral was like — until the account stops being unbearable and starts being a memory.
**APPLY WHEN:** Persistent disbelief that the ritual and reality-word work in [GR:ACTUALIZE-RITUAL] has not shifted; elevated grief symptoms confirmed by [GR:MEASURE-FIRST].
**AVOID WHEN:** Any solo attempt; someone already flooded; before stabilisation where the death was traumatic — [GR:TRAUMA-FIRST] comes first.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Worden's clinical experience; tiered here against the digest, because repeated retelling of the loss is an exposure operation and safety-protocol §3 plus PG26 place loss exposure and prompted retelling with a therapist — no solo format has been tested.

## F. Traumatic and suicide loss

### [GR:TRAUMA-FIRST] Sequence the trauma work ahead of the grief work
**Move:** After a violent or traumatic death — a homicide, discovering a suicide, mass violence, a death witnessed — bring down the intrusions, the avoidance and the hyperarousal first, because they disable the reflective work that mourning requires. Open with support and stabilisation, and only later work the mediators of the mourning. In the acute hours, give firm concrete help, pair plain death words with logistics, lend strength briefly and then hand functioning back.
**APPLY WHEN:** Posttraumatic symptoms after a loss; the death scene monopolising memory; a numb survivor in the first hours or days.
**AVOID WHEN:** Leading with grief excavation in someone already overwhelmed; a takeover long enough to build dependence; the blunt words without the support half.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Method-level consensus (Parkes 1993; Rando 1993; Rynearson's homicide-bereavement programme); crisis-intervention tradition since Lindemann 1944.
### [GR:RESTORATIVE-RETELL] Defuse the death imagery so the person becomes rememberable
**Move:** Where the death scene has taken over all memory of the person: restorative retelling with graded imagery work on the worst images, or EMDR as an alternative on intrusive discovery images, so that the peak affect subsides and ordinary memories become reachable again.
**APPLY WHEN:** Intrusive imagery of the death, witnessed or imagined, blocking access to any other memory of the person.
**AVOID WHEN:** Any solo attempt; before stabilisation ([GR:TRAUMA-FIRST]); a provider without training in the specific procedure.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Rynearson's restorative retelling (programme literature); EMDR support here is small studies (Sprang 2001; Solomon & Rando 2007) and modest.
### [GR:SUICIDE-WORK] The suicide-bereavement package
**Move:** Get in early, before the family myth sets, and use accurate language — died by suicide, not committed. Reality-test the guilt and the blame, including a search for the evidence that the survivor did reach out, and normalise the relief that follows years of crisis. Correct denial with direct words. Explore the imagined death scene, since for those who did not see it the imagination is usually worse than the fact. Rebuild the split image so the person is one person who also had an illness. Permit the rage while the survivor keeps control of how it is expressed. Reality-test the sense of having been abandoned. Sit with the unanswerable question rather than closing it. Plan for the real fears about the future, and monitor the survivors' own risk — postvention is prevention.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any loss to suicide; a family accident-myth; scapegoating; a son who fears the same end.
**AVOID WHEN:** Excavating culpability or death imagery alone; discharging rage with nothing to contain it; assuming the family will break — some come out closer.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Shneidman and Jordan postvention tradition; Worden's cases; Mitchell 2003 group study. The claim that suicide grief is inherently more intense is contested (Farberow versus Cleiren) — do not present it as established.
