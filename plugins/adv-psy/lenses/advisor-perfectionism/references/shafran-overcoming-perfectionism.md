# Overcoming Perfectionism — digest

## What this book is for the lens
The only source in the lens whose exact protocol has been trialled as a book, in the format the user will actually use it (unguided and guided self-help), with maintenance measured at 6 months.
Its mechanism-level thesis: the problem is not high standards but **self-worth made contingent on striving and achievement**, kept alive by rigid rules, all-or-nothing appraisal, counterproductive "safety" behaviours, and a rigged scoring system where missing a standard means personal failure and meeting one gets discounted so the bar rises.
Where it beats other sources: it converts that model into ~40 numbered worksheets, each fixing what to record, in what order, on which 0–100 rating scale — so the lens can hand over a procedure, not a principle.
It also beats others on **calibrated honesty**: it names its own failure modes (unguided use moved perfectionism but not anxiety/depression in the 2014 RCT), refuses to promise better performance, and prints a worked example where the compassionate response made mood *worse* first.
Delivery guidance to carry into any use: roughly one chapter per week, a fixed weekly slot, a pre-set approximate time cap for reading plus exercises, Part One read before Part Two tasks — and the schedule itself held as a guideline, never as a standard to fail.

## Core model
Maintenance cycle (Figure 3.1, personalised via Worksheet 5.1): self-evaluation depends on achievement → excessively high, self-imposed standards → rigid rules for meeting them → counterproductive behaviours (avoidance, procrastination, performance checking, over-thoroughness, multi-tasking, list-making, all-nighters) → **either** perceived failure (all-or-nothing appraisal → self-criticism → overgeneralisation to "I am a failure" → worth binds tighter to achievement) **or** success (discounted as "too easy", bar re-set higher). Anxiety, low mood and stress feed back into the loop; attention is biased toward failures over successes.
Three defining components (Ch. 1): demanding standards with self-criticism when missed; continued striving despite documented costs; self-evaluation contingent on achievement. Healthy striving differs on exactly two axes — worth contingency and persistence despite cost — so an elite athlete with high standards and intact self-worth is not the target.
Causes are deliberately deprioritised (Ch. 3): heritability 24–49%, retrospective aetiology data, and the authors' plain statement that knowing origins does not drive change. Treatment targets maintainers only (Ch. 4–5).
Programme map: Ch. 6 motivation → 7.1 areas + self-monitoring → 7.2 psychoeducation → 7.3 surveys → 7.4 behavioural experiments → 7.5 all-or-nothing/rules → 7.6 noticing the positive → 7.7 thinking styles → 7.8 procrastination/time/pleasant events → 7.9 integration → Ch. 8 self-criticism and compassion → Ch. 9 broadening self-evaluation → Ch. 10 freedom/relapse prevention. Blank worksheets sit in the back matter and at www.overcoming.co.uk.
Chapter-8 stance on the critical voice: a volume knob that turns down but never off, paired with turning the compassionate voice up — because "silence the critic" simply becomes one more standard to fail.

## Moves

### [PERF:SCREEN9] Nine-question fit screen (Worksheet 1.1)
**Move:** Answer the nine markers — relentless striving; attention on the unachieved; others calling the standards too high; fear of failing them; ratcheting the bar after success; self-worth resting on striving/achievement; repeated performance checking; persisting despite lost opportunities; avoidance or delay driven by fear of failure or time cost. Reading rule: yes to the self-worth item (Q6) plus a majority of the rest = unhelpful perfectionism, not healthy striving, and a likely fit for the programme.
**APPLY WHEN:** deciding whether the perfectionism lens applies at all; separating "high standards" from worth-contingency before committing time.
**AVOID WHEN:** treated as a diagnosis, or converted into one more arena to score well in.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Part of the self-help package tested in ≥4 direct RCTs of this book; no standalone psychometrics reported — it is a fit screen, not an instrument.
### [PERF:CYCLE-MAP] Personal maintenance diagram (Worksheet 5.1)
**Move:** Build your own version of the Figure 3.1 cycle through eight prompts: (1) is self-worth contingent on striving/achievement; (2) the excessively high standards; (3) the rules serving them; (4) all-or-nothing appraisal of a recent near-miss as failure; (5) the self-criticism that follows, with actual phrasing; (6) the counterproductive behaviours and their triggers; (7) where anxiety/low mood/stress feed in; (8) what happens when you DO meet a standard — how long satisfaction lasts, how it gets discounted, how the bar moves. Draw the arrows, including the loop from success back to raised standards. Then note your reaction to seeing it.
**APPLY WHEN:** at programme start before any change technique; again whenever motivation drops and the thread of "why this persists" is lost.
**AVOID WHEN:** the diagram becomes a perfectionist artefact — hunting for the correct arrows; a rough, example-rich sketch beats a tidy one, and it is meant to be revised throughout.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** First step of the protocol carried in all the book's RCT formats; CBT formulation is method-level standard.
### [PERF:AREA-MONITOR] Area map plus one week of live self-monitoring (Worksheets 7.1.1, 7.1.2)
**Move:** Circle which of the 19 listed areas apply (eating, shape, weight, social performance, checking locks/appliances, ordering, organisation, cleanliness, appearance, hygiene, artistic, musical, sporting, academic, work performance, intimate relationships, parenting, health and fitness, entertaining) and write the typical thought and typical behaviour for each. Then log for one week, in four columns and **at the time it happens**: area + situation; thought with belief rating 0–100%; behaviour; feelings rated 0–100%. Keep sheets physically to hand, set a daily review time or phone alarm, then hunt for repeating areas, thoughts, behaviours and the thought→behaviour→bad-feeling chain.
**APPLY WHEN:** always, before any change technique — and again later to measure change; especially when the user assumes perfectionism lives in only one domain.
**AVOID WHEN:** recorded retrospectively (markedly weaker), rewritten to look good, or made exhaustive; if fear of being upset by the records blocks starting, treat that fear as the first belief to test rather than a reason to skip.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core component of the RCT-tested package; self-monitoring itself has method-level support in anxiety, depression and eating problems, and the act of recording reduces the behaviour.
### [PERF:SURVEY] Survey other people (Section 7.3)
**Move:** Pick one belief or standard and write 3–8 concrete questions — frequency, duration, or judgement. Choose respondents strategically (people you respect for mistake-beliefs; objectively successful people for success-beliefs; note respondent sex where relevant). Summarise as a table or graph and extract three products: a reality check on your standard; the **spread** of answers as evidence against black-and-white rules; and any residual doubts converted into behavioural experiments. Same method covers the double standard — ask what standards others hold themselves to, and expect the counterintuitive finding that many non-perfectionists are more lenient with themselves than with others.
**APPLY WHEN:** the standard rides on fear of others' judgement; you suspect your bar sits far above the norm; the belief is "everyone manages this effortlessly except me".
**AVOID WHEN:** the standard is moral rather than comparative ("I don't care what others do") — surveys land flat there, use [PERF:RISK-BOTH-WAYS]; also when the survey runs long (>8 questions burdens respondents), when results are read as reassurance rather than calibration, or when a range of opinions is misread as "so my standard is fine". Some beliefs survey TRUE and then need acceptance, not argument.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core component of the protocol with ≥4 direct RCTs of this book; surveys are standard CBT method in social anxiety and OCD.
### [PERF:BEHEXP] Seven-step behavioural experiment (Worksheet 7.4.1 and variants)
**Move:** The book's central change engine. (1) State the belief. (2) Derive the general prediction. (3) Specify it precisely — observable behaviours plus belief and emotion ratings 0–100%. (4) Run it. (5) Record results. (6) Reflect. (7) Write the revised belief. Build from trivial stakes upward — lose a street race before risking a work deadline. Two highest-yield variants have their own worksheets: **sub-par performance** (7.5.3 — meet friends with messy hair and no make-up, bring a lopsided cake, leave the office without filing, host with one clean room) and **time-capping** (7.5.4 — Suzie cut case notes from 4 hours to 1 hour nightly for a week, zero client complaints), where quality holds because much of the "extra" time was anxiety and repetition, not work. Other stock targets: work hard / moderately / minimally on three comparable tasks; deliberately not come top; leave something half-finished; drop one item off the to-do list; train hard vs. normally on alternate weeks; and after meeting a goal, **hold the standard instead of raising it** and compare distress and self-worth against the usual bar-raise.
**APPLY WHEN:** a belief survives fact-finding and surveys; a specific falsifiable prediction exists; the target is a non-traumatic everyday task (an email, a clean-up, a report, hosting); or hours spent far exceed what the outcome needs.
**AVOID WHEN:** predictions left vague (nothing can be disconfirmed); the first test is high-stakes instead of graded; the test is so trivial the belief never engages; a single experiment is treated as decisive — the book prescribes many, across many beliefs; the target opens trauma material or is really a formal exposure programme; or the standard has a genuine safety function (the book's example: shoes in doorways really do trip people).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core component of the protocol with ≥4 direct RCTs of this book; behavioural experiments carry method-level trial support across CBT; the authors report clinically that clients typically get similar results with less time.
### [PERF:CONTRAST] Alternating-days safety-behaviour contrast (Box 7.4.1)
**Move:** Run the ritual as usual (or more) on odd days and once only on even days; rate worry 0–10 and certainty 0–10 immediately after and again one hour later; compare on day 7. Jeff's data ran opposite to his prediction — repeated-check days: worry 8–9 immediately, 7–8 at one hour, certainty 1–2; single-check days: worry 4 immediately, 1–4 at one hour, certainty 8–9 sustained. Generalises to perfectionism-"on" vs perfectionism-"off" weeks for training, report-writing, tidying before guests.
**APPLY WHEN:** checking, reassurance calls, mental replaying of conversations, post-event processing — any ritual believed to reduce anxiety or errors. This is also the closest instrument the book offers for reassurance-seeking inside a relationship.
**AVOID WHEN:** the days aren't comparable (confounded tasks); fatigue is high — Jeff's overwork made worries "take hold", so interpret with load in mind.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Direct application of checking research (repeated checking lowers confidence in memory — method-level experimental work) inside the RCT-tested package.
### [PERF:CONTINUUM] Continuum against all-or-nothing categories (Worksheet 7.5.2)
**Move:** Write the dichotomous thought, name the two poles, then place at least three **real** examples of your own behaviour at intermediate points, and write down what the spread shows. Variant: place people you know on a good–bad continuum and observe that nobody sits at either pole.
**APPLY WHEN:** behaviour is described in polar categories — total work vs no work, perfect diet vs abandoned diet, clean vs filthy, competent vs fraud.
**AVOID WHEN:** no concrete examples are collected (abstract lines don't move belief), or it is used as a debating tactic instead of evidence-gathering.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct RCT support; dichotomous thinking's maintenance role backed by Oxford and Egan et al. (Curtin) correlational work identifying it as a key factor separating healthy striving from unhelpful perfectionism.
### [PERF:RULE-LIST] Rules into guidelines
**Move:** List every personal rule, "must" and "should" — Fatima produced about 30 in roughly two minutes. Rewrite selected ones as guidelines that permit exceptions ("never eat chocolate" → "aim to eat healthily; occasional chocolate is fine"), and verify the relaxation is safe with behavioural experiments. Fairburn's distinction is the operative one: a rule snaps when violated, a guideline flexes. At sentence level (Box 7.7.1) do the same to should-talk: "I would like to / I'd prefer to" **plus** an explicit "and it's OK if I don't" — the worked example, "I should exercise 7 days a week", had produced avoidance of exercise entirely.
**APPLY WHEN:** self-imposed regulations feel like a prison; rules have spread to trivia (birthdays, oil changes, calorie caps, email wording); self-talk is saturated with should/must and runs the pressure → self-criticism → avoidance sequence.
**AVOID WHEN:** the rule has a genuine safety or ethical function; or the rewrite stays verbal — a reworded sentence is not sufficient if the underlying rule is untouched and untested.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct RCTs of this book; rule/guideline distinction credited to Christopher Fairburn; the claim that flexible phrasing makes attainment more likely is the authors' clinical reasoning, not trial data.
### [PERF:ATTN-BROADEN] Broaden attention in the moment (Worksheet 7.6.1)
**Move:** Name the perfectionism area, record the negative-focus thoughts with 0–100% belief ratings, then widen attention two ways: (a) ask what positive evidence about the performance is being ignored; (b) push focus outward onto the situation — the content of the conversation, colours, surrounding detail, asking the other person questions. Record what happened. Mechanism: selective monitoring for flaws works like noticing your car model everywhere after buying it, and sustained attention on a domain also inflates its importance.
**APPLY WHEN:** rumination on one flaw hijacks a live situation — a crease in a shirt mid-conversation, one error at a dinner party.
**AVOID WHEN:** applied only retrospectively (it works best in the moment); or it slides into suppressing the negative or seeking reassurance rather than adding the rest of the picture.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct RCT support; attention-bias work has method-level support.
### [PERF:POS-DIARY] Positive-evidence diary, including absent criticism (Worksheet 7.6.2 + Ch. 9 daily log)
**Move:** Keep a standing diary in three columns — area; positive evidence about performance; and the **absence** of negative comment (nobody criticised the presentation; the boss said nothing). Extend it with the Chapter 9 version: each day log at least one thing you did, deliberately including small things you would normally dismiss, spread across all life areas — Stephen's entries included walking the dog instead of working, starting an avoided job, phoning his parents while busy, keeping his cool with a difficult client, choosing a picnic over work.
**APPLY WHEN:** long-standing discounting of positives; achievements dismissed as "must have been too easy"; low baseline sense of doing anything well.
**AVOID WHEN:** stopped after one week — the book says several weeks of active searching are needed before balanced noticing becomes automatic; entries pre-filtered for "worthy" items or restricted to work output, which reproduces the bias; or the log itself becomes a standard to hit.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct RCT support; the absent-negative-evidence column is the authors' clinical addition.
### [PERF:THOUGHT-DIARY] Five-step thought diary (Worksheet 7.7.3)
**Move:** (1) Activating event — an event, image or memory. (2) Beliefs, each tagged with its thinking-style label and rated 0–100, including "what does this say about me as a person?". (3) Feelings rated 0–100. (4) Disputation — what would a friend say; what alternative views exist; what positives are being ignored; is this thought helpful. (5) Re-rate the feelings. Do 2–3 per week when strong emotion hits, and reuse recurring alternatives across situations. Perfectionism-typical styles to label: double standards, overgeneralising, "should" statements; plus catastrophising, emotional reasoning, labelling, personalisation, mind-reading, predictive thinking.
**APPLY WHEN:** any strong negative reaction tied to performance evaluation; recurring, identifiable self-critical or catastrophic appraisals.
**AVOID WHEN:** challenged only in your head — the book insists the writing is the mechanism; the first attempt feeling hard is normal and not a stop signal; and the activating event must not be a traumatic memory or image (that is [PERF:IMG-RESCRIPT] territory).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core CBT method with broad method-level trial support; programme component in the book's direct RCTs. Aimee's worked example: anxiety 95% → 60%, sadness 50% → 30% after one diary.
### [PERF:PROC-AUDIT] Procrastination map, log and cost challenge (Worksheets 7.8.1–7.8.4)
**Move:** Three passes before any fix. (1) Mark which perfectionism domains you delay in and write a concrete delay behaviour for each (put off phoning a friend, ask for an extension, postpone violin practice, delay the report). (2) Log episodes — area/situation; the perfectionist **prediction made at the moment of deciding to delay**, rated 0–100%; the actual delay behaviours (clock-watching, over-detailed to-do lists, aligning business cards); feelings 0–100%. (3) In a fifth column write exactly how each delay **raised** belief in the prediction, since the prediction never got tested. Then tabulate benefits and costs of procrastinating and explicitly challenge each supposed benefit — the relief is real but brief, tasks pile up, time shortage drops performance, overwhelm grows.
**APPLY WHEN:** chronic delay, deadline-crunch pattern, mounting undone tasks; delay defended as anxiety management or read as laziness/time-management.
**AVOID WHEN:** logged from memory days later (the prediction is lost); the benefits column left blank out of shame; the cost table left without the challenge column — the challenge is the active ingredient; or the exercise becomes another self-criticism vehicle.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme components with direct RCT support; procrastination gets the largest worksheet block in the book, and predicts worse academic performance in cited research.
### [PERF:PROC-EXP] Undersized experiment against the delay prediction (Worksheet 7.8.5)
**Move:** Take the prediction behind the delay ("they'll think I'm stupid") and test it with a deliberately undersized action. Gemma, instead of cancelling supervision, brought two pages of draft and discussed ideas; the supervisors did not ridicule her. Anxiety 90% → 45%, sadness 40% → 20%, "I will never write a good proposal" 90% → 65%.
**APPLY WHEN:** a testable prediction sits behind the delay and some concrete first action exists.
**AVOID WHEN:** the experiment is sized too big — the deliberately small start was the working ingredient; or the prediction is left unrated.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct RCTs of this book.
### [PERF:CHUNK] Difficulty-rated task ladder (Worksheet 7.8.9)
**Move:** Define the goal, break it into chunks, rate each 0–100 for difficulty on an 11-step ladder (Simon's flat: 0 = think about the problem … 100 = clean the oven), and **start at the easy end now** rather than at the most important piece and rather than waiting to feel motivated.
**APPLY WHEN:** the task feels monolithic; starting is the blocked step; the start keeps sliding.
**AVOID WHEN:** chunks are left too large (an oversized step re-triggers the delay), or the chunking is done so exhaustively that planning replaces doing.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct RCT support; graded task assignment has method-level support.
### [PERF:BALANCE-SCHEDULE] Weekly grid plus scheduled pleasant events (Worksheets 7.8.11, 7.8.12)
**Move:** Fill the hourly grid — 7 a.m. to 10 p.m., Monday to Sunday, 15 slots × 7 days — planning achievement time **and** recovery time, then answer what it showed about the balance and what must change. Plant several items from the ~65-item pleasant-activities menu (walks, swimming, cooking, music, reading, baths, board games, yoga, meditation, calling a friend, doing something for someone else) into the grid as fixed entries, and review the mood effect at week's end. Aimee scheduled TV with her partner, dinners out, magazines, a beauty therapist.
**APPLY WHEN:** overwork, working into the night, chronic 7 p.m. office exits, no scheduled recovery, guilt about "wasting time", relationship strain from constant busyness, low mood.
**AVOID WHEN:** the grid gets filled only with achievement blocks, becomes another rigid rule to fail, or the activities themselves get rated for how well they were done; also when it is dismissed as too simple to help — the book pre-empts exactly that discount.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme components with direct RCT support; activity scheduling and behavioural activation carry strong method-level support for mood.
### [PERF:PROBSOLVE] Six-step structured problem-solving (Worksheet 7.8.10)
**Move:** Define the problem objectively and specifically; brainstorm without judging; underline two or three viable options; weigh feasibility and likely effect and choose one; plan the concrete steps; carry it out; evaluate the effect and note what it taught you about intervening earlier. Aimee's version: vacuum only the two rooms guests would use, then switch to cooking as a distraction.
**APPLY WHEN:** the obstacle is a real practical constraint — also useful for generating chunk lists and alternatives to all-or-nothing behaviour.
**AVOID WHEN:** ideas are judged during the brainstorm (explicitly barred until the shortlist step); or it is used to route around testing a belief when the obstacle is predictive rather than practical.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component; structured problem-solving has method-level trial support as a general CBT technique.
### [PERF:RULERS] Importance and confidence rulers (Worksheet 6.1)
**Move:** Rate importance of changing perfectionism 0–10 and confidence in your ability to change 0–10 **separately**. For each, ask "why not 0?" (surfaces existing motivation and resources) and "why not 10 — what would raise it, what would I notice, who could help?". Re-rate both after the chapter's exercises and reflect on what shifted.
**APPLY WHEN:** at programme start; when motivation stalls mid-programme; when the user says they want to change but keeps not starting.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to talk yourself into a higher number; and if importance stays very low, the book itself says this may not be the right time to start — a low confidence score is data about what support to arrange, not a failing.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Component of the RCT-tested package; motivational-interviewing rulers have method-level support outside this book, but were not trialled standalone here.
### [PERF:PROSCONS] Four-cell decisional balance plus one-year projection (Worksheets 6.2, 6.3)
**Move:** Fill all four cells — advantages and disadvantages of changing, advantages and disadvantages of **not** changing — writing the perceived benefits of perfectionism out in full rather than arguing them away. If stuck, redo the boxes through the eyes of someone who cares about you, or actually ask them. Then project one year ahead twice, with perfectionism still in place and without it, across the ~12 life areas (social, work/education, finances, emotional health, partner, children, close friends, parents/siblings, community, spiritual life, feelings about self). Close by naming what most scares you about reducing perfectionism and the most compelling reasons to do it.
**APPLY WHEN:** ambivalence — "part of me doesn't want to lose it"; fear that dropping perfectionism means dropping standards or losing what made you successful; "I'll change later, now is a bad time".
**AVOID WHEN:** the "advantages of not changing" cell is skipped (hidden ambivalence collapses the plan later); the projection turns into catastrophising rumination rather than a bounded exercise — pair with a therapist if the user is acutely low; or the grid is used to debate whether perfectionism is a problem at all before the costs material has been read.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Standard CBT motivational component of the RCT-tested package; decisional balance has method-level support.
### [PERF:BROADBASE] Widen the base of self-evaluation (Worksheets 6.4, 9.3, 9.4)
**Move:** For each of the ~13 life areas (adding pastimes/hobbies and physical/nutritional health), write who you want to **be** and what you want to **do** — identity and action, not targets. Redo it later as Worksheet 9.3 and compare with the 6.4 version. Then pick four life areas *different* from your current self-evaluation domains and set six-month goals in each: changes wanted, the most important reasons, the planned steps, the observable sign it is working, the likely obstacles and countermeasures. Small steps, no musts, optionally sanity-checked with someone else. Principle: eggs in many baskets, so a setback in one area is buffered by the others.
**APPLY WHEN:** self-worth hangs on one or two domains; fear of being worthless without high standards; after [PERF:HOTSPOT-DIARY] shows a narrow base.
**AVOID WHEN:** the new areas get converted into fresh achievement arenas with standards and checking attached — pastimes are for enjoyment, not mastery; or the "how I'll know it's working" line is written as an all-or-nothing criterion.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Operationalises the book's core model; programme component with direct RCT support; Kothari 2019 reports self-esteem d = +0.7 for this programme.
### [PERF:CRITIC-COMPASSION-DIARY] Two-pass critic then compassion diary (Worksheets 8.1, 8.4)
**Move:** Pass one, for a week: log the triggering event (something someone did, something you did, or an upsetting image), the self-critical thought **including what it says about you as a person**, and the feeling rated 0–100%. Mine your earlier 7.1.2 thought column for the entries you would hesitate to say aloud to another person, and note the recurring names you call yourself. Pass two, for a month: add two columns — what the compassionate voice says (what you would say to a friend, with belief rated 0–100%) and what the original feeling did afterwards — re-rating honestly even when it rose.
**APPLY WHEN:** self-criticism is so habitual it reads as plain fact; and then, once the voice is named, to build the alternative.
**AVOID WHEN:** an initial worsening is read as failure — the book's own printed example shows depression 75 → 95% and humiliation 80 → 100% with one compassionate statement believed at only 30%, and keeps it there deliberately; also when monitoring becomes another performance to do perfectly, when mood drops sharply during the week (shorten it), or when the trigger is a traumatic memory.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Direct RCT of this book; Kothari 2019 secondary outcomes — fear of self-compassion d = −0.8, self-esteem d = +0.7.
### [PERF:VALUES-MIRROR] The same values list, turned on yourself (Worksheets 8.2, 8.3)
**Move:** Circle the twelve values you actually apply to friends — acceptance, caring, compassion, courtesy, forgiveness, generosity, helpfulness, hope, fun, justice, service, respect — and add your own. Then read the identical list rewritten in the first person (accepting yourself as worthwhile regardless of achievement; forgiving yourself; acknowledging that self-criticism does not extract your best performance; still believing in yourself after a poor performance; being able to laugh at yourself; attending to what you like about yourself and not only to faults) and name every place where you grant a friend what you refuse yourself. Pair it with the Coach Smith / Coach Jones thought experiment (Box 8.2): imagine your own child coached by a man who stays silent on success and berates by name on failure, or by one who praises effort and normalises mistakes as learning — then answer which coach you would keep, which produces better performance, and which one you are being to yourself.
**APPLY WHEN:** warmth toward others is articulable but warmth toward self is not; the double standard is denied or invisible; the belief "criticism is what keeps me sharp" is live.
**AVOID WHEN:** the friendship list is filled in aspirationally rather than from real behaviour (the contrast is the whole point); the inconsistency is used as fresh grounds for shame; or the coach exercise is taken as a one-off insight without the diaries — recognition alone does not shift the habit.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct RCT support; Kothari 2019 fear of self-compassion d = −0.8, self-esteem d = +0.7; coach experiment adapted from Pollack & Otto (2009), with the self-criticism ↔ less goal progress link established at correlational level only.
### [PERF:HOTSPOT-DIARY] Seven-day self-evaluation diary and the if–then rule (Worksheet 9.1)
**Move:** For seven days record what made you feel particularly good or bad **about yourself**, which life domain it belongs to (social, work, education, finances, emotional/spiritual health, close relationships, community citizenship, valued pastimes, fitness, sport), the thought, and feeling intensity 0–100. Then write your own governing rule as an "If … then …" statement, and note which domains never registered at all. Stephen's week showed only work and "good community citizen" moving his self-worth, while fitness, family and friends produced enjoyment but no effect on it.
**APPLY WHEN:** you cannot say which areas your self-worth actually rests on.
**AVOID WHEN:** filled in from theory rather than from the week as it happened.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct RCT support; structurally borrowed from Fennell's low-self-esteem work.
### [PERF:GOAL-FLEXIBILISE] Rewrite goals so they can survive reality
**Move:** Turn must/should goals into flexible, realistic ones. Stephen's "I must always be on time for site meetings" became "I will try to be on time but cannot control every eventuality; I'll carry the client's mobile number so I can call if I'm late." Test achievability by surveying admired people who **also have balanced lives**: what goals do you set each day, what happens when you miss them, how do you cope?
**APPLY WHEN:** daily goal lists are routinely unfinished; every day ends in frustration at what was not done.
**AVOID WHEN:** survey targets are chosen for achievement alone without the balance criterion — you will simply re-anchor high.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Survey and goal-setting components of a programme with direct RCT support; Stephen's survey of two architect colleagues is case material.
### [PERF:RELAPSE-BLUEPRINT] Toolbox, tough-going sheet and lapse protocol (Ch. 10, Worksheet 9.5)
**Move:** Re-read the book and your completed exercises and decide chapter by chapter which tools go in your toolbox, guided by two questions — what do you want to keep developing, what still needs attention — writing one line per retained strategy. Then complete the six-part sheet: what kept my perfectionism going; every strategy learned; my own top tips; the situations that will be hardest; how I will handle them; and one tweet-length reminder of the whole programme. For an actual setback run six steps: pre-identify likely triggers; catch the lapse early without fatalism or panic; recruit extra support (social or professional); reinstate the action plan; once settled, problem-solve the trigger with [PERF:PROBSOLVE]; and note what you did well despite the difficulty.
**APPLY WHEN:** finishing the programme; a wobble starting; the critical voice getting louder again; reverting to old coping under stress.
**AVOID WHEN:** done as a completeness exercise where every tool is kept (the selection is the point); written only after a lapse rather than in advance; postponed until it can be written perfectly; or the lapse is read as proof the work failed — that reading is itself the all-or-nothing target.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Relapse-prevention components of a programme with direct RCT support; blueprints are method-level standard in CBT.
### [PERF:COMORBID-TRIAGE] Route the comorbidities before starting (Boxes 2.1, 2.2, 2.4, 2.5)
**Move:** Work the symptom checklists and route accordingly: ≥5 depression symptoms daily for more than 2 weeks → depression resource or professional; any thoughts that life is not worth living → immediate professional help; BMI under 17.5 or regular vomiting → clinician; OCD rituals over 1 hour/day with impairment → OCD-specific treatment alongside; OCPD with perfectionism → just treat the perfectionism, since changing it changes the OCPD. Sequencing principle (Fairburn's house of cards): where perfectionism is the supporting card, treating it withers the dependent problems.
**APPLY WHEN:** before starting the programme, and again whenever mood or eating symptoms escalate mid-programme.
**AVOID WHEN:** never skip it — this is the book's own gate; but do not use the checklists to self-diagnose beyond routing.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Author clinical guidance anchored to DSM-5 criteria; the treat-perfectionism-first logic is supported by the book's RCTs showing transfer to anxiety, depression and eating symptoms.
### [PERF:FACTCHECK] Check the facts under the rule, then measure your own effort curve (Section 7.2)
**Move:** Identify the factual claim a rule rests on ("germs on cash machines cause HIV", "80:20 means always add another 20% effort") and research it via reputable sources — universities, respected national bodies, not scaremongering or opinion sites — then bring the corrected fact back against the rule. Because there is **no universal formula** for the right amount of effort, build personal ones empirically: log effort invested, outcome obtained and importance of the task, and derive task-specific "enough" levels by trial and error. Anchor facts: the effort–performance relation is an inverted U (Yerkes–Dodson, 1908) and most perfectionists sit past the peak, where effort can be cut with zero performance loss and only stress loss; high perfectionism predicts *lower* efficiency; a harsh reaction to mistakes damages performance more than insufficient training.
**APPLY WHEN:** a rule rests on a checkable empirical claim; risk overestimation; chronic over-preparation; the "point Y" pattern of far more effort than the result requires.
**AVOID WHEN:** expecting facts alone to change behaviour — Bernie's correction did not work overnight; facts only open the door to surveys and experiments. Also when the effort log itself becomes a new optimisation project.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical practice; the cited mechanisms are method-level research (Yerkes–Dodson; Stoeber-lineage efficiency studies; change-blindness work from Simons's lab).
### [PERF:OBSERVE-NOT-REACT] Watch the critical thought instead of arguing with it
**Move:** Eyes closed if comfortable, watch thoughts arrive as if projected on a cinema screen and label them — "a self-critical thought has just come in", "this is my self-criticism tape again". Acknowledge body sensations without pushing them away, and let the thought pass like a radio track you decline to turn up. The stated goal is a volume knob turned down, never off.
**APPLY WHEN:** arguing with the critical voice keeps feeding it; the loops are repetitive and recognisable.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to avoid the behavioural work; or if closing the eyes brings intrusive traumatic imagery — stop and take it to a clinician.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level trials of acceptance/mindfulness cited by the authors — fewer depressive episodes in recurrent depression, reduced body dissatisfaction; not isolated within this programme.
### [PERF:REALITY-INTEGRATE] Write down what your personal reality actually is (Section 7.9)
**Move:** Assemble the outputs of the separate exercises — survey results, experiment outcomes, attention-broadening notes, alternative thoughts — into one written statement of what is true in your own situation on this issue: which part of the negative belief is distortion, and which part is a genuine cost that has to be accepted. The authors explicitly refuse the "positive thinking" frame; sometimes reality is that a goal is not worth its cost, and the work is accepting that while blocking the all-or-nothing generalisation ("so the years were wasted").
**APPLY WHEN:** several exercises have been run on the same standard and the conclusions are scattered or contradictory.
**AVOID WHEN:** run as a substitute for the exercises — the integration has no content without prior data.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Direct RCT support for the programme as a whole (Egan 2014; Handley 2015; Rozental 2017; Shafran 2017); the integration step itself is author clinical practice.
### [PERF:RISK-BOTH-WAYS] Map the risk of both options when the standard is moral (Mohammed's case)
**Move:** When high standards are defended on moral or protective grounds, stop arguing about other people's norms and instead lay out the risks of loosening the standard **and** the risks of keeping it, side by side. Test the alternative with behavioural experiments and track outcomes other than the one you habitually monitor — for Mohammed, that his children do better with a non-anxious, available father, and that *every* parenting strategy carries risk, including the hyper-vigilant one.
**APPLY WHEN:** the standard is framed as duty or morality; surveys land flat because "I don't care what others do".
**AVOID WHEN:** expecting anxiety to drop as the proof it worked — relaxing control made Mohammed *more* anxious short-term, and the gain was decision-making with less stress once risk was accepted as unavoidable; also not a substitute for OCD-specific treatment where compulsions are present.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Author clinical case material; the behavioural-experiment method it rests on has direct RCT support within this programme.
### [PERF:PORTABLE-COUNTERS] Flashcards, cue cards, just-do-it reminders and coping images (Worksheets 7.8.6–7.8.8)
**Move:** Distil your own disputations and experiment results into portable form: a flashcard of helpful statements drawn from your data ("a small start broke the block before; my writing is acceptable"); the compassionate statements from Worksheet 8.4 that most shifted mood, one per index card, carried and posted where you will see them and said aloud when alone; a short visible reminder list of why not to delay; and a coping **image** built from an actual past success to replace the dread image (James swapped "stuck at the desk late into the night, checking endlessly" for one hour of work with limited checking, then stopping and meeting friends, including the felt relief).
**APPLY WHEN:** the decision point is brief and reasoning fails in the moment; the critical voice is loud enough that nothing balanced can be recalled; pre-task dread arrives as imagery.
**AVOID WHEN:** written before any experiments — the statements must come from your own evidence, not generic affirmations, and the coping image needs lived counter-evidence to be built from; used as reassurance-seeking or thought suppression; used as the sole intervention; or used to force push-through on genuinely misprioritised tasks or on the back of exhaustion.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme components; authors' clinical experience and anecdote — the weakest evidence tier in the book's toolkit.
### [PERF:IMG-RESCRIPT] Rescript a recurring distressing memory-image (Section 7.7)
**Move:** Re-enter the scene as your competent adult self, intervene, give the child self new information and compassion, and comfort that image. Mary's example: a childhood memory of classmates mocking her stumbling while reading aloud, still triggering her adult public speaking. The forward-looking variant is safer and can be run solo: take an anticipated catastrophe that arrives as a picture (the audience laughing AT you) and rebuild it into a realistic-coping or positive version (stumbling, landing a joke, the audience laughing WITH you).
**APPLY WHEN:** a specific past humiliation replays and blocks present performance situations; the book notes perfectionists have more frequent, sticky, distressing perfectionism-related images, and that imagery explained the perfectionism–distress link better than repetitive negative thinking in an Australian study.
**AVOID WHEN:** solo, on memories with real traumatic charge — the book presents this in a few paragraphs without safeguards and prints no blank worksheet for it, which is itself a signal; route memory work to therapist-guided rescripting.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Imagery rescripting has method-level trials in other disorders; its perfectionism application here rests on the authors' clinical experience only.
### [PERF:HISTORICAL-REVIEW] Historical test of the new perspective (Worksheet 9.2)
**Move:** Take the perspective "when I don't achieve the standards I set myself, that doesn't make me less worthwhile as a person" and scan each age band — birth–2, 3–5, 6–11, 12–15, 16–18, 19–25 — for experiences consistent with it, writing them down; optionally reinforce with photos and a scrapbook. Consolidate it by writing yourself a letter in the voice of a compassionate other — an imagined figure, a public figure you regard that way (Mother Teresa, the Dalai Lama), or someone compassionate you actually know — commenting on what the review showed makes you worthwhile apart from achievement.
**APPLY WHEN:** the new perspective is understood intellectually but not believed; perfectionism feels lifelong and the person cannot recall being valued for anything but output.
**AVOID WHEN:** the age bands hold abuse, loss or other trauma — the review will open that material and the form carries no containment structure at all; also when the letter turns into a performance to write well, or pulls up grief about people who were not compassionate to you. This is the single back-matter item most likely to surface painful autobiography solo.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Standard CBT historical-test method in the Fennell lineage plus compassion-focused work, embedded in a programme with direct RCT support; the trauma-opening potential, not weak evidence, drives the tier.
### [PERF:COST-BENEFIT-DREAM] Cost-benefit review of a long-pursued goal (Sally's case)
**Move:** For a goal that is not materialising, run an explicit cost vs benefit review, then separate two questions in writing: (a) did this achieve the stated goal? (b) was it therefore entirely worthless? Use a survey to check how common "cut your losses" experiences are — heavy investment in something that never materialises (a music career, a novel, a relationship) turns out to be ordinary — and deliberately search for side-benefits the goal-focus hid; Sally's writing courses had quietly paid off in her day job.
**APPLY WHEN:** sunk-cost paralysis; unable to start anything new until the old project succeeds; low mood tied to a stalled ambition.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to talk yourself out of a goal that is merely hard rather than genuinely costly; and when letting go opens active grief or identity collapse — that belongs with a clinician, not a worksheet.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Author clinical case material sitting on the method-level cost-benefit and survey techniques of the programme.
### [PERF:ESCALATE] Add guidance, then add a clinician (Ch. 4 and Ch. 10)
**Move:** Two escalation steps the book prescribes for itself. (1) If working solo and gains stall — especially when perfectionism moves but anxiety and mood do not — add guidance: a CBT therapist working through this book, or guided self-help with brief weekly feedback, the format that performed well in trials. Directories: babcp.com (UK), eabct.org (EU), abct.org (US), aacbt.org (AU); UK route is GP or IAPT self-referral. (2) If perfectionism still substantially interferes with quality of life and you are stuck after genuine practice, treat that as the signal to seek a skilled clinician rather than to try harder alone.
**APPLY WHEN:** unguided work reduces perfectionism but anxiety/depression persist (the exact pattern of the 2014 RCT); exercises repeatedly stall; comorbid depression, OCD or eating problems; distress worsening.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to abandon the work at the first plateau — and note that step (1) only adds support, it removes nothing.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** The authors' own stated limit on self-help, plus their direct RCT contrast: guided formats improved anxiety, depression and self-criticism where pure unguided self-help moved only perfectionism. Galloway's meta-analysis shows self-help ≈ face-to-face **on average**, not for everyone.

## What the book debunks
- **"Perfectionism just means having high standards."** The clinical target is worth-contingency plus persistence despite cost. High standards with intact self-worth and normal recovery from setbacks is not the target and is not treated.
- **"It's genetic, so it can't change."** Twin studies put heritability at 24–49%; environment dominates and the model is built entirely on present-tense maintainers.
- **"You must understand your childhood causes to recover."** The authors state plainly that knowing origins does not drive change; the one aetiological study cited (Maloney 2014) is retrospective and memory-biased.
- **"Self-criticism is what keeps me sharp."** Directly contradicted — criticism damages self-esteem, lowers motivation, drives self-punishment and giving up, and is associated with less progress toward valued goals. Shame or depression after a self-critical thought signals it is unproductive, not that determination is about to follow.
- **"Silence the critical voice."** A no-criticism goal manufactures new failures. Volume down, not off.
- **"Harder work means better results."** Ten fact-vs-fiction corrections: successful people don't necessarily work harder; success doesn't require single-mindedness; there is no cleverness-vs-hard-work law; feeling a fraud is common and doesn't mean being one; "more in = more out" is not universal; people notice far less than you think (change blindness); happiness doesn't require success; avoidance rarely resolves anything; "a job worth doing is worth doing well" has exceptions.
- **"Checking prevents mistakes."** Repeated checking *decreases* confidence in memory — the more you check, the more uncertain you feel; Jeff's data ran opposite to his prediction. Relatedly, "not feeling right" is a fluctuating internal state, not information about quality: Callie's early-submitted report felt no less right than the last-minute one.
- **"Motivation comes first, then action."** The authors state it runs the other way — action generates motivation, so waiting to feel ready guarantees delay. And procrastination's relief is brief, with anxiety returning amplified; leaving things late as a self-protective excuse backfires via rushed errors.
- **"Meeting the standard will settle it."** Success gets discounted and the bar re-set; the formulation worksheet exists to make the reader watch that happen.
- **Perfectionists don't all have immaculate homes.** Many live in mess precisely because the standard made the task impossible, so it was abandoned.
- **Misuse of the workbook itself.** The documented failure mode is applying perfectionism to the exercises — hours of "homework", hunting for the correct diagram, or avoiding the sheets because they can't be done perfectly. Also: quitting before starting out of fear of becoming second-rate (Gloria kept that fear even while failing at university *because* of perfectionism).
- **Anxiety reduction is the wrong success criterion.** Flexibility and workable choice are; increased uncertainty is an expected price. Mohammed got *more* anxious short-term.
- **Compassion work is not instantly soothing.** The book prints an example where mood worsened and belief in the kind statement sat at 30%, and keeps it in the blank-worksheet section on purpose.
- **Self-help is not sufficient for everyone**, monotonic improvement is an unrealistic expectation, and needing help is not a failure — both "I must improve every week" and "I must do this without help" are named as all-or-nothing thinking plus the critical voice.
- **OCPD is not OCD**, despite the acronym; and "Not Just Right Experiences" OCD resembles perfectionism so closely that qualified therapists struggle to separate them — research has not settled which treatment is better.

## Numbers that matter
- Evidence base: ≥4 direct RCTs of this book (Egan 2014; Handley 2015; Rozental 2017; Shafran 2017) plus Steele & Wade 2008 pilot; Lloyd et al. 2015 meta-analysis of 8 studies; every trial format ran **8 sessions**; 6-month maintenance in both Australian RCTs. Galloway meta-analysis: 15 RCTs, N = 912, self-help ≈ face-to-face.
- Kothari 2019 secondary outcomes for this programme: intolerance of uncertainty d = −0.9; self-esteem d = +0.7; fear of self-compassion d = −0.8.
- Prevention variant in classrooms, children 9+: reduced perfectionism vs control with benefits at 1 year (Nehmy & Wade 2015).
- Heritability of perfectionism: 24–49% (twin studies).
- Structure: 3 core components; 9 screening questions (W1.1); 8 formulation prompts (W5.1); 19 perfectionism areas (W7.1.1); 4 behaviour classes with 3 subtypes of performance checking; 7-step behavioural-experiment template; 3 continuum examples requested; 12–13 life areas; 4 decisional-balance cells; two 0–10 rulers; 6 problem-solving steps; 11-step chunk ladder (0–100 by tens); weekly grid 15 hourly slots × 7 days; ~65 pleasant activities; 12 friendship values mirrored one-for-one; 6 age bands birth–25; 4 new life areas × 5 planning fields; 6-month goal horizon; 6 relapse-prevention questions.
- Doses: 1 chapter/week suggested pace; 1 week baseline self-monitoring; 2–3 thought diaries in the first week; Worksheet 8.1 for 1 week, Worksheet 8.4 for 1 month; 7-day self-evaluation diary; several weeks (not one) for the positive diary.
- Ratings: belief and feeling 0–100% throughout; Jeff's contrast design uses 0–10.
- Referral thresholds: ≥5 depression symptoms daily for >2 weeks; BMI <17.5 or regular vomiting; OCD rituals >1 hour/day with impairment; any "life not worth living" thought → immediate help.
- Worked outcomes: Suzie 4h → 1h nightly case notes, zero complaints. Simon predicted 90% embarrassment / 100% lost enjoyment hosting with one clean room; actual 40% / 80% enjoyment. Aimee: anxiety 95% → 60%, sadness 50% → 30% after one thought diary. Gemma: anxiety 90% → 45%, sadness 40% → 20%. Jeff: repeated-check days worry 8–9 and certainty 1–2 vs single-check days worry 4 and certainty 8–9; invoice checking added ~30 min of customer time per job; one incident's rumination ran 3 months. Callie: 5 years since the agent's invitation, 3 years avoiding the manuscript, 6+ micro-experiments before writing an ending — book still rejected, resilience the gain. Hannah's survey, n = 8: 6/8 ironed underwear, 2/8 judged extreme tidiness negatively, 2/8 read it as organised. Compassion example: "I am useless" 90%, depression 75 → 95%; "I am a fraud" 85%, humiliation 80 → 100%; compassionate statements believed at 100% and 30%.
- Anchors: Yerkes–Dodson 1908 inverted U (p. 135); Pareto 80:20 as the misapplied principle; Fatima's ~30 "must" rules in ~2 minutes; Catherine of Siena 1347–80, dead at 33; Cohen's "Hallelujah" at UK Christmas chart nos. 1 and 2 in 2008, 24 years after release.
- Coverage map from the index: overcoming strategies pp. 109–350; procrastination 245–290 (thickest block); behavioural experiments 161–178; surveys 144–160; thinking styles 225–244; self-criticism 297–317; self-evaluation 318–341.

## Blind spots of this book
- **Interpersonal uncertainty has no protocol.** There is no worksheet for a pause in contact, an unanswered message, or ambiguity inside a relationship. The closest instruments are the reassurance-seeking and performance-checking entries under counterproductive behaviour and the alternating-days checking experiment ([PERF:CONTRAST]) — a real but oblique fit. The book's own referral for this is outward: Robichaud & Dugas for intolerance of uncertainty, Butler for social anxiety. Kothari's IU d = −0.9 shows the programme *moves* uncertainty tolerance without ever addressing it directly, which is a reason to use it, not evidence that it teaches the skill.
- **Component effects are never isolated.** Every move here carries package-level evidence. Nothing in the book tells you which of the ~40 worksheets did the work, so a lens that drops half the protocol cannot claim the trial results.
- **The trial base is largely the authors' own group.** Egan, Wade, Shafran, Rees and Kane recur across the RCTs — relevant when weighing effect sizes against independent replication.
- **Unguided use has a documented ceiling.** In the 2014 RCT pure self-help reduced perfectionism but *not* anxiety or depression. Solo use should expect the perfectionism gain and not assume the mood gain.
- **Trauma and memory work are entered without safeguards.** Imagery rescripting gets a few paragraphs and no blank worksheet; the historical review prints six childhood age bands with no containment structure. Both open material the format cannot hold.
- **Grief and identity loss are outside the model.** Sally's case brushes against giving up a life goal, but the book has no protocol for the mourning that follows.
- **Procrastination without perfectionism is explicitly out of scope** — the authors defer to general CBT-for-procrastination methods (rewards, distraction-free workspace, assertiveness about workload, prioritising) in further reading rather than covering them.
- **Comorbidity is triaged, not treated.** Where OCD, an eating disorder or depression is primary, the book routes outward and expects a parallel treatment; it will not carry that load.
- **No dosing or stopping rules.** It says practise for weeks and expect unevenness, but gives no criterion for when a technique has been given a fair trial and should be dropped, and no measure of change beyond re-reading your own worksheets.
- **Performance improvement is deliberately not promised.** The book's honest position — flexibility without deterioration, sometimes improvement — means it offers nothing to a user whose actual question is how to produce more.
