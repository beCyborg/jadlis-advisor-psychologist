# Cognitive Behavior Therapy: Basics and Beyond — digest

## What this book is for the lens
The defining manual of Beck-model CBT: construal — not the situation — drives emotion, behavior, and physiology, and durable change requires working both surface automatic thoughts and the beliefs underneath. The 3rd edition adds a recovery orientation (CT-R): conceptualize aspirations, values, and strengths, not only deficits, and let weekly steps toward what matters generate positive self-conclusions. It beats other sources on conceptualization-driven technique selection (technique piles are explicitly not therapy), session/work-block structure, the complete worksheet ecosystem, and honest troubleshooting of stuck change work. Its evidence is method-level (2,000+ CBT outcome studies), never book-as-bibliotherapy; every move below presumes the book's therapist–client frame, adapted here for a psychoeducation lens beside a live therapist.

## Core model
Cognition runs at three levels: automatic thoughts (fast appraisal stream, verbal or imagery, often milliseconds), intermediate beliefs (rules/attitudes/assumptions — convert rules to "if–then" form before evaluating), and core beliefs about self/others/world. Negative self-beliefs cluster into three categories: **helpless, unlovable, worthless** — same words can sit in different categories, so meaning must be probed, not assumed. Schemas hold beliefs; activated clusters = modes: every session tries to deactivate the depressive mode and activate the adaptive one (Ch. 17).
Onset is diathesis-stress (latent belief × matching stressor); maintenance is behavioral as much as cognitive: avoidance blocks the mastery/pleasure/connection experiences that would disconfirm the belief, rumination and withdrawal deepen it, and biased processing converts contrary data via discounting ("yes, but…") — automatically, as a symptom, not a choice.
The working map is the Cognitive Conceptualization Diagram (Ch. 3), in problem-based and Strengths-Based versions, built bottom-up from three typical situations, hypotheses question-marked until confirmed. Treatment runs two tracks (Ch. 18): strengthen realistic positive beliefs and weaken negative ones. Arc (Ch. 9): evaluation session (Ch. 5) → behavioral activation + automatic-thought skills → belief work with experiential techniques → relapse prevention and self-therapy (Ch. 21), tapering to boosters. Change principle: intellectual change first; emotional change needs affect present and experiences, not just words — behavioral experiments outrank verbal evaluation. Container: structured sessions (Ch. 10), Action Plans (Ch. 8), guided discovery — never persuasion. Worksheet anchors: Testing Your Thoughts Worksheet, Thought Record, Preparing for Therapy Worksheet, Session Notes, Cognitive Therapy Rating Scale; beckinstitute.org/CBTresources.

## Moves

### [CBT:ACT-SCHED] Activity scheduling with self-credit
**Move:** Schedule specific value-linked activities for the week — concrete where/what, range targets (leave the house 4×; a 5-minute walk counts); write the rationale plus a pre-scripted reply to the predicted obstacle thought ("too tired" → "go anyway"); optionally log on the hour-by-hour Activity Chart with mastery/pleasure 0–10 rated immediately after (depressed recall darkens days); record predicted vs. actual enjoyment and harvest conclusions from surprises; keep a personal "makes me feel better / worse" two-column chart to pick activities; give explicit self-credit for every completed item.
**APPLY WHEN:** withdrawal, couch/scrolling loops, dropped self-care; skepticism that behavior moves mood — predicted-vs-actual data answers it.
**AVOID WHEN:** promising an immediate mood lift (payoff may lag — jack-in-the-box analogy); packing an hourly schedule onto someone severely depressed (interleave rest); forcing ratings on rating-averse people.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** behavioral-activation component trials (Cuijpers et al., 2007); experiential disconfirmation beats discussion (Bennett-Levy et al., 2004); Ch. 7.

### [CBT:ACTION-PLAN] Action Plan design, likelihood check, and review
**Move:** End every session/work-block with a written Action Plan built by the eight design rules: tailor to capacity; state the rationale; agree collaboratively; err easy (10-minute chunks); explicit when/where/how-long; a reminder system anchored to routine; start the item in-session (continuing beats initiating — the worst minutes are just before starting); imagine completing it with credit. Before closing, ask "how likely are you to do this, 0–100%?" — under ~90%, interrogate the gap, covert-rehearse the moment in imagery to catch interfering thoughts, or make the item easier/optional. First 1–2 weeks frame as no-lose: done, or data on what blocked it. Review the plan at the next cycle's start and mine completed items for meaning.
**APPLY WHEN:** every week of change work — plan completion predicts significantly better outcomes; "no time/energy" excuses (probe with "if the obstacle vanished, would you do it?").
**AVOID WHEN:** assigning unilaterally or one-size-fits-all; keeping the no-lose frame past ~2 weeks of non-completion; skipping review — it teaches that plans don't matter; if an item was too hard, the designer owns that explicitly or the doer self-blames.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** homework-completion meta-analyses (Kazantzis et al., 2016; Callan et al., 2019); design rules are author's guidelines; Ch. 8.

### [CBT:MONITOR] Measured progress monitoring with expectation framing
**Move:** Weekly symptom scales (PHQ-9, GAD-7, or 0–10 self-ratings covering the whole week) plus well-being, functioning, and goal progress; compare to last week and probe narrative–score mismatches; attribute improvement to changed thinking and behavior, not only externals; agree up front on an expected course as a range (e.g., 15–20 sessions, weekly tapering toward monthly) and revise jointly. <!-- privacy-ok: GAD-7 — публичная шкала из книги-источника, не данные пользователя -->
**APPLY WHEN:** any ongoing change effort; every session start.
**AVOID WHEN:** rating only today instead of the week; elevated suicidality/hopelessness items — clinician risk assessment (Wenzel et al., 2009), never solo material; promising a fixed timeline.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** routine outcome monitoring with feedback improves outcomes (Lambert 2001; Boswell 2015; Miller et al., 2015); expectation-setting lowers dropout (Swift et al., 2012); Chs. 5–6, 10.

### [CBT:SOCRATIC-EVAL] Socratic evaluation of a selected automatic thought
**Move:** Filter candidates first (belief and emotion intensity, recurrence, goal obstruction — not every voiced thought merits work), then run the standard question set (Figs. 14.1/14.2): evidence for — asked FIRST — then against; alternative explanation; worst case plus coping, best case, most realistic outcome; effect of keeping vs. changing the thought; what you'd tell a specific friend; what to do now. Re-rate belief and emotion afterward; agreement without relief means vary the line (utility instead of validity) or suspect an unarticulated underlying belief; partial shift is the success criterion.
**APPLY WHEN:** an important, distressing, recurring, testable thought blocks action.
**AVOID WHEN:** never argue or "challenge" — the distortion degree is unknowable in advance, thoughts hold a grain of truth, and challenge invalidates; rumination/obsession streams (per-thought evaluation feeds the process — use mindfulness); demanding every question for every thought; when the worst fear is death, skip the coping question.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Socratic questioning linked to symptom change (Braun et al., 2015), preferred by clients (Heiniger et al., 2018); method canon; Ch. 14.

### [CBT:BEH-EXP] Behavioral experiment
**Move:** Turn a predictive thought — automatic-thought or belief-level — into a concrete, bounded test ("volunteer for 30 minutes, leave if energy runs out"; ask the neighbor for help and watch whether criticism occurs); check the prediction's evidence base first, pre-plan coping for the bad branch, run it, then explicitly harvest conclusions: what did you learn, what does it mean about you and the future; extend to other avoided situations.
**APPLY WHEN:** testable non-traumatic predictions; verbal evaluation produced intellectual-only agreement; avoidance maintains the belief.
**AVOID WHEN:** predictions about trauma disclosure or catastrophic bodily fears — therapist territory; experiments rigged so any outcome "confirms" the belief; skipping conclusion-drawing (wastes the experiment).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** experiential disconfirmation stronger than verbal methods (Bennett-Levy et al., 2004); necessary for some populations; Chs. 14, 18.

### [CBT:MINDFUL-PROCESS] Mindfulness for ruminative thought processes
**Move:** For thought *processes* — rumination, worry, obsessing, self-criticism — where per-thought evaluation fails because thoughts regenerate: psychoeducate; list advantages/disadvantages of ruminating and test the claimed advantages; deliberately start the rumination live, run ~5 minutes of mindfulness of breath (notice wandering without self-criticism, gently return; let background thoughts fade on their own), re-rate emotion 0–10, and draw the controllability conclusion. Then ~5 minutes formal practice each morning plus informal disengagement during the day (full attention to the current task). The exercise doubles as an experiment against "rumination is uncontrollable."
**APPLY WHEN:** repetitive negative thinking; fear of internal experiences; stuck problems that can't be fixed now but keep pulling attention (name the emotion as okay, declare further rumination unhelpful, refocus on a pre-listed valued activity).
**AVOID WHEN:** practicing only while calm — it won't transfer to distressed moments (documented failure mode); using it as thought suppression (inverts the method); deliberately activating traumatic material to practice on; chronic refocusing away from a solvable problem.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** method-level mindfulness trials (Hofmann et al., 2010; Chiesa & Serretti 2011; Segal et al., 2018 for relapse prevention); integration sequence is author's practice; Chs. 16, 19.

### [CBT:SETBACK-PLAN] Relapse-prevention plan and setback framing
**Move:** Draw the expected-progress graph as soon as improvement starts: improvement with plateaus and setbacks (a setback = visiting Texas on the way to Maine, not square one). Before ending structured work, write a plan naming (1) personal early warning signs (rumination, couch time, avoiding people, poor sleep, self-criticism), (2) what to remember (setbacks are a normal part of recovery), (3) what to do (self-therapy session, schedule activities, evaluate thoughts, mindfulness for rumination, named people to contact, call the therapist if insufficient); pre-answer predictable post-termination thoughts like "CBT didn't really work / it was a fluke."
**APPLY WHEN:** first weeks of improvement (graph), approaching the end of structured work (plan); anyone with recurrent depression.
**AVOID WHEN:** the plan is generic — warning signs and contacts must be personal and named; humor of the graph landing as dismissive.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** relapse-prevention-focused sessions delay relapse and recurrence (de Jonge et al., 2019); continued skill use protects even under stressful life events (Vittengl et al., 2019); Ch. 21.

### [CBT:FEEDBACK-REPAIR] Feedback loop and rupture repair
**Move:** Continuously watch face, body, and tone; on a visible shift, ask in the moment what just went through the person's mind. End every session asking what bothered them, what was misunderstood, what to do differently — and positively reinforce negative feedback first. On a complaint (direct, or indirect self-blame like "maybe I'm not expressing myself clearly"): check first whether it is accurate — if yes, model a good apology and fix it; if no, empathize, gather data, and collaboratively test the thought; then ask whether the same idea comes up in other relationships and generalize the correction.
**APPLY WHEN:** any ongoing helping/coaching relationship; suspected unspoken negative reactions — unaddressed ones predict disengagement and dropout; you may be the first to ever ask.
**AVOID WHEN:** jumping to "their distortion" without the am-I-actually-wrong check; processing every minor signal (an occasional sigh needs nothing).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** feedback collection/delivery rated demonstrably effective, rupture repair probably effective (Norcross & Lambert, 2018 review); Ch. 4.

### [CBT:VALUES-GOALS] Values → aspirations → stepped goals
**Move:** Elicit what really matters — or used to, before the disorder (offer domains: relationships, productivity, health, community, spirituality, creativity, nature); then aspirations; then meaning questions (what achieving them would say about you). Convert to a short written goal list — specific, under own control ("learn new ways of talking to Erica," not "make my sister nicer") — reviewed weekly. When stuck: the miracle question ("if you woke up recovered, what would be different? how could someone tell?"). Decompose big goals into ordered steps (staircase drawing, attention on the current step only) and list per-step obstacles in three classes — practical problems, interfering thoughts written out verbatim-as-predicted, skill deficits — each with a counter-plan.
**APPLY WHEN:** starting any change effort; drift or avolition; a goal exists but nothing moves; overwhelm at the distance to the goal.
**AVOID WHEN:** pressing open questions when nothing feels important — offer domain prompts; goals about other people's behavior left unconverted; a list long enough to overwhelm; steps so small they become avoidance.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** goal identification across life areas benefits depression (Ritschel & Sheppard, 2018); miracle question from solution-focused therapy (de Shazer, 1988); CT-R aspiration emphasis is early-stage evidence (Grant et al., 2012, 2017); Chs. 6, 9, 19.

### [CBT:AT-CATCH] Catching and formulating automatic thoughts
**Move:** At any shift in mood, body state, or behavior, ask the cardinal question: "what was just going through my mind?" — write the thought down and append "it may not be true, or not completely true"; wear a physical reminder (rubber band, sticky notes). Convert to evaluable form: the literal words, not interpretations; telegraphic exclamations spelled out; question-form thoughts restated as the feared outcome. Distinguish thoughts from emotions ("I'm a failure" is a thought; emotions are usually one word — verify the label by checking the thought content matches) and rate emotion 0–10. When the thought won't surface: describe the scene concretely in present tense, visualize it, role-play the exchange, ask whether an image appeared, or try the opposite thought.
**APPLY WHEN:** universally, from day one — the entry skill for everything else; thoughts arise before, during, and after situations, and any component (emotion, behavior, sensation) can trigger the next one.
**AVOID WHEN:** expecting instant accuracy — a skill trained over weeks; stacking retrieval techniques into an interrogation; heightening affect on traumatic memories; supplying your own guess at someone's thought (invites false agreement).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** method canon (Beck, 1964 lineage); core skill of the 2,000+-study CBT base at method level; Chs. 1, 12–13.

### [CBT:THERAPY-NOTES] Therapy notes and coping cards
**Move:** After any insight worth keeping, compose the response in the person's own words in "When I think X, I'll remind myself Y" form — only statements they actually endorse, reworded until they do ("Judy says it's not my fault," not "it's not my fault"); record on card, notebook, phone, or audio; read every morning plus before predictable hard situations, anchored to an existing habit; pre-write coping notes for the bad branch of uncertain steps (credit for trying; her criticism reflects her pattern, not my worth; concrete adjustment for next time).
**APPLY WHEN:** consolidating anything — unrecorded content gets forgotten, and forgetting session content predicts poorer outcomes; predictable anxiety before a known challenge.
**AVOID WHEN:** recording anything not endorsed — it kills the tool; wordy or superficial notes; reading only in crises — advance rehearsal is what integrates responses; recording whole sessions instead of key points.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** author's core practice; forgetting–outcome link (Lee et al., 2020); method canon; Chs. 1, 6, 15.

### [CBT:WORKSHEET] Testing Your Thoughts Worksheet / Thought Record
**Move:** For new distressing thoughts between sessions, answer the structured columns in writing: situation; thought; (optional) distortion; evidence for and against; alternative view; worst/best/most likely outcome; effect of keeping vs. changing the thought; advice to a specific friend; what to do now. The Thought Record (6 columns, 1979 Daily Record lineage) is the denser variant, taught in two stages; downgrade to a two-column shortcut (thought | response) once responses come out robust. Pre-write a reply to the predicted "I'm stuck, I'm so stupid" moment and frame first solo runs as no-lose.
**APPLY WHEN:** a new automatic thought between sessions where structure organizes thinking better than mental Q&A — but only after one successful guided run with the same questions.
**AVOID WHEN:** low functioning, writing aversion, or low literacy — use audio; assigning before a successful run-through (a failed first attempt discredits the tool); treating a non-helping worksheet as failure instead of data on which stuck point applies.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Beck Institute worksheets (2018 CBT Worksheet Packet); no worksheet-specific RCT — evidence is method-level trials of protocols containing them; Ch. 15.

### [CBT:DISTORT] Naming the twelve distortions
**Move:** Label thoughts against the 12-error list (Fig. 14.3): all-or-nothing, catastrophizing/fortune-telling, discounting the positive, emotional reasoning, labeling, magnification/minimization, mental filter, mind reading, overgeneralization, personalization, should/must statements, tunnel vision. First identify your own 3–4 habitual errors, then watch for just those in the week's thoughts.
**APPLY WHEN:** recurring thinking errors; needing distance from thoughts.
**AVOID WHEN:** the full list overwhelms — it is optional, not essential; labels overlap and one thought can carry several; using the list without understanding the categories.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** method canon (Beck, 1976; popularized by Burns, 1980); Ch. 14.

### [CBT:TRUE-THOUGHT] Routing for accurate thoughts
**Move:** When evidence supports the thought, stop testing accuracy and switch tracks: (a) structured problem solving — specify the problem, brainstorm (own past solutions, advice-to-a-friend flip), pick a small testable step; when distress is diffuse, hypothetically eliminate candidate problems one at a time and rate relief to find the real driver; (b) examine the conclusion drawn from the true fact ("I can't concentrate" is true; "so my brain is broken forever" is the testable part); (c) for genuinely unsolvable problems, acceptance plus redirect to valued action — "I don't like it, I can't change it, stop struggling."
**APPLY WHEN:** the thought is accurate; real-life obstacles block valued action; unchangeable situations feeding misery through unrealistic hope.
**AVOID WHEN:** premature acceptance of solvable problems — acceptance is not resignation; solving logistics when cognitions are the actual blocker (check first — practical excuses often mask thoughts, but real constraints deserve validation first); using problem solving to dodge a distorted conclusion riding on the true fact.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** problem-solving therapy literature (D'Zurilla & Nezu, 2006); acceptance strand via ACT lineage (Hayes et al.); method canon; Chs. 9, 14, 19.

### [CBT:POSITIVE-DATA] Positive data collection and self-credit
**Move:** Open every review with "when were you at your best this week?" — re-experience the moment conversationally, then extract meaning: what does doing this while depressed say about you? Keep a daily credit list of everything done that was even slightly hard but done anyway; later convert it into an Evidence of [Adaptive Belief] Chart (event → what it says about me), counting invisible baseline evidence (daily self-care never counted as competence, though its absence would have counted against). Name each positive emotion, re-imagine the situation as if happening now, and recall the best-period self ("how did you see yourself then? maybe you're a competent person who is currently depressed").
**APPLY WHEN:** every session and week — depression selectively attends to negatives and discounts positives; helplessness/worthlessness beliefs discounting daily accomplishments; building the adaptive mode from day one.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to dismiss real problems ("just look at the positives"); the chart becoming a performance audit that feeds self-criticism; replacement beliefs inflated into absolutes ("I'm great" instead of "I'm normal, with strengths and weaknesses"); hypomanic states — realism may require correcting downward.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** positive-affect broaden-and-build research (Fredrickson, 2001; Tugade et al., 2004); CT-R recovery orientation, early-stage (Beck, Finkel, & Beck, 2020); Chs. 2, 6, 13, 17–18.

### [CBT:REATTRIB] Reattribution: illness frame and responsibility pie
**Move:** Attribute symptoms — fatigue, indecision, negative thoughts — to the disorder, not character: "you haven't become incompetent; the depression is interfering," backed by concrete retained-competence evidence; analogies: depression vs. sadness as pneumonia vs. a cold, "black glasses," horse blinders. For excessive self-blame, draw a causal pie chart apportioning ALL alternative causes first and the self-blaming slice last. Catch unfair comparisons (with non-depressed others, the pre-illness self, the ideal self) and substitute comparison with self at the worst point, listing what is possible now that wasn't then.
**APPLY WHEN:** "I'm lazy / no good" self-blame; "it's 100% my fault"; comparison-driven mood drops.
**AVOID WHEN:** the illness frame becoming justification for inactivity; apportioning self-blame first — order matters, other causes get squeezed out; comparing at the person's lowest point, where no progress exists yet.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** author's clinical practice (Abe's fault attribution 100% → 20% via the pie chart); method canon; Chs. 6, 17, 19.

### [CBT:BELIEF-MAP] Mapping beliefs and conceptualization (self-safe portions)
**Move:** Map 3 typical recent upsetting situations (situation → automatic thought → emotion → behavior; add physiology for intense anxiety), scan for the recurring theme, and hypothesize the core-belief category — helpless, unlovable, or worthless — probing meaning rather than assuming from situation type. Elicit intermediate beliefs by probing rules and coping strategies ("what's the worst that could happen if you don't X?"; fill-in-the-blank "if I even try X, then ___") and convert rules into conditional assumptions before evaluating. Fill the Strengths-Based CCD: pre-onset strengths and resources → adaptive core and intermediate beliefs → helpful behaviors. Hold beliefs via the screen metaphor — a screen lets confirming data straight in while contradicting data bounces off — and the key concepts: beliefs are learned ideas, not truths; can feel true and be false; can be revised.
**APPLY WHEN:** recurring reactions need a working map; a behavioral pattern (avoidance, pleasing, perfectionism) needs its underlying logic named; counteracting an all-negative self-view.
**AVOID WHEN:** solo digging at negative core beliefs or their childhood origins — the book warns identification alone can trigger strong affect and felt unsafety (map behavior and assumptions solo; take core-belief work to the therapist); unrepresentative situations — the completed map will actively mislead; treating coping strategies as pathologies (overuse is the problem, not the behavior).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Beck Institute CCD worksheets; conceptualization-sharing itself can be therapeutic (Ezzamel et al., 2015); no diagram-specific trials; Chs. 3, 17.

### [CBT:CONTINUUM] Cognitive continuum
**Move:** For a polarized judgment ("I'm a failure"), draw a 0–100% line, place yourself, then populate it with progressively worse anchors (0% = never accomplishes anything and harms others) and re-place yourself; examine the cost of the all-or-nothing label; review the scale each morning and whenever the label re-fires; learn to build continua for other dichotomies.
**APPLY WHEN:** all-or-nothing thinking at thought or belief level.
**AVOID WHEN:** run with no affect present in the moment — the change stays intellectual-only.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** method canon; author's practice (Abe moved from 0% to 60% "successful," projected 90% in a job); Ch. 18.

### [CBT:ADV-DISADV] Advantage/disadvantage analysis
**Move:** For decisions: a four-cell grid (advantages/disadvantages of each option — filling one cell reminds you of items for the others), weigh by circling what matters most or rating 1–10, conclude, and check whether missing information is needed first. For ambivalence about actions or beliefs: list advantages/disadvantages of the behavior or of keeping the old belief vs. adopting the new one, surfacing the short-term-relief trap ("staying in bed felt better — for minutes; toward or away from your goals? haven't you already run that experiment for months?").
**APPLY WHEN:** depression-related indecision; ambivalence about tasks, rumination, or belief change.
**AVOID WHEN:** endlessly re-litigating a made decision; using the chart to argue someone into a conclusion instead of eliciting their own.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** method canon; author's practice (Fig. 19.1); Chs. 8, 17, 19.

### [CBT:SKILL-VS-COG] Skill deficit vs. blocking cognition test
**Move:** Before training any skill, test: "if you were sure it would go well, what would you do or say?" A good answer means the skill exists and cognitions block it → restructure the thought; a poor answer means a true deficit → teach, demonstrate, and role-play (play yourself, get feedback, watch a modeled alternative, switch roles, repeat), then an Action Plan to use the skill with a specific person.
**APPLY WHEN:** any performance obstacle — communication, assertiveness, parenting, interviewing, budgeting.
**AVOID WHEN:** never skip the test — training an existing skill wastes time and can insult; rehearsing while the interfering cognition ("she'll think I'm critical") stays unaddressed — handle both.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** author's clinical heuristic; method canon; ABCT self-help list referenced; Ch. 19.

### [CBT:IMAGERY-KIT] Working with spontaneous images
**Move:** Hunt for the image behind distress out of proportion to verbal thoughts ("did you picture how it would look?" — try synonyms: mental picture, movie in your mind, daydream); treat it as an automatic thought in another form and normalize graphic or strange content. Then, per case: reality-check the image against what is actually happening now; deliberately continue an image frozen at its worst point through to a realistic end, or time-jump to the finished-task relief; re-script it as "movie director" and bridge to behavior ("what could you do to make the better ending likelier?"); rehearse an upcoming stressor hour by hour with coping inserted at each spike; re-inhabit past-mastery memories ("you're still the person who does hard things") and a concrete recovered day about a year out.
**APPLY WHEN:** anticipatory anxiety about a concrete event; hopeless "this will go on forever" predictions; images freezing at peak threat; confidence collapse before a challenge resembling a mastered one.
**AVOID WHEN:** intrusive/repetitive images — that is a thought process, use mindfulness instead; continuation that keeps spawning new catastrophes; catastrophic loss/death imagery — the far-future grief variant is therapist territory; rehearsal degrading into a reassurance ritual; verbal-only responses to images (stated second-best).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** imagery affects emotion more strongly than verbal processing (Hackmann et al., 2011); author's practice; Ch. 20.

### [CBT:AWARE] AWARE stance for anxiety
**Move:** A—Accept the anxiety as normal energy, don't suppress or control it; W—Watch it from a detached, nonjudgmental distance, rating 0–10 as it rises and falls ("you are not your anxiety"); A—Act constructively as if not anxious — converse, walk, work, breathe slowly — without fleeing; R—Repeat the cycle; E—Expect the best: what is most feared usually doesn't happen, and confidence compounds per repetition. Teach by visualizing the five steps inside a specific upcoming situation. Rationale: fighting or avoiding anxiety intensifies and prolongs it; the brain responds identically to real and imagined danger.
**APPLY WHEN:** everyday and anticipatory anxiety, worry, anxiety-about-anxiety, intense but non-dangerous body sensations; adaptable to angry or depressive rumination.
**AVOID WHEN:** used as covert avoidance or a safety ritual; as a substitute for a structured exposure program on entrenched phobias/panic; before "these sensations are harmless" has been medically established.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Beck & Emery (1985) anxiety protocol — method-level canon, no standalone RCT; Appendix C, Ch. 16.

### [CBT:SELF-THERAPY] Becoming your own therapist
**Move:** The stated goal from session one — it also speeds treatment. Organize therapy notes into a synopsis of skills learned, framed as lifelong tools; whenever mood improves, ask why and write the self-attribution ("when I do things to help myself, I feel better" — acknowledge externals and medication but still name your own contribution). Run structured solo sessions (Fig. 21.3): review the week (positives and their meaning, credit due, unresolved problems, plan completion), look forward (desired feeling next week, goals, steps, obstacles), choose tools (worksheets, scheduling pleasure/mastery/social/self-care activity, notes, mindfulness, credit list); cadence monthly → seasonal → yearly with a reminder system; restart at early warning signs.
**APPLY WHEN:** tapering or after ending structured work; any out-of-proportion reaction or repeating unhelpful pattern.
**AVOID WHEN:** interfering thoughts ("too much work," "I can't do it alone") left unaddressed — the book is honest that many people don't follow through with formal self-sessions; notes hoarded unread — schedule review triggers.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** continued CBT skill use improves outcomes even under stressful life events (Vittengl et al., 2019); structure is author's practice; Ch. 21.

### [CBT:CORE-BELIEF-MOD] Direct negative core-belief modification
**Move:** Gate twice before touching a negative core belief — worth it? (drives significant distress, held strongly and broadly, blocks goals) and ready now? (accepts cognitions as ideas not truths, can tolerate the affect of exposing the belief, relationship trusting enough). Then the therapist-led kit: full problem-based CCD with AT-meanings and childhood origins; downward arrow ("if that's true — so what? what's the worst part?") to the core meaning; motivation work first (advantages/disadvantages of keeping the belief; two-scenario future-self imagery); historical test reframing each life period's "evidence" with written summaries read daily; point-counterpoint role play for "I know it's false but it feels true"; belief-level Socratic work inside specific situations, deliberately more leading than at thought level.
**APPLY WHEN:** (therapist-led) entrenched beliefs resist present-focused thought-level work; mid-treatment, solid alliance, automatic-thought skills already in place.
**AVOID WHEN:** early in the work — premature core-belief evaluation reads as invalidation and risks intense emotion and dropout; solo — even identification can trigger strong affect; origin review adjoining trauma without containment; expecting the belief to reach 0% — "sufficiently weakened" means behavior keeps changing despite a remnant.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** method canon (downward arrow, historical test; point-counterpoint from Young, 1999); belief change far slower in personality disorders than acute disorders; Chs. 2–3, 17–18.

### [CBT:AFFECT-HEIGHTEN] Deliberate affect intensification
**Move:** Intensify negative affect on purpose — imagery, somatic focus, schema activation in session — to access hot cognitions, change beliefs at the emotional level, and learn experientially that emotions are not dangerous, uncontrollable, or intolerable; cognitions change in the presence of affect, and low in-session distress predicts shallow, intellectual-only change.
**APPLY WHEN:** (therapist-led) intellectualized processing or emotion avoidance blocks progress; "I believe it in my head but not my gut."
**AVOID WHEN:** solo use; unassessed trauma history; no de-escalation plan in place.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** method canon; Chs. 13, 18.

### [CBT:EXPOSURE] Formal exposure program
**Move:** Build a 0–100-rated hierarchy of avoided situations; start around 30% predicted anxiety (the person's choice — occasionally someone picks the top item, which speeds treatment); expose daily where feasible, staying until the feared outcome fails to occur; identify and drop safety behaviors (allowed only as a first step); log predicted vs. actual anxiety and cross off failed predictions; monitor post-exposure cognitions ("next time I won't cope"); imaginal or VR exposure when in-vivo is too frightening or impractical; tie exposures to values ("visiting your grandmother").
**APPLY WHEN:** (therapist-led) avoidance — including subtle safety behaviors like no eye contact or no small talk — maintains fear or hopelessness.
**AVOID WHEN:** solo launch — the book itself states its outline is insufficient without further instruction; exposure to internal stimuli (memories, intense emotion, pain) is likewise therapist territory.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** method-level exposure literature (Craske et al., 2014; Salkovskis, 1996 on safety behaviors); Ch. 19.

### [CBT:MEM-RESTRUCT] Experiential restructuring of early memories
**Move:** From a current distressing trigger, heighten affect and bridge to the earliest memory with the same felt theme; restructure its meaning intellectually (Socratic reframing; "what would you have said to your own kids?") and experientially — role-playing the scene as parent then as child self, or the six-step younger-self/older-self dialogue (re-experience in present tense, invite the older self in, feed lines if needed, re-rate using child-appropriate language: a little/medium/a lot) — until the conclusion lands that the belief was untrue and the child deserved protection; convert into compassionate self-talk on the Action Plan. Gentler positive direction: elaborate positive memories to fortify adaptive beliefs (this direction is SELF-safe).
**APPLY WHEN:** (therapist-led) a core belief persists despite standard restructuring and traces to identifiable early experiences; middle/end of treatment, more personality-disorder than acute populations.
**AVOID WHEN:** solo, ever — this deliberately opens traumatic material; early in treatment or before standard belief work has begun; dissociation risk; the author herself gates it: "more advanced and may not apply to many of your clients."
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Gestalt-derived, adapted to the cognitive model; author's clinical experience — no trial evidence cited for these specific techniques; Appendix D, Ch. 18.

## What the book debunks
- **The CBT label guarantees nothing**: Aaron Beck's foreword states many self-described CBT therapists lack basic conceptual skills, and no book substitutes for supervision.
- **"Thoughts cause depression"**: explicitly rejected — causes are psychosocial, genetic, and biological; cognition shapes expression and maintenance. Depressive processing (discounting, negativity bias) is automatic and symptomatic — not laziness or willful negativity.
- **Challenging/disputing thoughts**: prohibited three ways — the distortion degree is unknowable in advance, challenge invalidates, and it breaks collaborative empiricism. Emotions are never disputed at all, and eliminating negative emotion is a non-goal (it's as functional as physical pain).
- **"Wait until you feel like it"**: motivation arrives after starting; clients have already run the stay-in-bed experiment for months without recovery. Avoidance pays in immediate relief and costs the disconfirming experience; safety behaviors teach "it went okay because I protected myself."
- **"Positive thinking"**: the emphasis is realism — hypomanic clients may need beliefs corrected in the negative direction.
- **Feeling better within a session ≠ progress**: progress is cross-session change on measures; pleasant-but-flat therapy is a named failure state with a diagnostic checklist.
- **"Resistant / manipulative / unmotivated" labels**: chiefly relieve the helper's sense of responsibility and block problem solving — replaced with "what exactly has each party said/done or not?"
- **"Rumination is uncontrollable" / "thinking hard will fix it"**: meta-beliefs that feel functional but regenerate the same thoughts; disconfirmed by live practice. Suppressing thoughts is impossible and maladaptive as a goal.
- **Tool worship**: worksheets are means, not the point; a "failed" worksheet is conceptualization data. Handing someone a pre-filled conceptualization is explicitly discouraged; guessing their thoughts invites false agreement; interpretations and labels are not automatic thoughts.
- **Linear recovery**: setbacks are part of the expected curve; the alliance alone doesn't heal — it grows through perceived improvement. Documented limit: Abe's goal of improving the relationship with his ex-wife failed — CBT does not deliver every interpersonal goal.

## Numbers that matter
- Method base: >2,000 outcome studies; first RCT Rush et al. (1977) — cognitive therapy matched imipramine, beat it on relapse prevention; treated outpatients kept improving 5–20 years post-therapy vs. medical treatment (von Brachel et al., 2019).
- Dose: uncomplicated depression/anxiety typically 6–16 sessions of 45–50 minutes; evaluation session 1–2 hours; 4–5-session trial offered to skeptics; taper weekly → biweekly → every 3–4 weeks; boosters at 3/6/12 months.
- Worked case (Abe, 56, severe MDD): 18 sessions over 8 months; PHQ-9 18 → 3, GAD-7 8 → 2, well-being 1/10 → 7–8; full remission by final booster. <!-- privacy-ok: GAD-7 — публичная шкала из книги-источника, не данные пользователя -->
- Action Plans: ~90% stated likelihood is the completion threshold — below it, shrink or rehearse; 10-minute chunks; no-lose framing expires after ~2 weeks of non-completion.
- Thought work: 12 distortions; 3 core-belief categories (helpless/unlovable/worthless); belief rated 0–100%, emotion 0–10; worksheet ~5–10 min; therapy notes 20–60 seconds to read, 2×/day; mood improvement ≥10% = "worth doing."
- Mindfulness: ~5-minute starter dose — brevity predicts adherence (formal range 5–60 min).
- Exposure: hierarchy 0–100, entry ≈30% predicted anxiety, daily when feasible.
- Sessions: 2–3 agenda items maximum; activity floor example: 4 outings/week, a 5-minute walk counts.

## Blind spots of this book
- **Depression-centric**: explicitly excludes suicidality, self-harm, substance use, youth, and older adults, and says other disorders need protocol variations it doesn't provide — the lens must not stretch its moves across diagnoses.
- **Written for therapist delivery**: every technique presumes a second person managing dosing, affect, and repair; the SELF tiering above is this digest's adaptation, and the book itself carries no bibliotherapy RCT (that base belongs to Burns' Feeling Good).
- **Evidence granularity**: the trials are of whole CBT protocols; individual worksheets and diagrams were never separately tested, and the 3rd edition's recovery-orientation (CT-R) layer rests on early-stage evidence while being presented as co-equal with the traditional track.
- **Composite pedagogy**: two idealized teaching cases (Abe, Maria) do most of the demonstrative lifting; real-world messiness appears mainly in the troubleshooting chapters.
- **Cultural loading**: acknowledges CBT encodes rationality, scientific method, and individualism (dominant-US values) but offers thin concrete adaptation guidance.
- **Context underweighted**: biology gets "consult the prescriber"; poverty, abusive environments, and systemic factors are named as interfering variables rather than treatment targets; interpersonal outcomes are the least assured (the ex-wife goal failed).
- **Adherence realism**: the method leans on literate, structured, writing-tolerant clients — and the author concedes many people don't keep up formal self-therapy sessions after termination.
