# Mind Over Mood — digest

## What this book is for the lens

Mechanism-level thesis: moods change when situation-specific appraisals are surfaced, tested against observer-grade evidence, and rewritten through lived behavioral tests — thought change and behavior change are the two most durable levers in a five-part system (environment, body, mood, behavior, thought). Where it beats other sources: the most complete worksheet apparatus in the lens — every technique has a numbered worksheet, a dose, an advance threshold, and a troubleshooting path — and it is synchronized with J. Beck's therapy manual, so homework assigned from it aligns with what a Beckian therapist does in session. Reading order routes by presenting mood (Ch. 13 depression, 14 anxiety, 15 anger/guilt/shame), which makes it retrievable by problem. Role here: structured homework under guidance — Burns is the standalone entry; this is the book a therapist-adjacent system assigns and paces. Core usage rule: daily paced practice, staying with a chapter until its skill works in real life (hours to months per chapter); speed is explicitly not the success metric.

## Core model

- **Five-part model** (Padesky 1986; Ch. 2, Worksheet 2.1): environment/life events, physical reactions, moods, behaviors, thoughts — all bidirectionally linked. Explains downward spirals; the leverage claim is that a small change in any area propagates, so hunt the smallest change with the largest overall improvement.
- **Three levels of thought**: automatic thoughts/images (surface; tested by the 7-column Thought Record, Chs. 6–9) → underlying "If … then …" assumptions (cross-situational rules; tested by behavioral experiments, Ch. 11) → core beliefs (absolute self/others/world statements; Ch. 12, explicitly optional — top-level work often suffices because links run both ways).
- **Mood signatures** (Ch. 7): depression = negative self/experience/future (Beck's triad, Ch. 13); anxiety = danger overestimated + coping underestimated, future "what if…?" chains, often imagery, maintained by avoidance and safety behaviors (Ch. 14); anger = perceived unfairness, broken (often unspoken) rules, judged intent (Ch. 15); guilt = violating one's own standards; shame = guilt + globally defective self + secrecy.
- **After testing a thought, three routes** (Ch. 10): strengthen the new thought with lived evidence; Action Plan when the thought is accurate and the problem solvable; acceptance when it can't be solved or must be endured. Routes combine.
- **Skill stages** (Ch. 16): deliberate written practice → effortful mental use → automatic. People quit at "feeling better" — the book instructs continuing to automaticity; relapse plans deliberately return to written mode because automatic skills fail first under relapse.
- **Sequence for depression**: behavioral activation first (Ch. 13), cognitive skills after activity is moving. For anxiety, Chs. 10–11 may precede 5–9 (Fig. 14.6).

## Moves

### [CBT:TR7] Seven-column Thought Record
**Move:** Worksheet 9.2, taught piecewise Chs. 6–9: (1) situation — Who/What/When/Where, a slice of ≤30 minutes; (2) each mood in one word rated 0–100%, circle the one to work, add physical reactions for anxiety/panic; (3) all automatic thoughts and images actually present — elicit with "what went through my mind just before the feeling" plus mood-specific probes (depression: what this means about me/my life/future; anxiety: what I fear will happen — write out the answer to every "what if…?"; anger: what this says about how they treat me; guilt/shame: what rule I broke), rate each thought 0–100% for how strongly it alone produces the circled mood, mark the hottest (Worksheet 7.4); (4) factual evidence supporting the hot thought — facts an observer would verify, not interpretations (drill: Worksheet 8.1); (5) actively hunted evidence against it — counterexamples, what I'd tell a friend, discounted small details, similar past situations, the 5-years-later view; (6) alternative thought if evidence contradicts, or balanced thought (col-4 summary + "and" + col-5 summary) if support is partial, belief rated 0–100%; (7) rerate the moods. Images count as thoughts and can be tested directly (compare a selfie with the imagined beet-red face); direct testing beats inference (Vic phoned and asked).
**APPLY WHEN:** any recurring or out-of-scale mood; mood-congruent tunnel vision suspected; the operative appraisal is unclear.
**AVOID WHEN:** logging whole days or generalized states — signal drowns; suicidal hot thoughts or surfacing trauma memories — the book models those evidence searches as therapist-guided; substituting cheerleading positives that ignore column 4.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Padesky's 7-column record (1983), core of trial-supported cognitive therapy; strongest method-level base in the book; no standalone worksheet RCT.

### [CBT:NOSHIFT] No-mood-change troubleshooting
**Move:** When a completed Thought Record moves nothing, audit (Ch. 9): situation specific enough? moods correctly named and rated? tested thought actually hot for the circled mood? multiple hot thoughts each needing separate evidence? an even hotter thought missed? column 5 thin? alternative not believable? Then route: evidence mostly supports the hot thought → Action Plan or acceptance (Ch. 10); alternative fits the evidence but feels false → strengthen it — for thoughts believed <50%, one week of daily real-world evidence logging with deliberate tests (Worksheet 10.1), revising the thought if data contradict it — or suspect an assumption (Ch. 11) / core belief (Ch. 12).
**APPLY WHEN:** a properly completed record yields no mood shift; a new thought stays "head-level" true.
**AVOID WHEN:** concluding from one flat record that the method failed or the hot thought must be true; rigging strengthening tests toward confirmation, or reading one setback as refutation.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** authors' clinical troubleshooting heuristic inside trial-supported CT.

### [CBT:ACT-SCHED] Behavioral activation pipeline
**Move:** Ch. 13: record one week hourly — an activity word + mood 0–100 (Worksheet 13.4, catching up a few times daily; the book pauses the chapter until the week exists); analyze with 8 questions (13.5: activity–mood links, better/worse times of day and week); then pre-plan several activities per day (13.6) across four categories — pleasure, accomplishment, approaching the avoided, values-fit — do them regardless of motivation, cross out the plan and log the actual + mood; verify with pre/post Depression Inventory scores over several weeks. While active, deliberately hunt tiny positives through all five senses ("capturing enjoyment") when positive affect is blunted.
**APPLY WHEN:** depression — the book's recommended first step before cognitive work; inactivity and rumination; unclear mood drivers.
**AVOID WHEN:** treating skipped items as failure (goal is more activity, not perfect completion); comparing enjoyment to the pre-depression baseline (Ben's golf backfire) — compare to sitting home doing nothing; using the review for self-criticism about wasted time.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** behavioral activation, named one of the two most effective depression treatments; strong method-level trials; no book RCT.

### [CBT:FEAR-LADDER] Build a Fear Ladder
**Move:** Worksheets 14.4–14.5: list everything avoided because of anxiety, rate anxiety 0–100 while imagining each item, order least→most feared onto ladder steps; steps may hold graded sub-variants (Paul: elevators 1→10 floors, driving 5→40 minutes, 5→50 miles); equal ratings ordered by feel, blank steps allowed.
**APPLY WHEN:** avoidance identified and approach work is being prepared — a single event (Juanita's speech) or a web of triggers (Paul's panic).
**AVOID WHEN:** none for the mapping itself — building the ladder is safe even when climbing it should be supervised (see [CBT:EXPOSURE]).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** standard graded-hierarchy construction; exposure methodology has method-level RCT support across anxiety disorders.

### [CBT:SAFETY-ID] Identify safety behaviors
**Move:** Per anxious situation, list safety behaviors — things done (checking locks, gripping a companion, pill in the pocket) and not done (no eye contact, aisle seat for quick exit) — and test each against the two criteria separating them from coping: aimed at eliminating danger rather than approaching/managing it, and maintaining anxiety over time rather than reducing it.
**APPLY WHEN:** anxiety persists despite apparent "coping"; an exposure step produces zero anxiety (diagnostic of hidden safety behaviors); subtle rituals suspected.
**AVOID WHEN:** stripping all supports at once with no approach plan to replace them.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** safety-behavior model, core to method-level CBT for panic and social anxiety; book vignettes (Tyra, Kenji, Roberta).

### [CBT:BEH-EXPT] Test If–then assumptions with behavioral experiments
**Move:** Surface the rule by completing "If I … then …" and the converse "If I don't … then …" (Worksheet 11.1: 2 behaviors, 2 avoidances, 2 emotion triggers). Then on Worksheet 11.2: assumption, a specific prediction, expected problems + coping strategies; run experiments — do the "if" and watch whether "then" follows, observe others (gentler entry for perfectionism), do the opposite behavior, interview friends about their rules; record outcomes vs. prediction in writing, including surprises; after ≥3 experiments write an alternative assumption fitting the data. Small daily-size steps first.
**APPLY WHEN:** the same mood/behavior recurs across situations; an evidenced alternative thought still isn't believed in the hot state (Linda: "anxiety, not heart attack" held only when calm); perfectionism, worry-as-protection, couple rule clashes, excess behaviors.
**AVOID WHEN:** concluding from one trial; running experiments unwritten — outcomes get re-narrated as luck; guessing others' assumptions from behavior and treating it as fact; trauma material or genuinely dangerous targets.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** standard Beckian change method, method-level trial support; synchronized with Beck-manual CBT. Mike's prediction ≥2 of 3 clerks negative → actual 0 of 3.

### [CBT:REGULATE] Regulation toolkit (breathing, PMR, mindful acceptance, imagery)
**Move:** Four skills with one purpose — tolerate anxiety long enough to stay in the situation, never to eliminate it: (a) balanced breathing, in 4 / out 4, belly-led, ≥4 minutes, practiced 4×/day for a week while calm before use under fire; (b) progressive muscle relaxation — tense 5 s / release 10–15 s, twice per group, ≈15 minutes; (c) mindful acceptance during ordinary activities — full sensory present-moment focus, notice drift without judging, return gently; anxiety lives in the future, absorbed present attention displaces it; (d) calming or competence imagery in all five senses (Jolene re-living piano-teaching confidence before a feared meeting). Choose your best 1–2 via Worksheet 14.6: rate anxiety 0–100 before/after every practice.
**APPLY WHEN:** physical arousal symptoms; needing to stay longer on exposure steps; future-oriented worry; stuck waiting situations.
**AVOID WHEN:** deployed as escape from exposure or to make anxiety vanish — that turns the skill into a safety behavior; first use mid-panic with no prior practice; judging a method on one bad trial; expecting a wander-free mind — noticing drift is the rep.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** standard CBT arousal-management components (method-level tradition); mindfulness at method level via mindfulness-based programs; authors' physiological O2/CO2 rationale for the 4-minute figure.

### [CBT:ACCEPT] Three paths of acceptance
**Move:** Worksheet 10.3: (1) observe thoughts and moods a few minutes daily with curiosity, without judging or changing them, gently returning when hooked; (2) big-picture weighing — name the counterbalancing aspects the distress-focus hides; (3) link tolerated distress to a chosen value and act toward it while the distress is present (Rodney visiting his father "as a nice man who visits"; Lupe's dignity-focused final months).
**APPLY WHEN:** unsolvable or must-be-endured circumstances (terminal illness, dementia, loss); recurring internal reactions; self-judgment about anxiety escalating it on entering feared-but-chosen situations.
**AVOID WHEN:** used as resignation, approval of events, or positive substitution; replacing the solving of solvable problems; expecting the observation practice to feel easy — frustration and boredom are part of it.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** acceptance/mindfulness-informed CBT component, method-level support; author cases (Sal, Lupe, Marissa).

### [CBT:MOOD-ID] Name and rate moods precisely
**Move:** Name each mood in a single word (Worksheet 4.1; ~30-word Mood List; body clues — tight shoulders → fear/irritation, heaviness → depression) and rate intensity 0–100 for the specific situation (4.2; anchors 0 / 50 / 100 = most ever). Discriminator: a mood fits one word — anything needing a sentence is a thought ("I'm out of control" is a thought; the mood is panic). Drill: sort 33 items into situation/mood/thought against the answer key (Worksheet 6.1).
**APPLY WHEN:** vague global states ("bad," "numb," "out of control"); thoughts offered when feelings are asked for; before Thought Record work — different moods respond to different techniques.
**AVOID WHEN:** rating averaged over a whole day instead of a specific situation — it masks the signal.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** standard CBT labeling/scaling skill embedded in trial-supported protocols; Vic's "out of control" lump split into anger vs. anxiety with distinct thoughts and targets.

### [CBT:MOOD-TRACK] Inventories and mood metrics
**Move:** Baseline before starting, then weekly or biweekly (not only on bad days): Mind Over Mood Depression Inventory (13.1: 19 items × 0–3, max 57) and/or Anxiety Inventory (14.1: 24 items × 0–3, max 72), charted on 13.2/14.2; when torn between two numbers, count the higher. For anger, guilt, shame, happiness: three 0–100 scales — frequency, weekly-peak strength, duration — one chart per mood (15.1–15.2). Happiness at least monthly; monthly monitoring once recovered. Judge strategies by multi-week trends.
**APPLY WHEN:** any sustained workbook use; deciding whether an approach works; communicating with a professional — bring the filled sheets.
**AVOID WHEN:** reading inventories as diagnosis — they are explicitly not diagnostic; over-reading single-week fluctuation (recovery is jagged); any endorsement of suicidality items 10–12 on the depression inventory → involve a professional, not self-help alone.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** author-constructed instruments — face-valid, not independently validated; the tracking cadence follows measurement-based-care practice.

### [CBT:5PART] Five-part problem map
**Move:** Worksheet 2.1: describe recent changes or chronic problems in all five areas — environment/life events (stressors over 1/3/5 years and childhood, ongoing adversity), physical reactions, moods (one word each), behaviors (done and avoided), thoughts/images during strong moods — then look for cross-area connections and pick the smallest intervention with the largest propagation.
**APPLY WHEN:** entry assessment; diffuse overwhelm; needing a shared map before choosing techniques.
**AVOID WHEN:** unexplained physical symptoms not yet medically evaluated (Ben was assessed only after his physician excluded medical causes) — the model maps a problem, it does not diagnose.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Padesky's five-part model (1986), the book's framing framework; cited by the authors as cross-culturally usable; no standalone trial.

### [CBT:GOALSET] Goals, ambivalence, strengths, early signs
**Move:** Worksheets 5.1–5.4 as a block: write 2+ observable, measurable goal changes ("more enjoyable conversations with my children," not "improve my relationships"); per goal a 2×2 grid — advantages/disadvantages of reaching and of not reaching it — to surface hidden payoffs of the status quo; list qualities, past overcome obstacles, supports, and values that make success plausible; pre-register concrete early improvement signs (~16-item checklist + 2–3 personal) and actively watch for them.
**APPLY WHEN:** starting the program; motivation dips; hopelessness about capacity to change; progress likely to be gradual and easy to discount (especially depression).
**AVOID WHEN:** goals stated as vague inner states with no observable marker — recast first; reading mixed feelings about change as a sign the goal is wrong (ambivalence is expected).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** authors' clinical practice; kin to decisional-balance and goal-specificity findings in behavior change; no direct trial.

### [CBT:ACTION-PLAN] Action Plan for real problems
**Move:** Worksheet 10.2: goal, concrete actions, start time, anticipated obstacles, a coping strategy per obstacle, dated progress log — one plan per problem area. Specificity is the active ingredient (Marissa: bounded asks with reciprocity, alarm across the room, clothes laid out, 10-minutes-early coffee reward; Vic: anger self-rating 0–10 with timeout at 3, break + Thought Record at 5). Use setbacks to refine, not quit.
**APPLY WHEN:** the Thought Record confirms an accurate distressing thought — a real solvable problem, including real mistreatment (then the record's job is confirming and managing, not disputing).
**AVOID WHEN:** the circumstance is genuinely unsolvable — route to acceptance; the problem is a distorted appraisal — test the thought instead; plans missing the obstacles/strategies columns tend to stall.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** problem-solving component of CBT, method-level; author cases include suicidality reduced by restored job control — done alongside therapy.

### [CBT:ANGER-KIT] Anger strategy set
**Move:** Ch. 15: (1) test angry thoughts with Thought Records — check personalization and assumed intent, gather information before reacting (Rick asked about the coffee instead of concluding thoughtlessness), and watch recurring labels ("thoughtless") as a box that harvests confirming misreadings; (2) rehearse predictable high-risk events in imagery, including things going poorly, optionally with a written script aimed at what you want to achieve; (3) learn personal early warning signs (clenched jaw/fists, chest pressure, yelling, saying untrue things) as a choice point; (4) timeout 5 minutes–24 hours at the signs — regroup, relax, test thoughts, re-enter with a new strategy; (5) rate anger 0–100 before/after each strategy across trials (15.5) and adopt the best 1–2.
**APPLY WHEN:** frequent anger damaging close relationships — where implicit expectations concentrate; one person consistently labeled and judged; foreseeable trigger events.
**AVOID WHEN:** anger flags a real ongoing injustice — the task is then constructive response, not dissolving the anger; timeout used as avoidance without return or as punitive silence; rehearsal sliding into grievance rumination; anger confined to family and self-help failing → couple/family therapy.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** standard anger-management components plus Beck-tradition restructuring, method-level; authors' formulation and cases.

### [CBT:ASSERT] Assertion
**Move:** The middle road between attacking and being attacked: start with "I" statements for reactions and needs instead of "you"-blame; acknowledge the true part of a complaint while standing your ground; state wants plainly instead of expecting mind-reading; judge success by clarity of communication (process), not by getting the result. Test interfering assumptions ("refusing means being disliked," "asking is pointless") via Thought Records or experiments.
**APPLY WHEN:** anger born of being taken advantage of; blowups after passive over-accommodation (the tired-parent pattern).
**AVOID WHEN:** expecting every assertive act to win compliance — assertion guarantees clear communication, never the outcome.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** assertiveness-training tradition, method-level; authors' four-strategy formulation.

### [CBT:CB-IDENT] Identify core beliefs
**Move:** Two routes: complete "I am …", "Others are …", "The world is …" while vividly re-imagining a recent strong-mood situation (Worksheet 12.1); or downward arrow — from an automatic thought repeatedly ask "if this is true, what does it mean about me / other people / the world?" until an absolute all-or-nothing statement appears (12.2–12.4). Failure to find a belief in one domain just means that situation doesn't involve it.
**APPLY WHEN:** Thought Records, Action Plans, acceptance, and experiments have been practiced yet moods persist; a recurrent life pattern needs explanation. Ch. 12 is explicitly optional — top-level work often suffices.
**AVOID WHEN:** used as first-line work — surface levels change faster and usually lift mood; choosing a traumatic memory as the situation to relive solo.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** standard Beckian technique, method-level support; Marissa and Vic illustrations.

### [CBT:CB-STRENGTHEN] Build the new core belief (not by disputing the old)
**Move:** Do NOT weigh evidence for/against a negative core belief — the active belief filters disconfirming data (Marissa discounted love and invitations as pity). Instead: word a new companion belief (12.5 — opposite, qualified "some people hurt, most are kind," reframed, or acceptance-based "OK because I can handle it"); log small supporting events for weeks–months (12.6, 25 slots — write items even if "too small to count"; the belief is the container that stops positives draining away) and rerate confidence 0–100 every few weeks (12.7); act the belief out — 2–3 behaviors expressing it, strangers first, then family, predictions from old vs. new belief recorded (12.9); for all-or-nothing self-judgment, rate partial results on a 0–100 continuum (12.8 — Vic's imperfect-but-contained outburst = 75% anger control, not failure). Work one worksheet at a time (exception: 12.6 + 12.7 as a pair); keep mood measures running.
**APPLY WHEN:** a dominant negative belief identified; perfectionism, binary self-evaluation; relapse first-aid — re-read the record in bad periods.
**AVOID WHEN:** expecting to erase the negative belief (a flexible pair is the goal) or fast movement — months-scale, flat stretches normal, mood usually improves near ~50% confidence; reading pushback from people invested in your old role as proof of the old belief (Carla's family resisted, then renegotiated); staring at the missing percentage.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Padesky strengths-focused core-belief protocol, clinical-consensus level within an evidence-based frame; Marissa ~0% → ~70% "I'm lovable" over a year of tracking.

### [CBT:GRAT-KIND] Gratitude and kind acts
**Move:** Gratitude journal: 5 minutes once a week for ≥6 weeks across three domains — world/my life, others, myself (12.10–12.12); a few items in depth beat long lists; re-read earlier entries weekly; 8 review questions after 6 weeks (12.13). Extend socially: voiced appreciation even to strangers, direct thanks face-to-face, by phone, or letter — writing counts even unsent (12.14). Kind acts: several weeks of planned small kindnesses, logged (12.15, 22 slots), then review effects on moods, relationships, and which positive core beliefs activated (Christine's post-office turnaround).
**APPLY WHEN:** strengthening positive core beliefs; low positive affect; wanting a maintenance habit; transforming recurring hostile micro-environments.
**AVOID WHEN:** gratitude used to ignore legitimate negatives — the book is explicit that you can be grateful and still send the cold meal back.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** generically cited gratitude research and one unnamed kindness study (daily acts × 4 weeks → happiness and relationship satisfaction); method-level positive-psychology findings, weakest-sourced part of the book.

### [CBT:GUILT-SHAME-PATH] Five components for guilt and shame
**Move:** Guilt usually needs 1–2 components, shame may need all 5: (1) assess seriousness — personal 0–100 scale anchored by your lifetime-worst act (15.6) plus perspective questions (would others rate it serious; my best friend's verdict; importance in 1 month / 1 year / 5 years; did I know the consequences then; is damage correctable; did I avoid worse); (2) weigh responsibility — [CBT:RESP-PIE]; (3) reparations — whom I hurt, which of my own values it violated, scripted amends: acknowledge → why it was wrong → apologize → offer repair → ask forgiveness "in time" (15.8); (4) break the silence — [CBT:BREAK-SILENCE]; (5) self-forgiveness — 9 prompts ending in a kind-voiced compassionate paragraph; reframe "I did it because I'm awful" → "I did it during an awful period of my life" (15.9).
**APPLY WHEN:** should/ought self-judgment; guilt over many small things needing calibration (affair vs. late-for-dinner contrast); secrecy-fed shame; compassion flows to others but not to yourself.
**AVOID WHEN:** talking yourself out of a genuinely serious ongoing violation instead of changing the behavior — sometimes guilt is healthy and the answer is amends plus change; amends made primarily to extract forgiveness (the other may never forgive); self-forgiveness used to skip warranted amends or confused with condoning, forgetting, or denying harm.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** authors' protocol; cognitive-reappraisal logic with compassion-focused parallels; no trials cited in the book.

### [CBT:RESP-PIE] Responsibility pie
**Move:** Worksheet 15.7: list every person and circumstance that contributed to the event, putting yourself last; divide a circle into proportional slices; then ask whether 100% was really yours, how the pie changes the feeling, and what amends fit your actual slice.
**APPLY WHEN:** inflated personal responsibility — survivor self-blame, chronic guilt over small things; sizing your real share before reparations.
**AVOID WHEN:** expecting it always to reduce guilt — it sometimes correctly confirms responsibility and points to amends (Vic); childhood-abuse events — do it with a therapist: Marissa needed multiple sessions to accept that molestation is entirely adult responsibility.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** classic CBT reattribution technique, clinical-consensus level; two worked cases in the book.

### [CBT:RELAPSE-PLAN] Skills checklist, relapse plan, and the upper floors
**Move:** While well: rate every book skill 0–3 on four axes — used, helpful, still use, future use — and mark which are automatic (16.1); then build the plan (16.2): personal high-risk situations; early warning signs across behaviors (withdrawal, procrastination, staying in bed), thoughts, moods (rising inventory scores, irritability), and body (ask family/friends for your blind spots); a numeric score that triggers the plan (Ben: depression >15 → resume written Thought Records until <10) — written deliberate mode, because automatic skills fail first under relapse. Rehearse the plan in vivid imagery — scene, warning signs, each step — and rate confidence: low plan confidence → extend the plan; low skill confidence → practice while well. Keep the book visible with tapering reviews (daily user → weekly for months; weekly user → monthly). Once recovered, redeploy the same skills on positive targets ("elevator to the upper floors"): gratitude, kindness, savoring, four-category activity planning, positive imagery of new ways you'd like to be, experiments for life changes.
**APPLY WHEN:** after improvement, while well; finishing active workbook use; goal shifting from relief to life satisfaction.
**AVOID WHEN:** postponed until crisis — the point is preparing before warning signs; score-watching turning into anxious self-monitoring; thresholds replacing clinical judgment when severe symptoms (suicidality) appear; the imagined high-risk scene is itself traumatic; pathologizing normal mood fluctuation — apply the four-criterion relapse definition (more severe / too long / too often / damaging life) first.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** method-level CBT relapse-prevention literature; continued-practice-lowers-relapse research cited without reference; Ben's case illustration.

### [CBT:EXPOSURE] Climb the Fear Ladder
**Move:** Enter the lowest step — in vivo, or first in five-senses imagery (optionally written/recorded for repeated exposures, imagining both smooth and rough versions to plan coping); stay until anxiety drops by more than half or below 40/100; repeat until confident, then take the next step; pace stays under the person's control; a trusted helper may support early steps with independence as the end state; combine with regulation skills (to stay, never to escape), Thought Records, and Action Plans (Juanita's speech). Rising anxiety at the start is a good sign; extend duration deliberately to test "if it continues, then catastrophe."
**APPLY WHEN:** avoidance maintains anxiety and a graded ladder exists — public speaking, elevators, driving, crowds, social fears; willingness to feel anxiety on purpose.
**AVOID WHEN:** trauma/PTSD memories or grief as the target — that exposure belongs in therapy sessions; a step produces zero anxiety (too easy, or safety behaviors active); regulation skills used as escape hatches; a helper becoming a permanent safety behavior.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** exposure is the best-supported intervention class for anxiety (method-level RCTs); the book offers it as self-help for non-traumatic fears, but under this lens a formal exposure program runs under guidance — especially with panic or PTSD in the picture; no book RCT.

### [CBT:PANIC-INDUCE] Interoceptive experiments for panic
**Move:** For catastrophic misinterpretation of body sensations: deliberately provoke the feared sensations (fast breathing for a few minutes; vivid start-to-finish imagining of the feared scenario, e.g., a flight), watch them normalize after stopping (Linda's heart rate settled in ~10 minutes), repeat daily, extend duration to test the "if it continues" prediction, then graded entry into avoided real situations (actual flights); distraction as a differential test — it reduces anxiety, not a heart attack.
**APPLY WHEN:** panic pattern — racing heart, dizziness, shortness of breath read as catastrophe — where Thought Records alone don't hold in the hot state.
**AVOID WHEN:** no fresh medical clearance (Linda's physician re-confirmed her heart before fast-breathing work); solo launch — the book depicts a therapist designing the program and accompanying the first office-based inductions.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** interoceptive-exposure component of CBT for panic — method-level RCT support; explicitly therapist-guided in the book.

### [CBT:BREAK-SILENCE] Break the shame secret
**Move:** Reveal the shame-feeding secret to the most trusted person(s), choosing a time and place with room to say everything and process the response; naming your anxiety about disclosing is allowed. Mechanism: the predicted condemnation finally gets reality-tested and is usually refuted — experienced acceptance forces a reassessment of the secret's meaning (Petra → Monique: teen drug use and university expulsion, met with acceptance).
**APPLY WHEN:** a long-held secret feeds shame (family secrets: alcoholism, abuse, abortion, bankruptcy); a genuinely safe confidant exists; the expected rejection has never been tested.
**AVOID WHEN:** the confidant is unsafe or judgmental; no adequate time or place; disclosure directed at or pressured by the abuser.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** authors' clinical mechanism, no trials cited; disclosure is irreversible, and for trauma-rooted secrets (Marissa's abuse — the chapter's flagship case) it opens traumatic material: plan it in therapy; non-trauma secrets like Petra's are lower-stakes.

### [CBT:FORGIVE-LETTER] Unsent forgiveness letter
**Move:** Worksheet 15.4, 8 prompts written uncensored and never sent: what you did to me; its impact; how it still affects me; how my life improves if I forgive; compassionate understanding of what shaped you; how I'd want to be viewed had I hurt someone; how I can forgive; my qualities for moving forward. Forgiveness is for the writer and need not be communicated. Alternative: tell the person directly with "I" statements, optionally setting conditions for forgiveness.
**APPLY WHEN:** long-carried anger over deep or repeated past hurt eating at wellbeing; contact with the person impossible or unwanted.
**AVOID WHEN:** the abuse is ongoing — the book says do not forgive then: get clear you are not to blame and build protection (Action Plans, distance); not ready — skipping and returning later is explicitly permitted; "forgiveness" used to pressure premature reconciliation.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** authors' clinical technique; adjacent forgiveness-writing research exists but none is cited in the book. An uncensored account of deep or repeated hurt opens traumatic material when the hurt was abuse — sequence those targets with the therapist; minor-hurt variants are lower-stakes.

## What the book debunks

- **Positive thinking** — explicitly rejected, repeatedly: simplistic, rarely produces lasting change, discards important information, and sets up a failure feeling when distress still occurs; a coping plan for expected anxiety beats reassurance. Balanced thoughts must honor column 4.
- **"Test every negative thought"** — hot thoughts are sometimes accurate ("my boss is abusing me"); then the record's job is confirmation and problem management, not disputation. Rationalization ("what do I care?") can deepen isolation.
- **Cognitive work on abuse** — reframing thoughts to tolerate ongoing abuse is a named misuse; the goal is stopping or leaving, with cognitive work only to mobilize getting help. Do not forgive ongoing abuse.
- **Waiting for motivation** — motivation follows action, not the reverse; start partially, for minutes if needed. Comparing enjoyment to the pre-depression baseline makes activity backfire (Ben's golf).
- **Avoidance and safety behaviors** — both relieve anxiety now and worsen it long-term (four learning channels blocked); zero anxiety during exposure is diagnostic of a too-easy step or active safety behaviors, not success. Eliminating anxiety entirely is the wrong goal (alarm-system analogy).
- **Medication myths** — "taking medication means I'm crazy" debunked; but medication-only treatment carries higher relapse than combined with CBT skills (anxiety: majority relapse within a year of stopping; tranquilizers show tolerance + withdrawal, and dampened anxiety blocks the learning that it is tolerable).
- **Depression ≠ normal aging** — Ben's fatigue and withdrawal misread as "just being old" blocked help-seeking; a harsh trauma history does not doom recovery (Marissa).
- **Self-help failure modes named by the authors** — rushing chapters to finish the book; quitting practice as soon as one feels better (skills need months to automate); testing a lukewarm instead of the hot thought; unwritten experiments re-narrated as luck; concluding from one flat record that the method failed.
- **Disputing negative core beliefs** — evidence-weighing fails at this level because the active belief filters the data (Marissa read love as pity); build the companion belief instead. Inflexible positive beliefs are also a risk — you still need to spot exploitation.
- **Absent anger can be the pathology** — learned helplessness after abuse can mean the goal is learning to feel anger at mistreatment, not controlling it.
- **Child sexual abuse is entirely adult responsibility** — a child's not saying no is not consent; stopping it later proves nothing about earlier ability (Marissa at 6 vs. 14).
- **Score-watching traps** — a single-week score rise ≠ no progress (Ben's week 3); judge multi-week trends; inventories are tracking tools, never diagnoses.

## Numbers that matter

- **Scales**: moods, beliefs, anxiety, seriousness all 0–100; skills 0–3; Vic's anger protocol 0–10 (timeout at 3, break + Thought Record at 5).
- **Depression Inventory**: 19 items × 0–3, max 57; suicidality items 10–12 endorsed → professional involvement. **Anxiety Inventory**: 24 items × 0–3, max 72. Cadence weekly/biweekly; monthly once recovered; happiness ≥ monthly.
- **6 weeks** of flat or rising scores despite exercises → change approach or seek professional help.
- **Thought Records**: 2–3/week; spontaneous balanced thinking typically after 20–40 records (Vic ~35); hot-thought rating practice ≥1×/day for a week before evidence work.
- **Belief <50%** in a new thought → one-week daily evidence log (Worksheet 10.1).
- **Experiments**: ≥3 before any conclusion; usually months before high confidence in a new assumption.
- **Exposure advance criterion**: anxiety cut by >50% or below 40/100; many challenges possible in one day; Paul topped a 7-step ladder in a few months; induced heart rate normalized ~10 minutes after stopping (Linda).
- **Regulation doses**: breathing 4-in/4-out ≥4 min, 4×/day for a week before deployment; PMR 5 s tense / 10–15 s release ×2 per group, ≈15 min.
- **Core beliefs**: 25-slot record; confidence rerated every few weeks; mood usually improves near ~50% confidence; Marissa ~0% → ~70% over a year.
- **Positive practices**: gratitude 5 min × 1/week × ≥6 weeks; kindness study daily acts × 4 weeks; 10 enjoyable activities/week likely beat 5.
- **Antidepressants**: 2–4 weeks to effect, 8+ weeks to therapeutic level of the right drug, typical course 1–2 years; taper only gradually and physician-directed.
- **Timeout**: 5 minutes to 24 hours, always with return.
- **Trajectories (Epilogue)**: Marissa's >30-score weeks fell 7 → 4 → 1 across successive 10-week blocks; 3+ years suicide-free at follow-up; Ben's re-engagement thresholds 15 (resume) / 10 (stop); recovery curves jagged by design — Ben's score rose in week 3 despite progress.
- **Context figures**: 50,000–70,000 thoughts/day (cited estimate); >1 million readers of the 1st edition, 22+ languages; Padesky trained 45,000+ therapists.

## Blind spots of this book

- **No book-level RCT** — every evidence claim rides on method-level CBT trials; the ABCT seal and NHS listing certify curation, not outcome data for the workbook itself. The inventories are author-constructed and not validated as diagnostic scales, yet the whole progress logic runs on them.
- **Self-report all the way down** — no external calibration of any measure; the same mind whose distortions are being treated scores its own change.
- **Trauma is fenced, not treated** — the book consistently routes trauma material to the therapist (correctly for this lens) but offers no trauma-processing protocol; a PTSD reader gets boundaries and referrals, not a path.
- **Severity and comorbidity ceiling** — nothing for bipolar, psychosis, or primary substance dependence (Vic's alcohol is handled adjacently); the book itself concedes that poor concentration/memory makes it unusable → professional care. Assumes literacy, writing habit, and weeks-to-months adherence — exactly the adherence problem the "under guidance" role exists to solve.
- **Individual-cognition bias** — environmental levers (saying no, leaving, reducing harassment) are acknowledged in Ch. 3 but underdeveloped; abusive relationships and systemic adversity (discrimination, finances) get referral pointers, not tools.
- **Interpersonal mechanics thin** — couple/family therapy is repeatedly pointed to but never taught; assertion gets four strategies, relationships otherwise stay background.
- **Weakly sourced positive-psychology chapters** — gratitude and kindness claims cite "a lot of recent research" without references; the cross-cultural usability claim rests on the authors' practice.
- **Generalization claim untested** — the promise that 20–40 written records produce automatic balanced thinking is the authors' clinical observation, not a measured outcome.
