# advisor-cbt — moves
Curated move set of the Beck-model CBT lens, deduplicated across the Beck manual (Basics and Beyond, 3rd ed.) and the Greenberger & Padesky workbook (Mind Over Mood, 2nd ed.). Tier is set per OPERATION under safety-protocol §3 — the same school yields both tiers. Codes are unique inside this file; where the two sources used one shortcode for one move it is merged and both sources appear in Evidence, where they used one shortcode for two different operations the entries were renamed apart.

## Entry: mapping the problem before choosing a technique

### [CBT:5PART] Five-part problem map
**Move:** On one page describe the present problem across five areas — life events and environment (recent plus chronic adversity), body reactions, moods (one word each), behaviours done and behaviours avoided, thoughts and images present during strong moods — then mark the links between areas and pick the smallest change with the widest knock-on effect (Worksheet 2.1).
**APPLY WHEN:** first pass on a diffuse "everything is bad" state; a shared map is needed before any technique is chosen; chronic problem with no obvious lever. / **AVOID WHEN:** unexplained physical symptoms not yet medically checked — the map organises a problem, it does not explain a body; using the map to characterise the person rather than the situation.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Padesky's five-part model (1986), the workbook's framing device; no standalone trial — method-level only.

### [CBT:MOOD-ID] Name and rate the mood precisely
**Move:** Give each mood a one-word name (Worksheet 4.1 mood list; body clues — tight shoulders toward fear or irritation, heaviness toward depression) and rate its strength 0–100 for one specific situation, not the day (Worksheet 4.2, anchored at 0 / 50 / worst ever). Discriminator: a mood fits in one word — anything needing a sentence is a thought ("I'm out of control" is a thought; the mood underneath may be panic, or anger).
**APPLY WHEN:** vague global states ("bad", "numb", "off"); thoughts offered when feelings were asked for; always before thought work, since different moods route to different techniques. / **AVOID WHEN:** rating an averaged whole day — the signal disappears; treating the label as a verdict about the person.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** standard labelling/scaling skill embedded in trial-supported protocols; workbook case where one "out of control" lump split into anger and anxiety with different targets.

### [CBT:AT-CATCH] Catch and formulate the automatic thought
**Move:** At any shift in mood, body state or behaviour ask the cardinal question — what just went through my mind — write the thought down and append that it may not be true, or not entirely true; use a physical reminder if it keeps slipping. Convert it to an evaluable form: the literal words rather than your interpretation of them, telegraphic exclamations spelled out, question-shaped thoughts restated as the feared outcome. If nothing surfaces, describe the scene in present tense, picture it, replay the exchange, or ask whether an image appeared instead of words.
**APPLY WHEN:** from day one — this is the entry skill everything else runs on; thoughts arise before, during and after a situation, and any component can trigger the next. / **AVOID WHEN:** expecting accuracy immediately — this is trained over weeks; stacking retrieval tricks into an interrogation; deliberately heightening affect on a traumatic memory to force the thought out; someone else supplying their guess at your thought, which invites false agreement.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** method canon (Beck lineage); the core skill of the 2,000+-study CBT base, evidenced at protocol level, never as an isolated skill.

### [CBT:MOOD-TRACK] Measured tracking with an agreed expectation
**Move:** Baseline first, then weekly or biweekly on a fixed schedule (not only on bad days): a symptom scale covering the whole past week — PHQ-9, GAD-7, or the workbook's own Depression Inventory (19 items × 0–3) and Anxiety Inventory (24 items × 0–3), charted; plus well-being, functioning and goal progress. Where two answers tempt you, score the higher. Compare with the previous point, probe mismatches between the number and the story, and agree in advance on an expected course as a range rather than a promise. <!-- privacy-ok: GAD-7 — публичная шкала из книги-источника, не данные пользователя -->
**APPLY WHEN:** any sustained effort; deciding whether an approach is working; preparing material to bring to a live session. / **AVOID WHEN:** reading inventories as diagnosis — they are trackers only, and the workbook's are author-built and not independently validated; over-reading one week's fluctuation; any endorsement of the suicidality items — that leaves the self-help frame entirely and goes to the crisis circuit and a person.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck manual Chs. 5–6, 10 + workbook Chs. 13–15; routine outcome monitoring is real but small (pooled d≈0.13–0.27, LOW per PG26) — treat it as cheap tail-protection, not a driver.

### [CBT:VALUES-GOALS] Values, observable goals, decisional balance, early signs
**Move:** Elicit what matters, or mattered before things narrowed (offer domains: relationships, work, health, community, meaning, creativity, outdoors), then convert to two or more goals stated as observable changes under your own control ("more conversations with my children I enjoy", not "improve my relationships"). Per goal run a 2×2 grid — advantages and disadvantages of reaching it and of not reaching it — to surface the hidden payoff of the status quo; list qualities, past obstacles overcome and supports that make it plausible; pre-register concrete early signs of improvement and watch for them; decompose big goals into ordered steps with obstacles named per step in three classes — practical, thought-shaped, skill-shaped.
**APPLY WHEN:** starting any change effort; drift and low initiative; a goal exists but nothing moves; improvement will be gradual and easy to discount. / **AVOID WHEN:** pressing open questions when nothing feels important — offer domain prompts instead; goals about other people's behaviour left unconverted; reading mixed feelings about changing as proof the goal is wrong; steps so small they have become avoidance.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** merged — Beck Chs. 6, 9, 19 (goal work across life areas; miracle question from solution-focused practice) + workbook Worksheets 5.1–5.4; authors' clinical practice, kin to goal-specificity findings, no direct trial.

## Working one hot thought

### [CBT:SOCRATIC-EVAL] Guided evaluation of a selected thought
**Move:** Filter first — belief strength, emotion intensity, recurrence, whether it blocks something you want (not every thought earns work) — then walk the question set: evidence for it, asked first, then evidence against; an alternative explanation; worst case plus how you would cope, best case, most realistic case; the effect of keeping the thought versus changing it; what you would say to a named friend in this position; what to do now. Re-rate belief and emotion afterwards; a partial shift is the success criterion.
**APPLY WHEN:** one important, distressing, recurring, checkable thought is blocking action. / **AVOID WHEN:** arguing or "challenging" — the degree of distortion is unknowable in advance, most such thoughts hold a grain of truth, and disputing invalidates; rumination and obsessing (per-thought work feeds the process — see [CBT:MINDFUL-PROCESS]); agreement arriving with no relief, which means switch to usefulness rather than accuracy or suspect an unspoken assumption; when the feared outcome is death, the coping question is not asked.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Braun et al. 2015 links Socratic work to symptom change (correlational, within-treatment); clients prefer it (Heiniger 2018); Beck Ch. 14 — method canon.

### [CBT:TR7] Written thought record
**Move:** Two formats of one operation. Padesky's seven columns (Worksheet 9.2): situation as Who/What/When/Where in a slice under ~30 minutes; each mood one word 0–100, circle the one being worked; every automatic thought and image present, each rated for how strongly it alone drives that mood, hottest one marked; facts an observer could verify that support the hot thought; then actively hunted evidence against it (counterexamples, advice to a friend, discounted details, similar past situations, the five-years-later view); an alternative or a balanced thought joining both evidence columns with "and", rated 0–100; re-rate the moods. Beck's Testing Your Thoughts sheet is the same operation with the [CBT:SOCRATIC-EVAL] questions as columns, taught only after one successful guided run. Optionally label the thought against the twelve-error list (all-or-nothing, catastrophising, discounting the positive, emotional reasoning, labelling, magnification, mental filter, mind reading, overgeneralisation, personalisation, should statements, tunnel vision) — first identify your own habitual three or four, then watch only for those.
**APPLY WHEN:** a recurring or out-of-scale mood where structure organises thinking better than mental question-and-answer; images count as thoughts and can be tested directly. / **AVOID WHEN:** logging whole days or global states — signal drowns; a hot thought that is suicidal, or a surfacing trauma memory — those evidence searches are shown as therapist-guided in both books; cheerleading positives that ignore the supporting-evidence column; assigning a sheet before one guided run, since a failed first attempt discredits the tool; low functioning, writing aversion or low literacy (use audio); the full distortion list when it just overwhelms — labelling is optional.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** merged — Padesky's seven-column record (1983), workbook Chs. 6–9; Beck Ch. 15 worksheet packet; no worksheet-specific RCT anywhere — the trials are of whole protocols containing them.

### [CBT:NOSHIFT] Troubleshooting a record that moved nothing
**Move:** Audit in order: was the situation specific enough; were moods named and rated right; was the thought tested actually the hot one for that mood; are there several hot thoughts each needing its own evidence; was an even hotter thought missed; is the evidence-against column thin; is the alternative simply not believable. Then route: if the evidence mostly supports the thought go to [CBT:PROBLEM-SOLVE] or [CBT:ACCEPT]; if the alternative fits the evidence but feels false, strengthen it — for beliefs under 50%, a week of daily real-world evidence logging with deliberate small tests, revising the wording if data contradict it; if it still holds, suspect a cross-situational rule ([CBT:BEH-EXP]) or a belief-level pattern ([CBT:BELIEF-MAP]).
**APPLY WHEN:** a properly completed record produces no mood change; a new thought stays true-in-the-head only. / **AVOID WHEN:** concluding from one flat record that the method failed or that the hot thought must therefore be true; rigging the strengthening log toward confirmation; reading one setback as refutation.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** authors' troubleshooting heuristic inside trial-supported cognitive therapy (workbook Ch. 9); no separate evidence.

### [CBT:PROBLEM-SOLVE] When the thought is accurate: solve, or examine the conclusion
**Move:** Stop testing accuracy and switch tracks. (a) Structured problem solving: name the problem concretely, brainstorm (your own past solutions; what you would advise a friend), pick one small testable step, and write it as a plan with a start time, anticipated obstacles, a coping strategy per obstacle and a dated progress log (Worksheet 10.2) — specificity is the active ingredient, one plan per problem area. (b) Examine the conclusion riding on the true fact — "I can't concentrate" may be true while "so my mind is permanently broken" is the testable part. (c) When distress is diffuse, hypothetically remove candidate problems one at a time and rate the relief to find the real driver. Use setbacks to refine the plan rather than to quit.
**APPLY WHEN:** evidence confirms the distressing thought, including real mistreatment or a real constraint — then the record's job is confirming and managing, not disputing. / **AVOID WHEN:** the circumstance is genuinely unchangeable — route to [CBT:ACCEPT]; the appraisal is the problem — test the thought instead; premature acceptance of a solvable problem; solving logistics while the actual blocker is cognitive, or the reverse — real constraints deserve validating before any thought work; plans written without the obstacle and coping columns tend to stall.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** merged — Beck Chs. 9, 14, 19 (accurate-thought routing) + workbook Worksheet 10.2; problem-solving therapy tradition (D'Zurilla & Nezu), method-level.

### [CBT:REATTRIB] Reattribution: illness frame and fair comparison
**Move:** Attribute the symptoms — fatigue, indecision, blankness, the negativity itself — to the depressed state rather than to character, backed with concrete evidence of retained competence: the interference is real and it is not the same as having become incompetent. Then catch unfair comparisons — against people who are not depressed, against your pre-onset self, against an ideal self — and substitute a comparison with yourself at your worst point, listing what is possible now that was not then.
**APPLY WHEN:** "I'm lazy / I've become useless" self-blame; mood drops that follow a comparison. / **AVOID WHEN:** the illness frame turns into a licence for inactivity; comparing at the very worst point when there is not yet any progress to see; using it to explain away a real problem someone can actually act on.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck Chs. 6, 17, 19; author's clinical practice and analogies — method canon, no component trial.

### [CBT:RESP-PIE] Responsibility pie
**Move:** List every person, condition and circumstance that contributed to the event, putting yourself LAST — order matters, because a self-slice drawn first squeezes out everything else — then divide a circle into proportional slices, and ask what changed in the feeling, whether 100% was ever yours, and what repair actually fits the slice you do own.
**APPLY WHEN:** inflated personal responsibility; "it's entirely my fault"; sizing your real share before making amends. / **AVOID WHEN:** expecting it always to shrink guilt — it sometimes correctly confirms responsibility and points to amends; childhood-abuse events — the workbook's own flagship case needed repeated therapist-led sessions to arrive at the conclusion that a child carries none of it.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** merged — Beck Ch. 6 (worked case moving a self-attribution from 100% to 20%) + workbook Worksheet 15.7; classic reattribution technique at clinical-consensus level.

## Behaviour: activation, plans, experiments

### [CBT:ACT-SCHED] Activity scheduling and behavioural activation
**Move:** Log one week hour by hour — one activity word plus mood 0–100, caught up a few times a day — then read it with the review questions (which activities track with which moods, better and worse times of day and week). Next, pre-plan several specific activities per day across four categories: pleasure, accomplishment, approaching something avoided, and fit with values; state where and what, with range targets rather than perfection (leave the house four times; a five-minute walk counts); write the rationale plus a scripted reply to the predicted obstacle thought; do them regardless of motivation; log the actual activity and mood next to the plan; record predicted versus actual enjoyment and harvest the surprises. While active, deliberately hunt small positives through all five senses. Give explicit credit for every completed item and check the effect against your tracked scores over several weeks.
**APPLY WHEN:** withdrawal, couch-and-scroll loops, dropped self-care — the recommended FIRST step in depression, before cognitive work; scepticism that behaviour moves mood, which predicted-versus-actual data answers. / **AVOID WHEN:** promising an immediate lift — the payoff lags; packing an hourly schedule onto someone barely functioning (interleave rest); treating skipped items as failure — the goal is more activity, not a clean sheet; comparing enjoyment with the pre-depression baseline, which reliably backfires — compare with sitting at home instead; using the review to audit wasted time.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** merged — Beck Ch. 7 + workbook Ch. 13; behavioural activation as a standalone component holds at MODERATE (PG26), one of the better-evidenced pieces of the whole method.

### [CBT:ACTION-PLAN] Designing the week's practice and checking its likelihood
**Move:** End every work block with one written micro-practice built to the design rules: sized to current capacity, rationale stated, agreed rather than imposed, deliberately easy (ten-minute chunks), explicit when/where/how long, a reminder anchored to an existing routine, started immediately rather than later (continuing is easier than initiating), and imagined through to completion with credit. Before closing, rate how likely you are to do it, 0–100%; below roughly 90%, interrogate the gap, rehearse the moment in imagery to catch the interfering thought, or shrink the item. For the first week or two frame it as no-lose: either it is done, or you have data on what blocked it. Review it at the next block's start and mine what was completed for meaning.
**APPLY WHEN:** every cycle of change work; "no time, no energy" — probe with whether you would do it if the obstacle vanished. / **AVOID WHEN:** assigning unilaterally or one-size-fits-all; more than one step (multi-step tasks are undone by default); keeping the no-lose frame past about two weeks of non-completion; skipping review, which teaches that plans do not matter; treating non-completion as a character failure instead of a design failure — whoever set too hard an item owns that out loud.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck Ch. 8 design rules are the author's guidelines; homework presence d=0.48 is LOW after PG26 demotion, quality beats quantity (g=1.07 vs 0.51), and NO completion benchmark exists — the circulating 41% and 51%-vs-72% figures are refuted.

### [CBT:BEH-EXP] Behavioural experiment, at thought and at rule level
**Move:** Turn a prediction into a bounded test. At thought level: state the specific prediction, check what evidence it currently rests on, pre-plan coping for the bad branch, run it ("volunteer for thirty minutes and leave if energy runs out"), then explicitly harvest the conclusion — what did you learn, what does it say about the future — and extend it to other avoided situations. At rule level: surface the rule by completing "if I … then …" and its converse "if I don't … then …" (Worksheet 11.1 — two behaviours, two avoidances, two emotion triggers), then on Worksheet 11.2 write assumption, prediction, expected problems and coping, and run experiments — do the "if" and watch whether the "then" follows, observe other people (a gentler entry for perfectionism), deliberately do the opposite, interview friends about their rules — recording outcome against prediction in writing including surprises, and after three or more write an alternative assumption that fits the data.
**APPLY WHEN:** verbal work produced head-level agreement only; avoidance is what keeps the belief alive; the same pattern repeats across situations; the alternative thought holds when calm and collapses when hot. / **AVOID WHEN:** predictions about trauma disclosure or catastrophic bodily fear — therapist territory (see [CBT:EXPOSURE], [CBT:PANIC-INDUCE]); experiments rigged so any outcome confirms the belief; concluding from a single trial; running them unwritten, after which outcomes get re-narrated as luck; guessing someone else's assumption from their behaviour and treating it as fact; skipping the conclusion, which wastes the whole thing.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** merged — Beck Chs. 14, 18 + workbook Ch. 11; experiential disconfirmation outperforms verbal methods (Bennett-Levy et al. 2004); cognitive restructuring as a standalone component holds at MODERATE (PG26), the experiment format itself only method-level.

### [CBT:SKILL-VS-COG] Skill deficit versus blocking cognition
**Move:** Before training any skill, run one test question: if you were certain it would go well, what exactly would you do or say? A good, concrete answer means the skill is present and thoughts are blocking it — work the thought. A poor answer means a genuine gap — teach it, model it, rehearse it in role play with feedback and swapped roles, then put its use with a specific person into the week's plan.
**APPLY WHEN:** any performance obstacle — a difficult conversation, saying no, an interview, managing money, parenting. / **AVOID WHEN:** skipping the test — training a skill someone already has wastes the week and can land as an insult; rehearsing while the interfering thought stays unaddressed, since both need handling.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck Ch. 19; author's clinical heuristic, method canon — no separate trial.

### [CBT:ADV-DISADV] Advantages and disadvantages
**Move:** For a decision, a four-cell grid — advantages and disadvantages of each option, filling one cell prompts items for the others — then weight by circling or rating what matters most, conclude, and check whether missing information should be gathered first. For ambivalence about an action, a habit or a belief, list the advantages and disadvantages of the behaviour, or of keeping the old belief versus adopting the new one, so the short-term-relief trap becomes visible: staying in bed did feel better, for a few minutes, and that experiment has already run for months.
**APPLY WHEN:** indecision that is itself a symptom; ambivalence about a task, about ruminating, or about changing a belief. / **AVOID WHEN:** re-litigating a decision already made; using the grid to argue someone into your conclusion instead of eliciting theirs.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck Chs. 8, 17, 19; method canon plus author's practice — no component trial. Goal-level version of the same grid sits in [CBT:VALUES-GOALS].

### [CBT:THERAPY-NOTES] Notes and coping cards
**Move:** After any insight worth keeping, write the response in your own words in the form "when I think X, I'll remind myself Y", including ONLY statements you actually endorse — reword until you do, up to and including attributing it ("my therapist says it isn't my fault") rather than faking agreement. Keep them on a card, in a notebook, on the phone, or as audio; read them every morning and before predictable hard moments, anchored to an existing habit. Pre-write a coping note for the bad branch of any uncertain step.
**APPLY WHEN:** consolidating anything — unrecorded material is forgotten, and forgetting predicts poorer outcomes; anticipatory anxiety before a known challenge. / **AVOID WHEN:** recording anything you do not believe, which kills the tool; wordy or vague notes; reading them only in crises, since it is the advance rehearsal that integrates them; recording whole sessions instead of the two or three key points.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck Chs. 1, 6, 15; author's core practice plus a forgetting–outcome link (Lee et al. 2020) — method-level.

## Anxiety and avoidance

### [CBT:AWARE] The five-step stance for anxiety
**Move:** Accept the anxiety as ordinary activation rather than fighting or suppressing it; Watch it from a small distance, rating it 0–10 as it rises and falls, which makes it a thing you have rather than a thing you are; Act constructively as if you were not anxious — keep talking, keep walking, keep working, breathe slowly — without leaving; Repeat the cycle as it recurs; Expect the ordinary outcome, since the feared event usually does not happen and confidence accumulates per repetition. Learn it by walking the five steps through a specific upcoming situation in imagery.
**APPLY WHEN:** everyday and anticipatory anxiety, worry, anxiety about being anxious, strong but non-dangerous body sensations; adaptable to angry or depressive spirals. / **AVOID WHEN:** it becomes a ritual performed to make the anxiety go away — that inverts it into a safety behaviour; substituting it for a structured graded programme on entrenched fear (see [CBT:EXPOSURE]); before the sensations have been medically cleared.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck & Emery (1985) anxiety protocol via Beck Appendix C / Ch. 16 — method-level canon, no standalone trial.

### [CBT:REGULATE] Regulation toolkit: breathing, muscle release, present-moment attention, imagery
**Move:** Four skills with one stated purpose — tolerating anxiety long enough to STAY in the situation, never removing it: balanced breathing, in for four and out for four, belly-led, at least four minutes, practised four times a day for a week while calm before it is used under pressure; progressive muscle relaxation, tense five seconds and release ten to fifteen, twice per muscle group, about fifteen minutes; present-moment attention during ordinary activities, full sensory focus, noticing drift without judging and returning — anxiety lives in the future and absorbed attention displaces it; and calming or competence imagery in all five senses, re-living a situation where you were capable before entering one where you fear you will not be. Choose your best one or two by rating anxiety 0–100 before and after every practice.
**APPLY WHEN:** strong physical arousal; needing to stay longer on an approach step; future-oriented worry; forced waiting. / **AVOID WHEN:** used to escape an approach step or to abolish anxiety — that converts the skill into a safety behaviour; first attempted mid-panic with no prior practice; judged on one bad trial; expecting a wander-free mind, since noticing the drift IS the repetition; pushing a practice through worsening in someone with a trauma history — stop the practice rather than trying harder.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** workbook Ch. 14 and Worksheet 14.6; standard arousal-management components, method-level; the four-minute figure is the authors' physiological rationale, not a trial result.

### [CBT:SAFETY-ID] Name the safety behaviours
**Move:** For each anxious situation list what you do (checking, gripping a companion, carrying something "just in case") and what you carefully do not do (no eye contact, always the seat nearest the exit), then test each against two criteria that separate a safety behaviour from coping: is it aimed at removing danger rather than at approaching and managing the situation, and does it keep the anxiety alive over time rather than reducing it.
**APPLY WHEN:** anxiety persists despite apparent coping; an approach step produces no anxiety at all, which is diagnostic rather than encouraging; subtle rituals suspected. / **AVOID WHEN:** stripping every support at once with no approach plan to replace them; treating a support agreed with a therapist as if it were a defect.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** workbook Ch. 14; safety-behaviour model is core to method-level CBT for panic-pattern and social fear (Salkovskis lineage).

### [CBT:FEAR-LADDER] Avoidance inventory and draft ladder
**Move:** List everything currently avoided because of anxiety, rate the anxiety 0–100 while imagining each item, and order them least to most feared, allowing graded sub-variants within a step (minutes, floors, distance) and blank steps; where two items rate equally, order them by feel. Bring the draft to the live session as material.
**APPLY WHEN:** avoidance has been identified and approach work is being prepared; you need to see the shape of the avoidance rather than its worst item. / **AVOID WHEN:** treating your own draft as a programme cleared for climbing — hierarchy design and any escalation are therapist operations, and a failed solo exposure strengthens the fear; raising the difficulty between sessions on your own initiative.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** workbook Worksheets 14.4–14.5; standard hierarchy construction — the mapping step only, with the boundary set by PG26 (exposure design/escalation therapist-side, executing an agreed hierarchy SELF).

### [CBT:IMAGERY-KIT] Working with spontaneous images
**Move:** Hunt for the picture behind distress that is out of proportion to the words — ask whether there was a mental picture, a short film, a daydream — treat it as an automatic thought in another format, and normalise strange or graphic content. Then, depending on the case: check the image against what is actually happening now; deliberately continue an image frozen at its worst moment through to a realistic ending, or jump forward to the relief after the task is done; re-direct it as if you were the director and bridge to action by asking what would make the better ending likelier; rehearse an upcoming stressor hour by hour with coping inserted at each spike; re-inhabit a memory of doing something hard well.
**APPLY WHEN:** anticipatory anxiety about a concrete event; "this will never end" predictions; images that stop at peak threat; confidence collapsing before a challenge resembling one already mastered. / **AVOID WHEN:** intrusive repetitive images — that is a process, use [CBT:MINDFUL-PROCESS]; continuation that keeps spawning new catastrophes; imagery of catastrophic loss or death, and any traumatic memory as the target — rescripting a traumatic image is therapist-led even inside therapy; rehearsal drifting into a reassurance ritual.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck Ch. 20; imagery moves emotion more strongly than verbal processing (Hackmann et al. 2011); non-traumatic targets only, with mood monitoring and a stop rule, per PG26.

## When testing thoughts is the wrong tool

### [CBT:MINDFUL-PROCESS] Mindfulness for ruminative processes
**Move:** For thought PROCESSES — rumination, worry, obsessing, self-attack — where per-thought evaluation fails because the thoughts regenerate: explain the mechanism; list the claimed advantages of ruminating and test them; then deliberately start the rumination, run about five minutes of attention on the breath (notice wandering without self-criticism, return gently, let background thoughts fade unattended), re-rate the emotion, and draw the conclusion about controllability. Afterwards, about five minutes of formal practice each morning plus informal disengagement during the day by giving full attention to the current task; for a real problem that cannot be acted on now, name the feeling as legitimate, declare further chewing unhelpful, and move to a pre-listed valued activity.
**APPLY WHEN:** repetitive negative thinking; fear of one's own internal experience; a stuck problem that keeps pulling attention. / **AVOID WHEN:** practised only while calm — it will not transfer to distressed moments, a documented failure mode; used as thought suppression, which inverts the method; deliberately activating traumatic material to practise on; chronically refocusing away from a problem that is in fact solvable; worsening during practice in a trauma history — stop, do not push through.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck Chs. 16, 19; method-level mindfulness trials (Hofmann 2010; Chiesa & Serretti 2011; Segal 2018 for relapse prevention); the integration sequence is the author's practice.

### [CBT:ACCEPT] Three paths of acceptance
**Move:** Worksheet 10.3 offers three, combinable: observe thoughts and moods for a few minutes daily with curiosity, without judging or changing them, returning gently when hooked; weigh the bigger picture by naming what the distress-focus is hiding; or link the tolerated distress to a value you have chosen and act toward it while the distress is present.
**APPLY WHEN:** circumstances that cannot be solved or must be endured; recurring internal reactions; self-judgement about being anxious escalating the anxiety on the way into a situation you have chosen. / **AVOID WHEN:** used as resignation, as approval of what happened, or as positive substitution; replacing the solving of a solvable problem; expecting the observation practice to feel pleasant — boredom and frustration are part of it; applied to loss as a way of moving someone along — grief work has its own gate and no solo format.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** workbook Ch. 10; acceptance-informed CBT component at method level; author cases only for the specific three-path formulation.

## Anger, guilt, shame

### [CBT:ANGER-KIT] Anger strategy set
**Move:** Five parts: test angry thoughts with a record, checking specifically for personalisation and assumed intent, and gather information before reacting rather than after; watch for a recurring label attached to one person, which then harvests confirming misreadings; learn your personal early warning signs (jaw, fists, chest, volume, saying things that are not true) and treat them as the choice point; take a timeout from five minutes to twenty-four hours at those signs — regroup, settle the body, test the thought, and re-enter with a different strategy; rehearse predictable high-risk events in imagery including the version where it goes badly; and rate anger 0–100 before and after each strategy across trials, keeping the best one or two.
**APPLY WHEN:** frequent anger damaging close relationships, where unspoken expectations concentrate; one person consistently labelled and judged; a foreseeable trigger event. / **AVOID WHEN:** the anger flags a real ongoing injustice — then the task is a constructive response, not dissolving the signal; timeout used as avoidance without return, or as punitive silence; rehearsal sliding into grievance rumination; anger that stays confined to the family while self-help does nothing — that goes to couple or family work.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** workbook Ch. 15; standard anger-management components plus Beck-tradition restructuring, method-level; the five-part sequence is the authors' formulation.

### [CBT:ASSERT] Assertion
**Move:** The middle road between attacking and being run over: state reactions and needs as "I" statements rather than "you" accusations; acknowledge the true part of a complaint while holding your position; say what you want plainly instead of expecting it to be read; and judge success by the clarity of what you communicated, not by whether you got the outcome. Test the interfering rules ("if I refuse, I'll be disliked"; "asking is pointless") with a record or an experiment.
**APPLY WHEN:** anger that grew out of being taken advantage of; blow-ups after long passive over-accommodation. / **AVOID WHEN:** expecting each assertive act to produce compliance — assertion guarantees a clear message and nothing else; using it on someone with power over you without thinking about consequences first.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** workbook Ch. 15; assertiveness-training tradition at method level; the four-strategy formulation is the authors'.

### [CBT:GUILT-SHAME-PATH] Five components for guilt and shame
**Move:** Guilt usually needs one or two of these, shame may need all five. Assess seriousness on a personal 0–100 scale anchored by the worst thing you have ever done, plus perspective questions (would others rate it as serious; what would your closest friend say; how much will it matter in a month, a year, five years; did you know the consequences at the time; is the damage repairable; did you avoid something worse). Weigh responsibility with [CBT:RESP-PIE]. Make reparations: who was hurt, which of your own values was violated, then a scripted amends — acknowledge, say why it was wrong, apologise, offer repair, ask for forgiveness in time. Break the silence where secrecy is feeding it ([CBT:BREAK-SILENCE]). Finally self-forgiveness: work the prompts to a compassionate paragraph in the voice you would use for someone else, and move from "I did it because I am awful" to "I did it during an awful stretch of my life".
**APPLY WHEN:** should-and-ought self-judgement; guilt spread over many small things that needs calibrating; secrecy-fed shame; compassion that flows to others and never to you. / **AVOID WHEN:** talking yourself out of an ongoing violation instead of changing the behaviour — sometimes the guilt is accurate and the answer is amends plus change; amends made mainly to extract forgiveness, which the other person may never give; self-forgiveness used to skip warranted repair or confused with condoning or forgetting.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** workbook Ch. 15; authors' protocol built on reappraisal logic with compassion-focused parallels — no trials cited in the source.

### [CBT:BREAK-SILENCE] Breaking a shame secret
**Move:** Disclose the secret that feeds the shame to the most trusted person available, choosing a time and place with enough room to say all of it and to sit with the response; naming your fear about disclosing is allowed and often useful. The mechanism is that the predicted condemnation finally gets tested against reality and is usually not confirmed, which forces a re-reading of what the secret means.
**APPLY WHEN:** a long-held secret is maintaining shame, a genuinely safe confidant exists, and the expected rejection has never actually been tested. / **AVOID WHEN:** the confidant is unsafe or judgemental; there is no adequate time or place; the disclosure is aimed at, or pressured by, the person who caused the harm; the secret is trauma-rooted — sequence that in session, since disclosure is irreversible.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST / **Evidence:** workbook Ch. 15; authors' clinical mechanism, no trials cited. Non-traumatic secrets are the lower-stakes end, but the operation opens traumatic material by default, which is therapist-side under safety-protocol §3.

### [CBT:FORGIVE-LETTER] The unsent forgiveness letter
**Move:** Worksheet 15.4, eight prompts written uncensored and never sent: what the person did; what it did to you; how it still affects you; how your life improves if you forgive; a compassionate account of what shaped them; how you would want to be seen if you had hurt someone; how forgiveness could work; the qualities you have for moving on. Forgiveness here is for the writer and does not have to be communicated; the alternative is saying it directly in "I" statements, with conditions if you want them.
**APPLY WHEN:** long-carried anger about deep or repeated past hurt is eating into the present, and contact with the person is impossible or unwanted. / **AVOID WHEN:** the mistreatment is ongoing — then the task is getting clear that it is not your fault and building protection, not forgiving; you are not ready, since skipping and returning later is explicitly allowed; "forgiveness" is being used to push a premature reconciliation.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST / **Evidence:** workbook Worksheet 15.4; authors' technique, no trials cited. An uncensored account of deep or repeated hurt reactivates traumatic material, which is therapist-side under safety-protocol §3.

## Beliefs and self-concept

### [CBT:BELIEF-MAP] Mapping the pattern to its rules
**Move:** Write out three recent upsetting situations in the same columns — situation, automatic thought, emotion, behaviour, adding body sensations for intense anxiety — scan for the recurring theme, and hypothesise the underlying rule rather than assuming it from the situation type. Elicit rules by probing what the worst part would be if you did not do the usual thing, and by completing "if I even try X, then ___"; convert every rule into an if-then form before evaluating it. Alongside, fill the strengths side: what you were like and what you had before this started, and which behaviours have actually helped. Two routes to a belief-level statement if needed: completing "I am … / others are … / the world is …" while re-imagining a recent strong-mood situation, or the downward arrow, asking repeatedly what it would mean if the thought were true, until an absolute statement appears. Hold all of it as a hypothesis: beliefs are learned ideas, can feel completely true and still be false, and can be revised.
**APPLY WHEN:** the same reaction keeps recurring and needs a map; a behaviour pattern — avoiding, pleasing, perfecting — needs its internal logic named; surface work has been practised and moods persist. / **AVOID WHEN:** used as first-line work, since surface levels change faster and usually lift mood; digging solo at negative core beliefs or their childhood origins, where identification alone can trigger strong affect and a felt loss of safety — map behaviour and rules solo, take the rest to the session; choosing a traumatic memory as the situation to re-live; unrepresentative situations, which produce a map that actively misleads; treating a coping strategy as a pathology when the problem is its overuse.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** merged — Beck Chs. 3, 17 (conceptualisation diagram; sharing the formulation can itself help, Ezzamel et al. 2015) + workbook Ch. 12 identification routes; no diagram-specific trial; the workbook itself marks belief-level work as optional.

### [CBT:CB-STRENGTHEN] Build the companion belief, and rate on a continuum
**Move:** Do NOT weigh evidence for and against a negative core belief — the active belief filters the disconfirming data, and love gets re-read as pity. Instead word a new companion belief (opposite, qualified, reframed, or acceptance-shaped) and grow it: log small supporting events for weeks and months in a fixed record, deliberately writing items that feel too small to count, because the belief is the container that stops positives draining away; re-rate confidence 0–100 every few weeks; act it out in two or three behaviours, strangers before family, recording what the old belief predicted against what happened. For all-or-nothing self-judgement, replace the binary with a scale: draw a 0–100 line, populate it with progressively worse anchors, place yourself, and re-place yourself — a contained but imperfect outcome scores as partial control, not as failure. Work one sheet at a time and keep the mood measures running.
**APPLY WHEN:** a dominant negative self-belief has been identified; perfectionism and binary self-evaluation; as first aid in a bad period, re-reading the accumulated record. / **AVOID WHEN:** expecting to erase the old belief — a flexible pair is the goal; expecting speed, since this runs in months with flat stretches and mood usually turns near the halfway point of confidence; running a continuum with no feeling present, which leaves the change intellectual; reading pushback from people invested in your old role as proof the old belief was right; staring at the missing percentage.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** merged — workbook Ch. 12 (Padesky strengths-focused protocol; one case moved from near 0% to about 70% over a year) + Beck Ch. 18 continuum; clinical-consensus level inside an evidence-based frame, no component trial.

### [CBT:POSITIVE-DATA] Positive data, credit, gratitude, kindness
**Move:** Open every review with when you were at your best this week, re-experience that moment concretely, then extract the meaning — what does doing that, in this state, say about you. Keep a daily credit list of everything that was even slightly hard and got done anyway, and later convert it into an evidence chart for the adaptive belief, counting the invisible baseline that never gets counted although its absence would have counted against you. Name each positive feeling and re-imagine the situation as if it were happening now. Extend with the maintenance practices: a gratitude entry five minutes once a week for at least six weeks across three domains — the world, other people, yourself — a few items in depth rather than long lists, re-read weekly; voiced appreciation to others, including in writing that is never sent; and several weeks of small planned kind acts, logged and then reviewed for their effect on mood and on which positive beliefs they activated.
**APPLY WHEN:** every weekly review — depressed processing selectively drops positives; helpless or worthless beliefs discounting daily accomplishments; low positive affect; wanting a maintenance habit after recovery. / **AVOID WHEN:** used to wave away a real problem; the chart becoming a performance audit that feeds self-criticism; replacement beliefs inflated into absolutes rather than ordinary ("a normal person with strengths and weaknesses"); gratitude used to ignore a legitimate negative — you can be grateful and still send the cold meal back; elevated mood where realism may require correcting downward.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** merged — Beck Chs. 2, 6, 13, 17–18 (recovery-orientation layer, early-stage evidence; broaden-and-build research) + workbook Worksheets 12.10–12.15; the gratitude and kindness claims are the weakest-sourced part of either book — cited generically, without references.

### [CBT:CORE-BELIEF-MOD] Direct modification of a negative core belief
**Move:** Gate twice before touching it — is it worth it (drives significant distress, held strongly and broadly, blocks goals) and is now the time (cognitions are already held as ideas rather than truths, the affect of exposing the belief is tolerable, the relationship is trusted). Then the therapist-led kit: the full formulation including thought meanings and origins; downward arrow to the core meaning; motivation work first, weighing keeping the belief against changing it and imagining both futures; a historical review that re-reads each life period's "evidence" with written summaries read daily; point-counterpoint role play for the "I know it is false but it feels true" state; and belief-level questioning inside specific situations, deliberately more leading than at thought level.
**APPLY WHEN:** entrenched beliefs resist present-focused thought-level work, mid-course, with automatic-thought skills already in place and a solid alliance. / **AVOID WHEN:** early in the work, where premature belief evaluation reads as invalidation and risks intense emotion and dropout; solo, since even identification can trigger strong affect; origin review adjoining trauma with no containment; expecting the belief to reach zero — sufficiently weakened means behaviour keeps changing despite the remnant.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST / **Evidence:** Beck Chs. 2–3, 17–18; method canon (downward arrow, historical test; point-counterpoint from Young 1999); belief change is far slower in entrenched personality patterns than in acute states.

### [CBT:AFFECT-HEIGHTEN] Deliberate intensification of affect
**Move:** Raise negative affect on purpose in session — through imagery, somatic focus, or activating the pattern live — in order to reach the hot cognition, change the belief at the emotional and not only the verbal level, and learn experientially that the emotion is neither dangerous nor unbearable. The rationale is that cognitions shift in the presence of affect, and low in-session distress predicts shallow, head-level change.
**APPLY WHEN:** intellectualised processing or emotion avoidance is blocking progress; "I believe it in my head but not in my gut". / **AVOID WHEN:** solo, in any form; unassessed trauma history; no de-escalation plan and no one present to run it.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST / **Evidence:** Beck Chs. 13, 18 — method canon, no component trial; the state gate that precedes experiential work (stable sense of self, intact reality testing) is not self-administrable.

### [CBT:MEM-RESTRUCT] Experiential restructuring of early memories
**Move:** From a current trigger, heighten affect and bridge back to the earliest memory carrying the same theme; restructure its meaning both verbally (what you would have said to a child in that position) and experientially — role-playing the scene from each side, or the structured younger-self and older-self dialogue, re-experienced in present tense with age-appropriate rating language — until the conclusion lands that the belief was untrue and the child deserved protection; then convert it into compassionate self-talk in the week's plan. The gentler opposite direction — elaborating positive early memories to strengthen an adaptive belief — is SELF-safe and belongs with [CBT:POSITIVE-DATA].
**APPLY WHEN:** a core belief persists despite standard restructuring and traces to identifiable early experiences; middle or end of a course, therapist-led. / **AVOID WHEN:** solo, ever — this deliberately opens traumatic material; early in the work, before standard belief work has begun; dissociation risk; the author herself gates it as advanced and inapplicable to many people.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST / **Evidence:** Beck Appendix D, Ch. 18; Gestalt-derived and adapted to the cognitive model, supported by clinical experience only — no trial evidence cited for these specific techniques, and rescripting is therapist-performed even inside therapy (Arntz 2025 via PG26).

## Exposure-class operations

### [CBT:EXPOSURE] Formal exposure programme
**Move:** From the avoidance inventory, build a 0–100 hierarchy and enter around the 30% mark (the person chooses; occasionally someone starts at the top, which speeds things up); go in vivo, or first in five-senses imagery when in-vivo is impractical, staying until the feared outcome fails to occur — the working advance criterion is anxiety down by more than half or below 40 — then repeat until confident before moving up; identify and drop the safety behaviours, which are allowed at most as a first step; log predicted against actual anxiety and cross off the failed predictions; watch the post-exposure thought ("next time I won't cope"); extend duration deliberately to test the "if this continues, then…" prediction; tie steps to something you value; a helper may support early steps with independence as the end state.
**APPLY WHEN:** avoidance — including the subtle kind, like not making eye contact or not starting small talk — is what maintains the fear, and the programme has been designed with the therapist. / **AVOID WHEN:** launching solo, which both books hedge and PG26 rules out — hierarchy design and escalation are therapist operations and a failed exposure strengthens the fear; a step that produces no anxiety at all (too easy, or safety behaviours are running); trauma memories, grief, loss or rejection as the target; regulation skills used as escape hatches; a helper who has become a permanent safety behaviour.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST / **Evidence:** merged — Beck Ch. 19 + workbook Ch. 14; graded exposure is the best-supported intervention class for fear at method level (Craske et al. 2014; Salkovskis on safety behaviours) and holds at MODERATE in PG26, where equivalence between schools explicitly fails. Executing and logging a hierarchy agreed in session is SELF; designing or escalating it is not.

### [CBT:PANIC-INDUCE] Interoceptive experiments for a panic pattern
**Move:** For catastrophic misreading of body sensations, deliberately provoke the feared sensations — fast breathing for a few minutes, or vividly imagining the feared scenario start to finish — then watch them normalise after stopping (an induced heart rate settled within about ten minutes in the book's case), repeat daily, extend the duration to test the "if it keeps going" prediction, and then move into graded entry to the avoided real situations. Distraction works as a differential test: it reduces anxiety, and it does not reduce a heart attack.
**APPLY WHEN:** racing heart, dizziness or breathlessness read as catastrophe, where written records alone do not hold in the hot state — with a therapist designing the programme. / **AVOID WHEN:** no fresh medical clearance; solo launch, since the source itself depicts the therapist designing it and accompanying the first inductions.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST / **Evidence:** workbook Ch. 14; interoceptive exposure has method-level RCT support as a component and is explicitly therapist-guided in the source.

## The helping relationship and maintenance

### [CBT:FEEDBACK-REPAIR] Feedback and repair
**Move:** Watch for shifts in face, body and tone during the work and ask in the moment what just went through your mind. End every session by naming what bothered you, what was misunderstood, and what should be done differently next time — and whoever receives that feedback reinforces the negative parts first. On a complaint, direct or indirect, the first check is whether it is accurate: if yes, apologise properly and change the thing; if not, empathise, gather the data and test the thought together; then ask whether the same idea shows up in other relationships.
**APPLY WHEN:** any ongoing helping relationship, including with the AI layer itself; suspected unspoken negative reactions, which predict disengagement and dropping out; you may be the first person ever to ask. / **AVOID WHEN:** jumping to "that's their distortion" without the am-I-actually-wrong check; processing every small signal — an occasional sigh needs nothing; giving feedback and then judging on a single session rather than on the reaction to the feedback and the trajectory.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck Ch. 4; feedback collection rated demonstrably effective and rupture repair probably effective in the Norcross & Lambert review — but PG26 demotes the alliance correlation itself (r=.278) to LOW, so treat this as hygiene, not as the active ingredient.

### [CBT:RELAPSE-PLAN] Expected curve, warning signs, and the re-entry threshold
**Move:** As soon as improvement starts, draw the expected curve honestly: rising, with plateaus and setbacks — a setback is a detour, not a return to the start. Then, while well, rate every skill you learned on what you used, what helped, what you still use and what you would use again, marking which have become automatic; and write the plan: your personal high-risk situations; early warning signs across behaviour (withdrawing, putting things off, staying in bed), thoughts, mood and body, asking family or friends for the blind spots; a numeric threshold on your tracked score that triggers the plan and a lower one that stops it; and the actions — return to WRITTEN records rather than mental ones, because automatic skills fail first under relapse, schedule activities, use the process tools, name specific people to contact, and contact the therapist if that is not enough. Rehearse the plan in imagery and rate your confidence in it; low confidence in the plan means extend the plan, low confidence in a skill means practise it now, while well.
**APPLY WHEN:** the first weeks of improvement (the curve) and the end of structured work (the plan); anyone with a repeating history. / **AVOID WHEN:** postponed until the crisis, which is the whole point of writing it early; a generic plan — the warning signs and the named contacts must be personal; score-watching turning into anxious self-monitoring; thresholds used in place of judgement when severe symptoms appear; pathologising ordinary mood fluctuation instead of applying the four criteria (more severe, too long, too often, damaging life).
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** merged — Beck Ch. 21 + workbook Ch. 16 (Worksheets 16.1–16.2); relapse-prevention-focused work delays recurrence (de Jonge et al. 2019) and continued skill use protects under stressful events (Vittengl et al. 2019) — method level, both books untested as books.

### [CBT:SELF-THERAPY] Structured solo sessions
**Move:** Organise the notes into a synopsis of the skills learned, framed as lifelong tools; whenever mood improves, ask why and write down your own contribution alongside the external causes. Then run structured solo sessions on a fixed shape: review the week (positives and what they mean, credit due, unresolved problems, what happened to last week's practice), look forward (how you want next week to feel, goals, steps, obstacles), and choose the tools for it. Cadence monthly, then seasonal, then yearly, with an actual reminder; restart immediately at early warning signs.
**APPLY WHEN:** tapering or after ending structured work; any reaction out of proportion, or a pattern that keeps repeating. / **AVOID WHEN:** the interfering thoughts ("too much work", "I can't do this alone") are left unaddressed — the source is candid that many people never keep formal self-sessions going; notes hoarded and never re-read; using solo sessions to replace something that should be going to the live session.
**Tier:** SELF / **Evidence:** Beck Ch. 21; the structure is the author's practice, the continued-use benefit is Vittengl et al. 2019 at method level. Two indirectness layers apply throughout (clinical→subclinical populations, therapist→solo delivery): every solo application here operates outside the evidence base it cites.
