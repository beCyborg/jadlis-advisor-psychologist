# advisor-compassion — moves

Curated move set for the CFT/CMT lens, deduplicated across Gilbert (*Compassion Focused Therapy: Distinctive Features*, canon) and Irons & Beaumont (*The Compassionate Mind Workbook*). Tier is assigned per OPERATION per safety-protocol §3, not per school: observing, naming, regulating, skills training, psychoeducation, pattern mapping and non-traumatic behavioural experiments are SELF; anything that reactivates or reprocesses traumatic material, opens loss, or designs/escalates an exposure hierarchy is REQUIRES-THERAPIST — a routing instruction, not a refusal. Codes are unique; merged entries name both sources in Evidence.

## 1. Understanding the pattern

### [CFT:OLDNEW] Tricky-brain psychoeducation — why insight does not move feeling
**Move:** Teach the loop: old-brain threat motives plus new-brain imagination, rumination and self-identity generate self-sustaining distress with no external trigger; the threat system runs conditioned protective strategies and does not answer to argument, so knowing you are not to blame while still feeling blamed is a systems mismatch, not a personal failure. Walk the imagery chain — imagined food produces saliva, an external bully produces stress physiology, therefore self-attack is an internal bully firing your own threat system and compassionate imagery is an internal carer firing the soothing one. Trace one of the person's own loops through it.
**APPLY WHEN:** Early in any work; despair that understanding changes nothing; reading one's own reactions as proof of being defective; before introducing imagery.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used to dismiss rather than de-shame a feeling; sold as established neuroscience — it is a teaching simplification; it closes inquiry instead of opening a functional map.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Psychoeducation drawn from evolutionary and affective neuroscience; premise indirectly consistent with Longe et al. 2010 fMRI and imagery-physiology work; the de-shaming effect is clinical-experience level. Both sources (Gilbert Point 4; Irons Ch. 1–2).
### [CFT:FORMULATION] Three-systems profile plus five-column loop map, with the safeness check
**Move:** Three layers. (1) Profile threat, drive and soothing separately — triggers, frequency, duration, intensity 0–10, thoughts, urged behaviours, formative experiences — then draw the three circles sized by real dominance. (2) Map one recent episode as a loop: which new-brain content fed which old-brain emotion and back. (3) Five columns: background → key external fears (rejection, criticism, harm) and internal fears (unworthiness, being overwhelmed) → protective strategies (avoidance, submissiveness, suppression, striving, self-criticism-as-improvement) → unintended consequences → feedback arrows. Add the safeness check: is the calm produced by safeness or by safety-seeking — does isolation or appeasement prevent a feared outcome while also cutting off affiliative contact? Close by asking whether you would blame someone else who had lived this history.
**APPLY WHEN:** Entry assessment; a recurring difficulty with unclear origin; symptoms experienced as defects; before choosing any intervention target.
**AVOID WHEN:** Recall floods or dissociates — pause, stage it, take it to the therapist; used to prove the brain is broken or to bypass real current causes (poverty, an abusive relationship, a workplace keeping threat accurate); tick-box schema hunting or rumination about rumination.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Gilbert's four-domain framework built on CBT safety-behaviour work and attachment research; Irons Worksheets 3.1 and 4.1–4.5. Method-level at best; no trial of self-administered use.
### [CFT:NOTFAULT] Not chosen — still yours to train
**Move:** State plainly, about one specific strategy, that these motives and coping moves were built by evolution and unchosen circumstance, then hand back responsibility for training the mind now (the untended garden still grows, just tangled). Anchor with three reality checks: evolved mind (brains are tricky and unchosen), tragic mind (illness, loss and death are structural), social mind (you are one of many versions you could have been). Deliver slowly enough for it to land as felt, not as a slogan.
**APPLY WHEN:** Hot self-blame for coping that had clear survival logic; shame blocking engagement with any practice; a diagnosis story functioning as identity.
**AVOID WHEN:** Only the first half is delivered — no-fault without responsibility becomes a reason not to change; offered as consolation over a live and changeable problem; recited without felt connection.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Gilbert's clinical stance, including work with severe presentations; Irons Ch. 1–2 restates it. No component trial.

## 2. Building the base

### [CFT:SBR] Soothing rhythm breathing with embodiment layers
**Move:** Grounded posture (feet flat, spine long, chest open) plus a warm facial expression; breathe slightly slower, deeper and smoother than usual for about 5 minutes daily, starting at 1–2. Add layers as each stabilizes: pacing near 5–6 breaths per minute (roughly 5 seconds in, brief hold, 5 seconds out, brief hold), a stretched out-breath word in a warm inner voice, an external anchor (textured object, visual) if body focus is uncomfortable, a tested smell or sound. Frame bodily tension as protection to acknowledge, not badness to purge. Transfer in steps: alone → beside a comfortable person → safe conversation → live stressor. Precedes almost every other exercise.
**APPLY WHEN:** Core daily regulation; bridging threat into soothing; opener before imagery, letters or emotion work.
**AVOID WHEN:** Chasing a soothed feeling — the deliverable is physiological slowing and groundedness; breathing focus itself alarms (documented in chronic mental-health groups) — grade it or substitute another anchor; breathing-related health conditions.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level slow-paced-breathing physiology; the signature CFT protocol with no trial of its own (Gilbert's full version in *The Compassionate Mind* pp. 224–228; Irons Ch. 9). The facial-expression layer rests on weakly replicated facial-feedback findings — a cheap add-on, not an established mechanism.
### [CFT:MINDFUL] Anchored attention, open monitoring, and threat-mind noticing
**Move:** Start anchored, about 5 minutes: breath at one focal point, body scan around 30 seconds per region, or receptive sound; on wandering, notice without criticism and return — the noticing is the repetition, and frequent noticing means it is working. After weeks, add open monitoring: label what arises (worrying, anxiety, a memory) for distance without suppression. Generalize through one fully attended routine activity daily. Extend to daily threat-mind monitoring: track shifts into threat mode by attention, body, thought theme and action urge, and on noticing, refocus toward breathing and the compassionate pattern rather than arguing with the content.
**APPLY WHEN:** Rumination and worry loops; over-identification with thoughts; waking irritable or touchy after stress; foundation before all imagery work.
**AVOID WHEN:** Framed as emptying the mind or as a compliance test; breath or body focus activates threat — switch to sound or an external anchor; labelling becomes covert suppression; open monitoring used as a first practice; the threat state itself is shamed — post-stress threat mind is normal.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level mindfulness trial literature (Kabat-Zinn; Segal et al.); the sequencing and the threat-mind extension are the authors' clinical practice. Merged from both sources, where the code collided.
### [CFT:SAFE-PLACE] Safe-place imagery where the place itself welcomes you
**Move:** After breathing, build a real or imagined peaceful place, walking each sense for roughly 30 seconds; relax the body, allow a soft smile; the load-bearing detail is that the place is aware of you, welcomes you and wants you at ease — the target is the feeling of being wanted, not merely low arousal. Gentler first rung: a calming colour as light or mist carrying caring awareness. Amplify with real props — a matching photo, nature sounds, a tested smell, a grounding object, hands over the heart.
**APPLY WHEN:** First imagery exercise; building on-demand access to safeness; a lower rung before compassionate-self or compassionate-other work.
**AVOID WHEN:** Vividness is demanded — hazy, fleeting or non-visual imagery is normal and sufficient; the word "safe" itself pulls bad memories — rename it a peaceful place; high agitation (movement works better); used as escape from a problem that needs action.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Standard therapeutic imagery adapted by both sources; the welcoming-place element is theory-driven and untested separately.
### [CFT:COMP-SELF] Become the compassionate self (actor method) plus daily micro-doses
**Move:** Ground in breathing and posture, then, as an actor would, imagine BEING a deeply compassionate person, dwelling about 60 seconds on each quality in turn — wisdom (everyone arrives here with an unchosen brain and a learned history), strength (upright body, steady voice, calm authority), warmth and caring commitment, non-condemnation with the wish to help. Build the facial expression, tone and posture. Entry ramps: write why becoming compassionate matters to you; recall being kind to someone who was NOT in acute distress and reconstruct your own posture, voice and actions. Variants: picture the compassionate self in front of you and step into it. Micro-dose daily in found moments — waking, waiting, before a meeting.
**APPLY WHEN:** Daily base practice and prerequisite state for nearly every applied move; doubting you possess the qualities — the acting frame removes that requirement; first move for histories that cannot yet receive compassion from anyone else.
**AVOID WHEN:** Judged by whether feelings arrive — "am I doing it right" monitoring recruits threat; all qualities at once overwhelms (one per session); the entry memory centres on someone's acute distress; strong resistance is present — work [CFT:FBR-WORK] first; micro-doses fully replace longer practice.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Acting and identity techniques plus correlational identity-goal research (Crocker & Canevello 2008); physiological rationale from Rockliff 2008 and Longe 2010; both sources describe a growing correlational literature linking compassionate identity to lower self-criticism — no component trial. Merged (Gilbert Points 17–26; Irons Ch. 12).
### [CFT:PRACTICE-LADDER] Order the practices by how much threat they raise, and step down
**Move:** Rank the lens's practices from least to most threat-activating for YOU (illustratively: mindfulness → breathing → safe place → compassionate self → compassionate other), and when an exercise stirs too much, step DOWN a rung until regulated and then climb back rather than stopping or forcing. Redraw the rung order to fit your own experience; never leave the ladder entirely.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any exercise triggers overwhelm, grief or a block; all pacing decisions across the programme.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used to permanently avoid the harder rungs; used as a substitute for escalation when distress does not settle with grounding; forcing instead of grading — fears lose power through small tolerable steps.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** The authors' clinical heuristic (Irons Ch. 25); no trial. Consistent with the graded-exposure logic that carries the strongest method-level support in the tradition.

## 3. The three flows

### [CFT:FLOW-OUT] Compassion flowing out — well-wishing plus planned kind acts
**Move:** From the embodied compassionate self, direct wishes by name and in a warm tone at a being you care about: be well, be happy, be content, find the strength to bear what you face; picture them receiving it. Warm up by recalling a moment when you were kind to someone NOT in acute distress, a minute each on body expansion, the wish itself, voice tone, and the pleasure of being able to be kind. Advance a target ladder only when the current rung is easy: loved one → friend → stranger → groups → someone you dislike. Behavioural arm: pick from a kindness menu (greet someone, ask about their day, send a card to someone struggling, text a lapsed contact, give specific praise), plan it in writing, execute from the compassionate-self state, then replay or log it. One kind act for another and one for yourself daily; setting a boundary counts.
**APPLY WHEN:** Building caring motivation, which is usually easier than self-directed compassion — a faint glimmer counts; moving imagery into between-session behaviour.
**AVOID WHEN:** The kindness is approval-seeking — submissive niceness is a documented counterfeit that predicts feeling unappreciated; the target is someone you hold live anger toward, or whose suffering is severe — both hijack into threat; rungs are skipped; the recalled memory centres on the other's distress.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** The strongest trial evidence in the tradition, and all of it against passive comparators: loving-kindness (Fredrickson et al. 2008, 67 vs 72 waiting list, six weekly sessions), Lutz et al. 2008, Pace et al. 2008. Worksheets untested. Merged from both sources, where the code collided.
### [CFT:IDEAL-OTHER] Ideal compassionate other, the inner team, and training the receiving channel
**Move:** Write a specification for a perfect supporter — form (human, animal, natural), voice, expression, optional backstory in which its compassion grew through its own struggle — with deliberately superhuman qualities: unwavering commitment to you, wisdom that understands unchosen brains from the inside, strength that is not overwhelmed by your distress, warmth, acceptance plus commitment to help. Practise receiving about 60 seconds of its incoming care and hear it wish you well by name; it already knows what you are ashamed of and stays. Non-human first forms are common after neglect and often humanize over time; a compassionate colour entering at the heart is the gentlest entry when a personified image threatens. Team variant: as your compassionate self, meet it in your safe place and stand shoulder to shoulder. Train the real-world channel alongside: dwell about a minute on one memory of someone being kind to you at a time you were NOT in high distress — their face, their movement toward you, their words and tone, the feeling they had for you — and scan each day for small kindnesses you barely register. Asking a real person for help is itself a compassionate act toward yourself.
**APPLY WHEN:** Little lived experience of feeling cared for; high shame, where progress will be slow; a threat-busy mind that misses care signals; priming support before a hard task — receiving is the flow people most often skip.
**AVOID WHEN:** Receiving feels strongly aversive — that is blocks territory, slow down rather than force; the care memory comes from a period of struggle; grief for a lost carer surfaces — ground and change memory, and if it recurs or is trauma-linked, take it to the therapist; the image turns critical or vanishes and untreated fears dominate; treated as a substitute for real relationships rather than a bridge toward them; hostile images in complex histories.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Pilot data (Gilbert & Irons 2004) and a case series in voice-hearers (Mayhew & Gilbert 2008); felt-care-regulates-threat research cited generally (spousal hand-holding reducing threat-area activation under shock); no controlled trial. Merged (Gilbert's ideal image; Irons Ch. 13–14 and 16, including received-kindness noticing).
### [CFT:SELF-LKM] Self-directed well-wishing, mirror variant
**Move:** From the embodied compassionate self, visualize your everyday self — or a mildly stressed version, ordinary stressors only — and offer warm-toned wishes by your own name: be well, be happy, be safe, find the strength and courage to bear your difficulties. Strongest variant: say them at a mirror with a gentle smile; step down to a photo or a selfie camera if that is too much; extend into brief daily mirror pauses.
**APPLY WHEN:** Self-criticism and self-invalidation; being kinder to everyone else than to yourself; phrase-only practice feels flat.
**AVOID WHEN:** The distressed-self variant is aimed at trauma-linked material; the mirror overwhelms — take the step-down. Initial awkwardness is expected and is not a stop signal.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Best-evidenced single exercise in the lens: Petrocchi et al. three-arm study, where phrases plus mirror gazing produced the highest positive affect and significantly lower self-criticism — one study, not of these books, and not against an active treatment.

## 4. Self-criticism and shame

### [CFT:CRIT-WORK] Map the critic's function, externalize it, and convert its aim
**Move:** (1) Log self-critical episodes for about a week first — most people underestimate the rate. (2) Profile the critic on five attributes: focus, content (its actual words), form (inadequacy put-down vs disgust-based self-persecution), function (what do you fear would happen if you could never criticize yourself again — typically laziness, arrogance, loss of control), and origin, mapped not relived. (3) Externalize in about 5 minutes of imagery — size, face, voice, and above all the emotion it directs at you — and notice those are threat emotions, not care. (4) Engage from the compassionate self: wisdom (what is it trying to do for me), strength (step the image back if it overwhelms), caring (how could its concern be met more wisely). (5) Convert: run the two-teachers test — this critic teaching a struggling child you love, versus the teacher qualities you would actually choose — then set an intention of compassionate self-correction with the same improvement aims and a different delivery. Explicitly permit keeping the critic for now while the alternative is built; let comparison decide.
**APPLY WHEN:** Recurring self-attack after mistakes; the belief that self-criticism keeps standards up; fear of what happens without it; the start of any self-criticism work.
**AVOID WHEN:** The critic is trauma-derived — an abuser's face or voice appears, flooding or dissociation follows — that is therapist territory and a different protocol; the origin question drags into reliving; no real function exists (simple frustration), so do not over-interpret; permission to keep it is heard as endorsement of self-attack.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** The two forms and two functions of self-criticism are published questionnaire research (Gilbert et al. 2004a; the Forms of Self-Criticising/Attacking and Self-Reassuring Scale); the mapping, externalization and conversion protocol is clinical practice. Merged (Gilbert's critic map; Irons Ch. 24).
### [CFT:SELFCORR] Compassionate self-correction in the moment
**Move:** On catching self-attack after a mistake, name the threat emotions inside it — anger, contempt, disappointment aimed at yourself — and then re-issue the correction from care: what specifically to do differently, forward-looking, in the tone you would use with someone you were responsible for, anchored in actually wanting to do this well. Apply the same stance during tasks: pace, permission to stop, encouragement instead of driving yourself with threat.
**APPLY WHEN:** Mistakes, setbacks, performance review; tasks finished but followed by rising self-attack; the belief that self-compassion lowers standards.
**AVOID WHEN:** The correction is dropped altogether — this is not self-excuse; the "compassionate" voice is contempt in different wording — check the felt emotion, not the phrasing; it turns into blaming whoever taught you the critical voice, which is just new rumination.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Gilbert Point 13 and its Table 2 contrast — the author's model, untrialled as a switch; converges with evidence that the emotional tone rather than the content of self-criticism does the damage (Whelton & Greenberg 2005).
### [CFT:SHAME-WORK] Sort the shame, then tolerate it from strength
**Move:** First sort: split an episode into external shame (what you believe now exists about you in other people's minds) and internal shame (how you judge yourself), naming the key fear under each; classify the emotion by attention focus, rumination content and action urge — hide and appease, counterattack, or repair — to distinguish shame, humiliation and guilt. Where real harm was done, move deliberately to guilt: behaviour-focused, repair-motivated, bearable without self-condemnation. Then work it: embody the compassionate self and at first only tolerate the feeling from groundedness; apply wisdom by naming shame as an evolved social-threat response tracking how you exist in others' minds, whose concealment urge is design rather than verdict; follow with a letter or thought record. Counter the withdrawal pull explicitly — hiding cuts you off from the very care that resolves shame.
**APPLY WHEN:** Post-rejection or post-failure rumination that mixes "what they think" with "what I am"; identifiable shame about body, failure, role or a past event; shame-driven concealment and avoidance.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used to argue yourself out of legitimate guilt, or to aim at shamelessness — brief shame has prosocial functions; the shame is rooted in abuse or relational trauma — specialist and therapist territory; tolerance collapses — step down the ladder rather than pushing; the childhood-root line reactivates trauma, in which case stop at naming.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** The external/internal distinction has psychometric support and the shame–guilt–humiliation separation rests on self-conscious-emotion research (Tangney & Dearing 2002); the shame–trauma link is cited at method level; the worksheets and the tolerate-then-wisdom sequence are clinical protocol. Merged (Gilbert Points 10–11; Irons Ch. 24).

## 5. Applied skills — thinking, attention, emotion, parts

### [CFT:THREC] Compassionate thought record
**Move:** Column one: trigger event, the upsetting thoughts and images, the emotions. Then EMBODY the compassionate self before writing anything, and answer in four sub-blocks — empathy and validation (this is understandable given what happened and what you learned; it is not your fault), compassionate attention (what balancing facts are being filtered out), compassionate thinking (what you would say to a friend, what is common to people here, the threat system is active so the thinking is biased, thoughts are events rather than facts), and compassionate behaviour (one supported action). Finish by re-reading it slowly in a warm voice and noting what shifted. Make it portable: flash card or phone note by the bed, the fridge, the mirror.
**APPLY WHEN:** A specific recent difficulty keeps generating threat thoughts; best completed when an emotion actually moves.
**AVOID WHEN:** The embodiment step is skipped — without it this degrades into cold disputation, the documented failure of rational work in high shame; used to erase sadness rather than stop self-attack; the target is trauma-linked material.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Thought records are core CBT with method-level support; the compassionate variant (Irons Worksheet 19.2) has no direct trial.
### [CFT:WARMREAD] Two-pass warm re-reading of alternatives you already believe
**Move:** Generate alternative or balanced thoughts — validation, common humanity, specific past successes, treating help-seeking as a compassionate act — and rate how helpful each feels 1–10. Then one minute of soothing breathing plus one minute of compassionate-self embodiment, and re-read exactly the same sentences slowly with as much warmth as you can produce, attending to felt tone rather than logic. Re-rate and compare. Diagnostic question: does the alternative currently sound cold, analytical or irritated in your head?
**APPLY WHEN:** Alternatives feel true but hollow; rational reframing produces no emotional shift.
**AVOID WHEN:** Content-tinkering replaces the state shift — the engaged affect system is the active ingredient; warmth itself triggers dread, grief or contempt, in which case the fear of compassion is the actual work.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Gilbert's demonstration exercise, untested standalone; the premise that the active emotion system changes the impact of identical cognition is consistent with Brewin's retrieval-competition account.
### [CFT:ATT-FUNC] Rebalance attention, then read what it is trying to resolve
**Move:** Rebalance: after a threat-captured episode, upright posture plus about 30 seconds of breathing, connect to the compassionate self, then deliberately hold in detailed attention what went well, positive feedback received, past coping, times you were valued — resting on the feelings rather than listing facts. Function-read: if the same content keeps returning, ask from the compassionate self what the mind is trying to resolve — which threat emotion, fear or unmet need sits behind the pull. In the worked case a recurring humiliation memory turned out to signal unexpressed protest and led to assertiveness practice.
**APPLY WHEN:** Post-event rumination after talks, meetings or social evenings; redirection only partly works and the content recurs; suspected avoidance-by-distraction.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used to deny a real problem that needs action — this is bias correction, explicitly not positive thinking; the recurring content is a trauma memory that floods.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Clinical practice consistent with method-level CBT attention training; no trials cited by the source.
### [CFT:EMO-ENGAGE] Validate, then tolerate the avoided emotion
**Move:** Work the four regulation skills — detect, name, understand, tolerate. Validate first from the compassionate self: given this situation and this learning history, the feeling is an understandable human response — and never follow that with a reversal. If you cannot validate, validate the invalidation (it makes sense you dismiss feelings, given how you were taught), or route through how you would validate a friend. Then tolerate: breathing and a friendly expression for about a minute, embody the compassionate self with emphasis on strength, bring up the avoided emotion and its most recent trigger, and stay in its presence from groundedness; if it overwhelms, return to the breath and re-approach. Optionally picture the struggling version of you and offer it care.
**APPLY WHEN:** "I should not feel this" self-talk; emotions avoided out of fear of being overwhelmed; a missing emotion — no access to anger and therefore no ability to assert — since absence is data, not health.
**AVOID WHEN:** The emotion is welded to trauma memories, dissociation appears, or overwhelm does not settle with grounding; validation is used to justify harmful acting-out — validate the feeling, not every behaviour; validation slides into pressure to change the feeling.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Clinical practice; the graded affect-tolerance logic is shared with method-level exposure and emotion-focused work; no direct trial. Merged (Gilbert's validation formula, Point 15; Irons Ch. 20).
### [CFT:MULTISELF] Meet the parts, then address them from the compassionate self
**Move:** Pick a recent, deliberately LOW-activation episode. Step into the angry, then the anxious, then the sad part in turn, breathing between them, noting for each its thoughts, body, what it would do if fully in control, and what outcome it wants; then answer how each part views the others. Return to the compassionate self, run the same prompts from that stance, and address each part — what it understands about it, what it would say, how it would help — so each is heard while its grip loosens. Applied variant for a single state: from the compassionate self, picture the anxious or self-critical version of you in front of you, observe its face and rushing feelings, surround it with compassion and watch the image change; for self-criticism, find the fear behind the critic and aim compassion at that fear. Recall it in the moment when the state recurs.
**APPLY WHEN:** Mixed or contradictory reactions to one event; one emotion chronically running the show; a blocked part (often sadness) — hard access is learning-history data, not failure; everyday anxiety or self-criticism when some compassionate-self practice already exists.
**AVOID WHEN:** The chosen example is still highly activating; trauma memories surface through the memories prompt; the critical part is allowed to vent at length — the aim is strengthening the compassionate pattern, not exposure to the critic; you are pulled in and cannot re-anchor; the parts framing hardens into new self-labels or is taken literally, which is dangerous where reality testing or sense of self is unstable.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core clinical exercise in both sources (Gilbert's compassion-focused parts work, whose roughly ten hypothesized mentalizing mechanisms the author says still await research; Irons Worksheet 23.1); no component trial.

## 6. Behaviour, drive and voice

### [CFT:APPROACH] Map avoidance and take single graded steps toward ordinary situations
**Move:** Map your avoidances, safety strategies and feared outcomes; from the compassionate self validate both the fear and the cost, then ask what would help you tolerate rather than avoid. Take one small, repeated step toward an ordinary feared situation, staying until the anxiety eases, crediting the attempt rather than the outcome. For predictable challenges use the three-phase plan: before (skills, information, expect the bias), during (breathing, embodied posture, slow down), after (debrief with someone, savour or tolerate, extract the learning, block the rumination).
**APPLY WHEN:** Life narrowed by avoidance of ordinary, non-traumatic situations; procrastination on a change you already know helps; shame about avoiding blocking the change itself.
**AVOID WHEN:** The avoidance is trauma-rooted, panic-level or keeps flooding; the plan becomes a designed fear hierarchy with escalation rules — designing and escalating a hierarchy is a therapist operation, see [CFT:EXPOSURE-PROG]; validation stops at accepting the avoidance; the before-phase becomes perfectionist over-preparation.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Graded exposure has the strongest method-level base of anything in this lens; the compassionate wrapper and the three-phase plan are clinical adaptation (Irons Ch. 21). Tier follows the operation rule: executing an ordinary non-traumatic step is self-work, designing or escalating a hierarchy is not.
### [CFT:ASSERT] Compassionate assertiveness and emotional expression
**Move:** Six steps: identify where you cannot assert; find what sits behind it (fear of being selfish, fear of rejection, a copied family pattern); bring compassion to that struggle rather than to the failure; script from the compassionate self — I need or I feel, this matters because, I would like you to, and an invitation to discuss; plan when and with whom; handle the aftermath with a thought record or letter. Rehearse aloud, at a mirror, in imagery, or with a safe person first.
**APPLY WHEN:** Chronic yes-saying, unrecognized needs, accumulating resentment, the hoard-then-erupt pattern.
**AVOID WHEN:** It tips into the aggressive pole — demands, blame, threats; instant acceptance is expected, since people unused to you expressing anything may react with confusion or threat; the relationship is currently unsafe, which is a therapist and safety question, not a scripting problem.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Assertiveness training has an old method-level base; the compassionate protocol is clinical adaptation (Irons Ch. 21).
### [CFT:DRIVE-VALUES] Rebuild drive from soothing, not from threat
**Move:** Enter drive counter-clockwise — regulate first, then act. Three tools plus a rebalancing arm. Values: for five domains (work, family, friends, personal development, self-care) write what you actually value, rate it 0–10, and list one short-term and one long-term move; values are a compass, goals are items to strike off, and value-congruent action still works in low mood. Best possible self: about 5 minutes imagining a realistic good future, then at least 10 minutes writing it. Three good things: nightly for at least a week, three events plus what led to each, relived in detail. Rebalancing arm: schedule non-achievement fun and deliberately pointless pleasure, treat discomfort with contentment as something to desensitize to, and separate enjoying-having from must-have and value-based from validation-seeking achievement.
**APPLY WHEN:** Underactive drive, anhedonic narrowing, fuzzy direction after low mood; perfectionism where success brings only brief relief; striving welded to identity; aloneness under the striving — treat the connection deficit, not only the striving.
**AVOID WHEN:** Already over-striving, where doing more is counter-indicated — build soothing first and audit which activities are fear-driven; gratitude is used to paper over real pain; framed as abandoning ambition, which discredits the work — behavioural activation stays; severe fear of positive emotion — grade it first.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Values work, best-possible-self and gratitude interventions have method-level support with modest effects; behavioural-experiment methodology is method-level CBT; the achievement-motivation distinctions are correlational (Dykman; Dunkley; Gilbert et al. 2007; Crocker & Canevello 2008; the authors' own striving-to-avoid-inferiority findings). Merged (Gilbert's behavioural experiments against threat-based striving; Irons Ch. 11).
### [CFT:LETTER] Compassionate letter writing
**Move:** Ten steps, written FROM the embodied compassionate self: embody first; hold the intention; greet yourself by name and name the difficulty; validate and empathize, including where the pattern was learned; frame the coping strategies and their costs as understandable rather than your fault; shift to responsibility; give compassionate thoughts and concrete actions; anticipate blocks and setbacks; close with commitment and companionship; re-read slowly in a warm voice, or record it and listen. Audit against the eight qualities — genuine concern, sensitivity, being moved, helping you face the feeling, reflective understanding, non-judgement, warmth throughout, and orientation toward what to do next — and revise. Second person is allowed if first person is harder.
**APPLY WHEN:** Integrating every skill channel around one persistent struggle; assimilating a difficult experience; processing after an event; setbacks in behaviour or assertiveness work.
**AVOID WHEN:** The critical voice hijacks mid-letter — stop, re-embody, resume, and monitor which part is writing; early letters go unedited, since tone drifts preachy, advice-listy or critical; it becomes unstructured venting during active rumination-driven distress; perfectionism about wording.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Rides on the expressive-writing paradigm (Pennebaker 1997) — method-level, direction only, and not CFT-specific; the tone-steering and the ten steps are the authors'. Merged from both sources.

## 7. Blocks, setbacks, maintenance

### [CFT:FBR-WORK] Work the fears, blocks and resistances — including the absence of feeling
**Move:** When compassion practice produces resistance, aversion or nothing at all, treat that as the work rather than as an obstacle to push through. Start from your associations to the word compassion; optionally baseline with the Fears of Compassion questionnaire, whose three subscales mirror the three flows, and inspect the highest-endorsed items rather than the total. Classify each block: fear (wanting it but dreading what it would open — grief, rage), block (circumstances such as time or workload), resistance (I do not deserve it, usually a masked fear). For each: validate it; trace where it was learned; recheck it against the definition of compassion as sensitivity plus commitment, which requires strength and courage; ask how you would answer a loved one who held this belief; for undeserving, identify which system is speaking — threat's fear or drive's earn-it-first — and run it as a sceptical experiment, practising anyway and judging by results. When no warm feeling arrives, normalize it: soothing may be underdeveloped by history, suppressed by threat or blunted by low mood, so redirect the target from feelings to intentions and motives, on the fitness analogy that the body responds to training before it feels different. Micro-dose entry: one kind act or 1–2 minutes daily. Proceed only with your own explicit agreement.
**APPLY WHEN:** Resistance, flatness, scorn or dread during any compassion practice — before adding practice hours; undeserving thoughts, which are expected in high shame; compassion feels alien, which is a learnable-language problem.
**AVOID WHEN:** Used to override reluctance rather than understand it; the tracing turns into reliving relational-trauma memories rather than naming the pattern; the flatness is actually dissociation from material that has opened, or conditioned trauma is surfacing; blocks keep flooding despite self-paced steps — escalate instead.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Established CFT theory with method-level standing; the Fears of Compassion scale is a validated instrument with no clinical cut-offs, whose three subscales correlate with stress, anxiety and depression — self-directed most strongly — all correlational. Case material is clinical experience. Merged (Gilbert's fear-of-compassion work; Irons Ch. 7 and 25).
### [CFT:SETBACK-SEQ] Eight-step protocol for an acute non-traumatic setback
**Move:** In order: (1) validate — given what happened, this feeling is an understandable human response, with no reversal attached; (2) tolerate the emotion instead of fighting it; (3) look behind the self-criticism to the fears and sadnesses under it and direct compassion there; (4) soothing breathing; (5) refocus on genuinely helpful material — past coping, people who support you; (6) imagine the compassionate self handling this; (7) counter all-or-nothing thinking while acknowledging its pull, rehearsing the alternative in a kind inner voice; (8) choose one supportive short-term and one long-term action.
**APPLY WHEN:** Acute setbacks with a self-critical aftermath; distress being dismissed or minimized, by others or by you; a failed or aborted attempt at a practice.
**AVOID WHEN:** The setback tears open trauma material; validation slides into pathologizing or into pressure to feel differently; the deeper extensions — chair work, deep letters — are attempted solo; repeated avoidance is relabelled as setbacks without changing the plan.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Gilbert's composite sequence — components have method-level CBT support, the sequence itself is untested; the validation step is shared with alliance literature (Leahy, Linehan). Irons Ch. 21 gives the short in-the-moment version.
### [CFT:SETBACK-PLAN] Forward maintenance plan and soothing kit
**Move:** From the embodied compassionate self, write out in advance the situations where a setback is likely, the early warning signs, the prevention steps and the recovery steps, and put the key points on a card or in your phone. Assemble a soothing kit — around six personal soothing triggers in one accessible place. Plan practice separately for good periods and hard periods, on the assumption that these skills decondition like muscles.
**APPLY WHEN:** Finishing a block of work; entering a known high-risk period; after a course of practice has started to lapse.
**AVOID WHEN:** The plan replaces practice; it becomes another perfectionist project; it is written during acute distress instead of from a regulated state; it substitutes for escalation when warning signs are already present.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Relapse-prevention analogue (Irons Worksheets 26.2–26.3); clinical experience, no trial.

## 8. Therapist-tier operations — route these, do not run them

### [CFT:BASEFIRST] Sequence and state gate before any experiential work on aversive material
**Move:** Establish a working compassionate self and a usable inner compassion base BEFORE engaging trauma memory, avoided emotions or feared material, and approach that material from the base as the stance rather than as a preliminary relaxation trick. Before experiential work of any kind, the readiness gate — a stable sense of self and intact reality testing — is checked in session, not by the person alone, and the therapist titrates by felt safety throughout.
**APPLY WHEN:** Aversive memories or emotions will eventually have to be faced and some access to soothing already exists; imagery, chair work, parts contact or exposure is being proposed.
**AVOID WHEN:** Attempted solo; the compassion base itself still fires threat — that fear comes first, see [CFT:FBR-WORK]; the gate is skipped because the person feels ready, which is exactly when self-assessment is least reliable.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Gilbert's clinical sequencing claim, labelled by the author as clinical rather than trial-tested; the state gate itself is a normative guardrail from the guidelines layer, which states it cannot be self-administered.
### [CFT:MEMWORK] Shame-memory work: decompose, re-engage, re-script
**Move:** After the compassionate self is established, decompose the shame memory into its conditioned components — the trigger affect, the other person's attack, the verbal labels injected, others withdrawing, the submissive body defence, the aloneness afterwards, the entrapment — and intervene per component rather than disputing a summary belief. Then staged re-engagement: then-versus-now body contrast; imagery re-scripting with new elements such as helpers, support to the remembered self and different endings, with the compassionate self in control throughout; assertiveness toward the perpetrator in imagery; unsent letters; desensitizing the fear of one's own rage. Pick one target element at a time — powerlessness, aloneness, entrapment, betrayal. Compassion toward the perpetrator comes much later, if at all, and never first.
**APPLY WHEN:** Trauma-linked shame memories with intrusions or hyperarousal maintain the current threat response, and belief-level disputation has already failed.
**AVOID WHEN:** Solo, always; pacing beyond what the person has agreed to; no compassionate-self grounding in place.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Imagery re-scripting has method-level trial support outside CFT (Brewin et al. 2009; Wheatley et al. 2007; Lee 2005); the conditioning account and the CFT sequencing are the author's protocol. The guidelines layer places early re-scripting with the therapist even inside therapy.
### [CFT:COMP-CHAIR] Asymmetric compassionate chair work, with the abuser-voice gate
**Move:** Unlike balanced two-chair dialogue, the aim is to strengthen the compassion chair. The person sits in a part's chair, recalls a mild instance of anger, anxiety or self-criticism and voices its feelings and body sensations; then physically moves to a facing chair, rebuilds breathing and the compassionate self, and only from there engages the part — what threat drives it, what the compassionate self would say, how the part would feel if fully helped. If pulled into the part's affect, break contact and rebuild before re-engaging. Mandatory gate before befriending any critic: screen whether it is felt as the internalized voice of a past abuser, and if so mark it not-self and handle it differently — assertive confrontation, inviting it to leave, empty chair, or trauma re-scripting, by the person's preference.
**APPLY WHEN:** Entrenched self-attack where the felt relationship to the critic has to change; the person accepts the parts framing; starting mild and scaling to capacity.
**AVOID WHEN:** Solo — dialogue with vulnerable parts can open trauma and dissociation; before any compassionate-self capacity exists, since the critic simply wins; the part repeatedly overwhelms and cannot be re-anchored. The chair-work authors themselves contraindicate it at risk of retraumatization, self-harm or suicidality.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Emotion-focused chair work with process-experiential research (Whelton & Greenberg 2005) is method-level; the CFT variant and the abuser-voice gate are untested separately. Immediate post-session overwhelm is documented in a substantial minority of chair-work sessions even in therapy.
### [CFT:POSEXP] Graded exposure to warmth, care and closeness
**Move:** Treat fear of warmth and closeness as an exposure target in its own right: approach affiliative feeling in graded steps, surface the beliefs attached to it — if you really knew me you would leave, needing anyone is weakness — and work through the grief and resistance that surface, because positive affiliative affect needs desensitizing just as threat does when closeness historically predicted shame, rejection or dependency.
**APPLY WHEN:** The person is dismissive of, contemptuous toward or frightened by kindness from themselves or others; compassion practice keeps firing threat rather than settling.
**AVOID WHEN:** Attempted solo — it opens attachment loss, abuse memory and dependency fear; used as a way to push past reluctance rather than with explicit agreement.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Gilbert's clinical experience — the model's originating case — physiologically consistent with Rockliff et al. 2008; never separately trialled.
### [CFT:EXPOSURE-PROG] Formal graded exposure programme on hard targets
**Move:** Run the full sequence as a programme: imaginal rehearsal first — embody the compassionate self, imagine engaging the easiest step while carrying wisdom, strength and commitment, re-ground on threat spikes, end in the safe place — then in vivo, repeating each real step until anxiety eases and advancing rung by rung over weeks with the compassionate mind online before each step. Hierarchy design and any escalation are session decisions; between sessions you execute and log the agreed steps, never raise the difficulty.
**APPLY WHEN:** The avoidance is trauma-rooted, panic-level or unsupported, and the work runs WITH the therapist using the book as a shared protocol.
**AVOID WHEN:** The target is a traumatic memory itself, which is specialist work; imaginal rehearsal indefinitely replaces real-life action; run solo on anything that keeps flooding — small everyday steps belong to [CFT:APPROACH] instead; the person escalates the hierarchy alone, since failed solo exposure reinforces the fear.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Graded imaginal and in-vivo exposure carry the strongest method-level trial support in this lens; the tier reflects both the workbook's own routing of trauma-linked execution to therapy and the guidelines rule that hierarchy design and escalation are therapist operations.
### [CFT:GRIEF-WORK] Grief that compassion work uncovers
**Move:** Compassion practice commonly awakens grief — for the care that never came as well as for what was lost — and Gilbert argues, explicitly against the view that grieving the past is unhelpful, that the capacity to grieve it is often necessary. The operation itself is not a self-help one: measurement comes first (prolonged-grief symptoms plus a depression screen), and only confirmed elevated symptoms lead to therapist-led loss exposure with a restorative component, individually rather than in a group, over months. Solo work stops at noticing that grief has surfaced, naming it, and taking it to the session.
**APPLY WHEN:** Compassion work triggers deep sadness and yearning; grief surfaces during imagery of a lost carer; unfinished grief comes up as a theme — measurement is the entry condition, not the theme.
**AVOID WHEN:** Solo, in any form; early-stage overwhelm or dissociation; screening scores are unremarkable — at ordinary grief these protocols have near-zero effect and can do harm; the response offered is any kind of reframe, silver lining or growth narrative; what is present is perseverative frustrated self-pity rather than grief.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST (the measurement step is SELF)
**Evidence:** Explicitly weak — the author states good evidence is lacking and offers clinical reports and a small uncontrolled pilot (Gilbert & Procter 2006). The guidelines layer adds that no solo format exists, that entry requires elevated symptoms, and that no grief modality has shown superiority (Hao 2024 network meta-analysis: five modalities, overlapping intervals).
