# Grief Counseling and Grief Therapy — digest

## What this book is for the lens

The field's clinical canon: mourning as ACTIVE work organized in four tasks, with seven mediators explaining why the same loss produces wildly different grief. Two structural gifts: (1) the counseling/therapy boundary — facilitating normal grief needs no profession, resolving blocked grief needs real training, most bereaved need neither; (2) built-in targeting logic — screen for risk, help the indicated minority, leave the resilient alone — matching the lens's evidence stance better than any other grief book. It beats popular grief literature by refusing stage models and refusing to pathologize pain intensity. Terminology: grief = the reaction, mourning = the adaptation process, bereavement = the loss itself.

## Core model

- **Attachment base (Ch.1)**: grief is an attachment-system response (Bowlby); the aim is *adaptation*, not "recovery". Normal grief spans 4 categories — feelings, physical sensations, cognitions, behaviors (normal-grief inventory) — all normal unless extreme in intensity AND duration.
- **Four Tasks of Mourning (Ch.2)**: I accept the reality of the loss (intellectually AND emotionally); II process the pain (anger, guilt, anxiety, loneliness — not only sadness); III adjust to a world without the deceased (external roles/skills, internal self-definition, spiritual assumptive world); IV find an enduring connection with the deceased while embarking on the rest of life (continuing bonds, not decathexis). NOT a sequence — revisited, reworked, parallel. Failure modes: I denial/mummification; II not-feeling, substances, "geographic cure"; III promoted helplessness; IV "not living".
- **Alternate models kept as tools (Ch.2)**: Stroebe & Schut's Dual Process Model — oscillation between loss- and restoration-orientation IS the adaptive mechanism; Rubin's Two-Track Model — assess functioning and the bond separately (TTBQ).
- **Seven Mediators of Mourning (Ch.3)**: (1) kinship (strongest predictor), (2) nature of the attachment (security, ambivalence, dependency, abuse history), (3) how the person died (NASH; sudden/violent/stigmatized/ambiguous; preventability), (4) prior losses and depression, (5) personality (coping style, attachment style, rumination, ego strength), (6) social variables (support SATISFACTION, not availability; disenfranchised grief), (7) concurrent stresses. Mourning is multidetermined — single-factor explanations are systematically wrong.
- **Counseling vs therapy (Ch.4 vs Ch.6)**: counseling facilitates the tasks in uncomplicated recent grief; therapy resolves separation conflicts blocking the tasks. "Finished" = tasks addressed; under 1 year is suspect, ~2 years typical for stability, grief recurs to be reworked.
- **Complicated mourning (Ch.5)**: continuum with normal grief in intensity/duration, NOT a distinct symptom set. Four shapes: chronic, delayed, exaggerated (grief escalates into a DSM disorder, loss link known), masked (facsimile illness/behavior, loss link unknown). Diagnose conservatively; Lazare's 12 clues.
- **Special losses (Ch.7)**: suicide ("unspeakable" — shame/guilt/rejection), sudden/violent death (trauma before grief), SIDS/miscarriage/stillbirth/abortion (disenfranchised — make the unreal loss real), anticipatory grief (work of worry), HIV/AIDS (stigma + contagion guilt).
- **Family systems (Ch.8)**: the family grieves as a unit — role vacancy, emotional integration, permission to feel; child outcome rides on the surviving parent's functioning (Harvard Child Bereavement Study); elderly = interdependence, overload, emotional loneliness.
- **The helper (Ch.9–10)**: map your own loss history (15 sentence stems) and the client types you can't work with (they track your unresolved conflicts — refer); cap concurrent attachments, grieve each death actively, arrange support in advance; grief-sketch role-play training; volunteers only after their own grief has some resolution.

## Moves

### [GR:RISK-SCREEN] Target help at the indicated minority
**Move:** Do not counsel every bereaved person. Screen early (~4 weeks) against risk profiles — Parkes & Weiss's 8 dimensions (young children at home, low SES, high anger/pining/self-reproach, few relationships, judged poor coping), Raphael's predictors (perceived nonsupport, traumatic circumstances, ambivalent marriage, concurrent crisis), high initial distress — and route only high scorers or self-referrals at felt difficulty to help, customized to the mediators.
**APPLY WHEN:** Deciding whether help is indicated after any significant death; judging whether one's own state warrants counseling.
**AVOID WHEN:** Treating a composite as a checklist to fully match; reading an ICG score as a predictor (it's a snapshot); routine referral of all bereaved — no benefit shown (Parkes 1998).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Prospective cohorts (Parkes & Weiss 1983; Raphael 1977; Worden 1996) + meta-analytic reviews (Schut 2010; Larson & Hoyt 2007); converges exactly with indicated-only d≈0.53.
### [GR:DOSE] Dose the pain — oscillation, not immersion
**Move:** Approach the painful feeling as intensely as tolerable, then deliberately back off before re-engaging; alternate loss-oriented grief work with restoration-oriented life tasks. Dosed avoidance is part of the mechanism, not cheating.
**APPLY WHEN:** Fear of being overwhelmed; white-knuckle constant confrontation; between-session self-management.
**AVOID WHEN:** Dosing becomes permanent avoidance with no return trips; "pure grief work at all times" pressure.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Dual Process Model (Stroebe & Schut 1999/2010) — the book's best externally supported regulation principle.
### [GR:COPING-AUDIT] Audit coping style, especially substances
**Move:** Map current coping once trust exists; always ask about alcohol/drug use ("for sleep" escalation). Steer from avoidant coping (blame, persistent distraction, denial, withdrawal, substances — worst outcomes) toward active emotional coping (reframing, humor, venting that includes positive affect, accepting support); problem-solving is trainable.
**APPLY WHEN:** Escalating drinking, refusal to keep any reminders, total withdrawal.
**AVOID WHEN:** Confronting before trust exists; treating short-term relief as proof a strategy works.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Systematic review (Stahl & Schulz 2014); avoidant coping predicts PTSD/complicated grief (Schnider 2007) — observational.
### [GR:RUMINATE-REDIRECT] Break the rumination loop
**Move:** For passive looping on grief symptoms: train problem-solving focus, rebuild social contact in ways that don't repel supporters, and re-anchor the bond to the deceased in something other than pain — pain must stop being the last tie to them.
**APPLY WHEN:** Chronic passive looping; "the pain is my last tie to them"; circular meaning-search.
**AVOID WHEN:** Mislabeling normal early preoccupation as rumination.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Rumination harms are solid observational research (Nolen-Hoeksema; Eisma 2015 — ruminators find LESS meaning); counter-moves are the author's recommendations.
### [GR:GUILT-TEST] Reality-test irrational guilt
**Move:** Spell the accusation out ("I should have taken him to the hospital sooner"), then check it against what was actually knowable and doable at the time; enumerate "what did you do? what else?" until the person concludes they did what was possible. Most grief guilt is irrational and collapses under this.
**APPLY WHEN:** "If only" loops; guilt fixed on death circumstances; self-blame.
**AVOID WHEN:** Guilt survives reality testing (real culpability) — see [GR:CHAIR] forgiveness work; guilt woven into traumatic imagery.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Author's technique; targeting justified by Stroebe 2014 (longitudinal: self-blame, not regret, strongest determinant of grief outcome).
### [GR:SUICIDE-ASK] Ask the suicide question directly
**Move:** Routinely ask, plainly: "Has it been bad enough that you've thought of hurting yourself?" Retroflected anger and the wish to rejoin the deceased both occur in normal grief; asking does not implant the idea.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any acute grief contact — self-check or supporting someone.
**AVOID WHEN:** Never avoid asking; but a positive answer requires professional escalation, not solo handling. Keep lethal drug quantities away from acute mourners.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Standard clinical safety practice; author's clinical experience.
### [GR:CHILD-NEEDS] Bereaved children: needs checklist + the parent lever
**Move:** Cover the child-needs list: assurance of care ("who will take care of me" — answer even unasked), absolution from magical-thinking self-blame, clear age-appropriate facts (incl. non-contagion), involvement, stable routines (play is coping, not indifference), respectful answers to repeated questions, ways to remember (family memory book). Offer the funeral as a coached choice. Above all: invest in the surviving parent's functioning — the strongest predictor of the child's outcome.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any child bereaved of a parent or sibling; designing help after a parental death.
**AVOID WHEN:** Euphemisms and information vacuum (children fill blanks with scarier stories); forced funeral attendance; child-only intervention around a struggling parent; blanket programs instead of screening.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Harvard Child Bereavement Study (125 children, 2-year controlled follow-up): ~80% cope, parent functioning = strongest predictor; parent-targeting programs (Sandler 2010/2016).
### [GR:JOIN-GROUP] Loss-matched peer support group
**Move:** Join a bereavement group matched to the loss type — homogeneous, or with at least one other member of the same loss type (a lone widower among widows becomes the group deviant; a lone suicide survivor raises whole-group anxiety). Networks: The Compassionate Friends, SIDS network, survivors-of-suicide groups, Parents of Murdered Children.
**APPLY WHEN:** ≥6 weeks post-loss; isolation, disenfranchised loss, "nobody who hasn't been through this can understand".
**AVOID WHEN:** <6 weeks post-loss; serious pathology (individual work instead); overwhelming multiple losses; as a substitute for indicated therapy at elevated symptoms.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Program experience (Hospice of Pasadena); mixed trials for complicated-grief groups (Piper 2009; Johnsen 2012); one RCT for AIDS-bereavement groups (Sikkema 2006).
### [GR:LETTER-WRITE] Write to the deceased
**Move:** Write letters to the deceased saying the unsaid, including a farewell letter; keep a grief journal; build a coherent narrative of the event. Online memorial pages serve the same expressive/continuing-bond function.
**APPLY WHEN:** Unfinished business, unexpressed feelings; disenfranchised losses without ritual access.
**AVOID WHEN:** The death is traumatic and writing floods rather than integrates — escalate instead of pushing; memorial pages replacing live support entirely.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level writing-therapy trials (O'Connor 2003; Ihrmark 2011); memorial-page use descriptive only.
### [GR:ROLE-SKILLS] Rebuild the roles the deceased carried
**Move:** Inventory the roles the deceased filled (decision-maker, home administrator, emotional caretaker, sexual partner), pick the concrete problems those gaps create, and train the missing skills; counter helplessness by reviewing how one managed before/without the deceased (mastery reframe). Meanwhile postpone irreversible decisions — selling the home, relocating, career change, a rushed next pregnancy (replacement-child risk) — framed "when you're ready", not "you can't". Elderly: allow a transitional dependency period first.
**APPLY WHEN:** Task-III floundering; "I can't survive alone"; decisions driven by pain-avoidance; over-dependence on adult children.
**AVOID WHEN:** Doing tasks for the person; substituting problem-solving for emotional processing when avoidance is the real pattern; delay-framing that breeds helplessness or blocks necessary decisions.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Mastery as component (Onrust 2007); elderly skills intervention (Caserta, Lund, & Obray 2004); relocation/replacement-child harms observational (Lopata 1996; Legg & Sherick 1976).
### [GR:MEANING] Meaning work — only if the search is already running
**Move:** If a meaning search is spontaneously present and stalling, work the questions explicitly ("What does my life look like now? Who am I after this death?") through narrative telling; support channels like memorials, scholarships, advocacy tied to the manner of death; include benefit-finding and competence gained; revisit at life stages.
**APPLY WHEN:** Spontaneous meaning-search present; senseless-feeling deaths; shattered assumptive world.
**AVOID WHEN:** No spontaneous search — do NOT initiate: 20–30% adapt well without meaning-making; pushing growth-meaning early invalidates the loss (Gamino & Sewell 2004); premature positivity that skips task-II pain.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Neimeyer's framework + Davis 2000 (correlational; searchers who fail adjust worst); reframing linked to best outcomes in Harvard study (observational).
### [GR:NORM-MAP] Normalize against the normal-grief inventory
**Move:** Map reactions onto the 4-category inventory and label them normal: hallucinations of the deceased, sense of presence, distractibility, numbness, dreams, relief, anger — "feeling crazy" ≠ going crazy. Pre-educate: long wave-like course, no return to a pre-grief state, support collapses by ~3 months, family members legitimately grieve differently. Plan support for critical dates (3 months, first anniversary, holidays — rehearse them).
**APPLY WHEN:** "Am I losing my mind?" fears; first significant loss; family policing one member's grieving style; hard dates approaching.
**AVOID WHEN:** Reactions extreme in intensity AND persisting late — a complication signal, not material for normalizing; decompensation risk (prior psychosis, borderline organization).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Lindemann 1944 descriptive symptomatology; Demi & Miles 1987 Delphi; Harvard observations; author's practice.
### [GR:TASK-MAP] Reframe mourning as tasks; find the stuck one
**Move:** Replace "phases I must sit through" with "tasks I can work on" — the antidote to helplessness ("time heals" only via what you do with the time). When grief is stuck, locate WHICH task is blocked: I → what makes the death unbelievable; II → what feeling is unfelt; III → which adjustment is refused; IV → what blocks remembering-while-reinvesting.
**APPLY WHEN:** Passivity, waiting for time to heal; any stalled grief, as the organizing assessment.
**AVOID WHEN:** Turned into a schedule ("I should be done with task II by now"); resolving task-II/IV conflicts on loaded material solo — that is therapy work.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** The book's core framework; face-valid, no direct trial of the model itself.
### [GR:MEDIATOR-MAP] Profile the loss against the seven mediators
**Move:** Run a specific loss through all seven mediators (who died; attachment quality; death circumstances; loss/psychiatric history; personality and coping; support satisfaction; concurrent stressors) to anticipate the course and flag risk. The same grid diagnoses WHY grieving fails.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any significant loss; deciding whether help is indicated; grief that isn't moving.
**AVOID WHEN:** Letting one salient mediator (e.g., suddenness) carry the whole explanation; reading high pain as pathology by itself.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Framework synthesized from cohort studies (Harvard, Cleiren 1993, Bonanno 2002) — predictive-observational.
### [GR:ACTUALIZE] Make the death real
**Move:** Task-I work: retell the death in detail (where, how, who told you, the funeral) repeatedly; use hard reality words ("died", not "lost"; past tense); see the body when offered the CHOICE (informed, never coerced); use the funeral deliberately (personalized service, honest mixed feelings); visit the grave when timed well; for missed burials or perinatal losses build tangibility — name the baby, hold a memorial, collect mementos, use the autopsy report to reality-test the cause.
**APPLY WHEN:** Unreality/disbelief; sudden death; body not seen; ritual-less losses (miscarriage, stillbirth).
**AVOID WHEN:** Pathologizing normal early belief–disbelief alternation; forcing retelling on someone actively flooded; coercing viewing or rituals either way; delusional denial (psychiatric care, not ritual).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Author's clinical experience + ritual literature (Hoy 2013; Kristensen 2012); perinatal practices method-level (Brier 2008; one RCT — Pruitt Johnson & Langford 2015).
### [GR:RELOCATE-BOND] Remember the deceased AND license new life
**Move:** Task-IV work: address fear of forgetting head-on — plan concrete remembrance (objects, rituals, inherited traits, memory book); then explicitly license reinvestment — new people fill the void without replacing or dishonoring. Ask the completion question: "What would you LOSE by giving up your grief?" and build memorializing routes for grief's real functions.
**APPLY WHEN:** New attachments blocked by loyalty; fear the memory will fade; grief apparently held onto.
**AVOID WHEN:** The opposite pattern — rapid replacement (quick remarriage can abort grief and add a divorce-loss); pressure to "move on" before pain work has traction.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Continuing-bonds literature (Klass et al.; Field 1999: possessions→memories shift = healthier bond) + author's procedures (Powers & Wampold 1994).
### [GR:WORRY-WORK] Anticipatory grief: legitimize the work of worry
**Move:** Before an expected death: interpret role rehearsal ("where will I live") as adaptive coping — to the rehearser and to relatives who read it as cold; block reassurances that shut it down. Prompt unfinished business while the person lives: express appreciation AND disappointment, with explicit permission. Name distance distortions: premature withdrawal (goodbyes said, patient lives on → resentment/guilt) and ambivalence-driven over-closeness.
**APPLY WHEN:** Terminal illness in the family; caregiver guilt; family judging a member's planning talk.
**AVOID WHEN:** Pushing rehearsal on someone not doing it spontaneously; forcing confrontations that burden the dying; promising forewarning means easier bereavement (unsettled).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Janis 1958 analogue; pre-loss communication predicts outcomes (Metzger & Gray 2008); clinical observation (Weisman & Hackett 1961).
### [GR:UNRESOLVED-CLUES] Screen old losses with Lazare's 12 clues
**Move:** Take a structured loss history, then screen against Lazare's clues: fresh intense grief when speaking of an old loss; outsized reactions to minor or others' losses; recurring loss themes; possessions frozen in place or all tossed at once; physical symptoms like the deceased's (anniversary- or age-timed); radical lifestyle cut-offs; long subclinical depression with guilt; compulsive imitation; self-destructive impulses; same-time-each-year sadness; illness phobia tied to the deceased's disease; missed rituals/support at the time.
**APPLY WHEN:** Vague somatic complaints or stuck patterns possibly grief-driven; delayed grief surfacing via a lesser loss or media.
**AVOID WHEN:** Treating any single clue as diagnostic — one is never sufficient; judging possession-keeping without cultural context; forcing disclosure in acute crisis.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Lazare's clinical taxonomy (1979/1989), author-endorsed; not validated as an instrument.
### [GR:MOOD-SCREEN] Distinguish grief from depression; escalate when indicated
**Move:** Check against depression markers: persistent GLOBAL loss of self-worth (grief guilt is loss-specific; "in grief the world feels empty, in depression the self does"), generalized culpability, symptoms persisting through year 1, or grief escalating into a full disorder (MDE, panic, PTSD, substance abuse — "exaggerated grief"). If present → professional evaluation; treat the disorder first, then the separation conflicts (medication lifts depression but cannot resolve attachment conflicts).
**APPLY WHEN:** "Depression-like" state not lifting; prior depression history; comorbidity is common.
**AVOID WHEN:** Early transient sadness, insomnia, appetite loss — normal grief, do not medicalize; antidepressants for normal acute grief (see debunks).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** DSM-5 guidance + Zisook and Jacobs cohort studies (method-level); author's differential.
### [GR:TRAUMA-FIRST] Sequence trauma work before grief work
**Move:** After violent/traumatic death (homicide, suicide discovery, mass violence, witnessed death): moderate posttraumatic symptoms FIRST — intrusions, avoidance, and hyperarousal impair the reflective work of mourning. Open with supportive resiliency-building; only later work the mourning mediators. Acute phase: firm concrete help ("we need to call the funeral home"), plain death words paired with logistics, lend ego strength briefly then restore the survivor's own efficacy.
**APPLY WHEN:** PTSD signs after a traumatic loss; death-scene imagery monopolizing memory; numb survivors in the first hours/days.
**AVOID WHEN:** Leading with grief excavation in an overwhelmed survivor; prolonged takeover that builds dependence; tough words without the support half.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Method-level consensus (Parkes 1993; Rando 1993; Rynearson's homicide-bereavement program); crisis-intervention tradition since Lindemann 1944.
### [GR:CONTRACT] Time-limited grief-therapy contract
**Move:** For complicated grief: 8–10 weekly sessions with an explicit contract focused on the loss; intake screening for comorbid depression, anxiety, trauma, personality disorder; off-topic drift treated as resistance — named and returned to the contract. Mechanism: experience the avoided thoughts and feelings, with the therapist supplying the missing social support and permission to grieve.
**APPLY WHEN:** Focused unresolved grief (chronic/delayed); person self-labels "this isn't ending" (typically 2–5 years out).
**AVOID WHEN:** Serious underlying pathology emerges — longer non-grief treatment overrides; dependent personality structures need more than brief work.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Author's practice; Shear's 16-session complicated grief treatment is the trialed method-level analogue.
### [GR:FAMILY-WORK] Treat the family as the grieving unit
**Move:** Meet survivors individually AND as a family at least once — live interaction reveals role vacancies (beware "you're the man of the house now" to a teenager), alliances, scapegoating (displaced anger, often onto a vulnerable child). Take a 2-generation loss history (unresolved grief transmits). Have the family tell the story of the deceased together, building joint meaning without forcing consensus; operational mourning (Paul & Grosser) induces the response in one member in conjoint session.
**APPLY WHEN:** Post-death family friction; child death (blame risk, grieving-style mismatch between partners); silent or myth-holding families.
**AVOID WHEN:** Visibly siding with the more expressive partner; forcing a consensus narrative; assigning roles to children; operational mourning outside conjoint family therapy.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Bowen systems + Nadeau 1998; Kissane 2006 — family intervention effective for a SCREENED at-risk subset (fits indicated-only); Sandler's Family Bereavement Program.
### [GR:SUICIDE-WORK] Suicide bereavement package
**Move:** Postvention early, before family myths set ("died by suicide", not "committed"). Reality-test guilt and blame (search for evidence the survivor DID reach out; normalize relief after chronic pre-death crises); correct denial with direct words; explore the imagined death scene (for non-witnesses imagination is often WORSE than reality); rebuild the split image ("super dad WHO had clinical depression"); permit rage while the survivor controls its expression; reality-test the abandonment; support the unanswerable "why" (the medical model of depression helps some); plan for real future fears; monitor survivors' own suicide risk — postvention IS prevention.
**APPLY WHEN:** Any suicide loss; family accident-myths; scapegoating; sons fearing their own suicidal fate.
**AVOID WHEN:** Solo excavation of culpability or death imagery; venting rage without containment; assuming the family will fall apart (some grow closer).
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Shneidman/Jordan postvention tradition; author's cases; Mitchell 2003 group study; "suicide grief inherently more intense" is contested (Farberow vs Cleiren).
### [GR:RESTORATIVE-RETELL] Defuse traumatic death imagery
**Move:** When the death scene monopolizes memory of the person: restorative retelling + imagery exposure with systematic desensitization on the worst images; EMDR as an alternative on intrusive discovery images — so peak affect subsides and positive memories become reclaimable.
**APPLY WHEN:** Intrusive death-scene imagery (witnessed or imagined) blocks access to any positive memory.
**AVOID WHEN:** Any solo attempt; before stabilization (see [GR:TRAUMA-FIRST]); untrained provider.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Rynearson's restorative retelling (program literature); EMDR small studies (Sprang 2001; Solomon & Rando 2007) — modest.
### [GR:AMBIV-GRADE] Graded memory work for ambivalent bonds
**Move:** Revive memories in graded order — extensive positive memories first as ballast, then mixed, then hurt/anger ("What do you miss? What don't you miss? How did she disappoint you?" — never "are you angry?" head-on); reverse if the patient arrives all-negative; always rebalance so one valence doesn't erase the other. With "bigger than life" idealization, allow it early, then surface ambivalence; establish that anger does not cancel love. Tailor to attachment style: preoccupied → internalized psychological proximity; dismissing → watch for somatic expression; fearful → protect against isolation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Idealization with suspected suppressed anger; ambivalent or dependent bonds; unfinished relationship business.
**AVOID WHEN:** Jumping to negative material without the positive groundwork; surfacing anger without resolving it — retroflected anger can leave the patient WORSE (depression); typing attachment style from one behavior.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Author's method + attachment-bereavement theory (Mikulincer & Shaver; Field 2006); tailoring not trial-tested.
### [GR:CHAIR] Empty chair / psychodrama with the deceased
**Move:** The high-leverage mechanism of grief therapy: talking TO the deceased in the present tense beats talking ABOUT them. Empty chair or eyes-closed imaginal conversation; power increases by switching chairs and speaking FOR the deceased; a favorite photograph can anchor the address. For real (reality-tested) guilt: a seeking-and-granting-of-forgiveness exchange via two-chair or group psychodrama, ending in reconciliation.
**APPLY WHEN:** Unfinished business, guilt, regrets, blocked finality; chronic grief stuck for years; genuine culpability that survived reality testing.
**AVOID WHEN:** Schizophrenic and borderline patients — explicitly contraindicated; solo use on a loaded loss; untrained facilitator; irrational guilt (use [GR:GUILT-TEST]).
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Gestalt tradition (Polster & Polster) + empirical paradigm support (Field & Horowitz 1998) + author's extensive case work; no RCT.
### [GR:MULTI-LOSS] Grieve stacked losses one at a time
**Move:** In bereavement overload (several deaths in one event or short window): grieve each loss separately, starting with the relationship carrying the FEWEST complicating factors, then progressively harder ones — jump-starts a frozen "too much to feel" state.
**APPLY WHEN:** Multiple deaths; frozen affect; cumulative losses masquerading as anxiety.
**AVOID WHEN:** Attempting all losses at once; starting with the most traumatic loss; solo work on overwhelming loss stacks.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Author's clinical practice (case reports); overload concept from Kastenbaum 1969.
### [GR:LINK-OBJECT] Work the linking object
**Move:** Distinguish keepsakes from linking objects — invested items whose absence triggers panic (belongings, sense-extensions, representations, objects present at death-news). Bring the object to sessions and examine what conflict it externalizes; expect spontaneous putting-away as work completes (the Harvard boy's cap: worn year 1, bedpost year 2, closet after — normal titration).
**APPLY WHEN:** Object must be carried at all times; panic when it's missing; mummified rooms.
**AVOID WHEN:** Confiscating or pushing disposal — movement must come from the patient; judging possession-keeping without cultural/religious context.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Volkan's clinical concept + author's cases; Field 1999 (possessions→memories shift) — observational.

## What the book debunks

- **Stage models, including Kübler-Ross applied to mourning**: Worden's own critique — stages invite literalism, fixed order, and disappointment at "skipped" stages (cites Maciejewski 2007); he replaces them with tasks. Fully aligned with the lens prohibition list.
- **Universal grief counseling**: routine referral of all bereaved shows NO benefit (Parkes 1998); most bereavement is self-limiting; a substantial resilient group (Bonanno) needs nothing. The mirror myth — "counseling doesn't work / harms" — is also overstated: weak studies plus a statistically invalid deterioration analysis (Larson & Hoyt 2007). It works for screened, indicated, self-referred mourners.
- **"Everyone must do grief work / meaning-making"**: 20–30% adapt well with no meaning search; constant confrontation is not the adaptive pattern (DPM); absence of visible affect is NOT automatically pathology.
- **Venting as progress**: unfocused vehement emotion predicts WORSE outcomes a year on (Parkes 2001); feelings need targets and dosing. Venting ≠ integration.
- **"Time heals"**: only what one does with the time heals; fixed deadlines (4 weeks, 4 months, 1 year) set mourners up for self-blame.
- **Antidepressants for normal acute grief**: usually inadvisable — slow, rarely touch normal grief, may theoretically pave the way to abnormal grief; medicate only established disorders (MDE); antianxiety agents acceptable for anxiety/insomnia.
- **Platitudes**: "I know how you feel", "be brave", "you've still got your kids", "you can have another child" — collusion with denial and caregiver self-soothing; "you will survive this" is the only truthful reassurance.
- **Gender myths**: "women fare better because of more support" refuted (Stroebe 1999); intervention response is gender-CROSSED — men gained more from affect-focused, women from problem-focused work (Schut 1997): train the non-dominant style. Fathers do grieve — alone, in cars, at graves.
- **Misc corrections**: divorce after child death not conclusively elevated; "bereaved parents don't want to talk about their child" is backwards; children CAN mourn (with a child-shaped model); anniversary reactions normal for 10+ years; relief after abortion ≠ absence of grief; expected stigma-rejection (AIDS, suicide) often fails to materialize — concealment itself becomes the toll.
- **Live controversies flagged honestly**: delayed grief exists (Worden: 8%) vs doesn't (Bonanno: 4%); forewarning helps (Parkes) vs no effect (Nielsen 2016 systematic review).

## Numbers that matter

- 10–15% of bereaved develop complicated mourning needing more than facilitation — the lens's indicated population.
- Indicated interventions d≈0.53 at elevated symptoms; universal prevention ≈ zero (evidence context; the book's targeting logic converges on it).
- 20–30% function well with no meaning search; of searchers, fewer than half find meaning at 1+ year; searchers who fail adjust worst (Davis 2000).
- Self-blame (not regret) = strongest determinant of grief outcome, grief-specific (Stroebe 2014) — why [GR:GUILT-TEST] is load-bearing.
- Timeline: ~3 months for widows to accept the death as real; support collapses by ~3 months (cliff at 6–12 months); fewer than half of widows feel like themselves at 1 year; ~2 years to a "modicum of stability" (Shuchter & Zisook); some take 3–4 years (Parkes).
- Harvard Child Bereavement Study (125 children, 70 families, 2-yr controls): ~80% cope well; surviving-parent functioning = strongest predictor; ages 5–7 most vulnerable; 66% still "felt watched" by the dead parent at 2 years (normal continuing bond).
- Delayed grief frequency: 4% (Bonanno, 18 months) vs 8% (Worden, 2 years, same instruments).
- >40% of bereaved spouses have an anxiety-disorder episode in year 1 (Jacobs 1990).
- Formats: grief-therapy contract 8–10 weekly sessions; Shear's trialed treatment 16 sessions; groups 8×90 min, 8–10 members, entry ≥6 weeks post-loss; SUDS 0–10 for affect tracking.
- Suicide: ~750,000 newly bereaved per year (US); notes in only ~18% of deaths.
- Screening: Parkes & Weiss 8 risk dimensions at 4 weeks; Lazare 12 clues; ICG = snapshot, not predictor.

## Blind spots of this book

- **Evidence base is mostly the author's clinical experience**: the four-task model, the chair work, most named techniques have no direct trials — method canon, not outcome evidence; where trials exist they are method-level analogues (Shear, writing therapy). The lens must not present Worden's moves as trial-proven.
- **Self-confirming data loop**: the empirical spine is Worden's own Harvard studies; his closing "grief therapy works, and its effectiveness is visible" is author confidence — calibrate to indicated-only d≈0.53.
- **Resilience under-weighted**: Bonanno's challenge (large resilient majority, delayed grief mostly absent) is cited then argued around rather than integrated; grief work remains the default frame.
- **Dated diagnostic edge**: written pre-ICD-11/DSM-5-TR Prolonged Grief Disorder (PCBD-era terminology); medication guidance is old consensus level.
- **Anglo-American individual-clinic frame**: culture appears as a mediator and a caution ("no knowledge about grief is culture-free"), but the moves assume Western rituals, office dyads, verbal processing; non-Western mourning gets anecdotes, not method.
- **No dismantling**: which component of any package (retelling? chair? homework?) carries the effect is never isolated; technique choice runs on clinical judgment.
- **Counselor-facing by design**: moves address the helper; the lens must invert perspective for self-use, and that inversion is untested — a reason the REQUIRES-THERAPIST tier here is strict.
