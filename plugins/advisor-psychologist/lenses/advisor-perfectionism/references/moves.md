# advisor-perfectionism — moves

Curated move set for the perfectionism lens (citation prefix `[PERF:]`). Source: the Shafran/Egan/Wade workbook digest, deduplicated by operation. Tier is set per OPERATION against safety-protocol §3, not per school; where the workbook bundles two operations of different tier under one section, the entry is split and the codes disambiguated (`[PERF:IMG-FUTURE]` vs `[PERF:IMG-RESCRIPT-MEM]`).

## A. Deciding whether this lens applies at all
### [PERF:SCREEN9] Nine-marker fit screen (Worksheet 1.1)
**Move:** Answer nine markers — relentless striving; attention fixed on what is still unachieved; other people calling the standards too high; fear of failing them; raising the bar after meeting it; self-worth resting on striving and achievement; repeated checking of your own performance; persisting although opportunities are lost; delay or avoidance driven by fear of failing or by how long doing it properly would take. Reading rule: a yes on the self-worth marker (Q6) plus a majority of the rest means the target is worth-contingent perfectionism rather than high standards.
**APPLY WHEN:** deciding whether this lens is the right one before spending weeks on it; separating high standards with intact self-worth from worth-contingency.
**AVOID WHEN:** read as a diagnosis, or turned into one more arena to score well in.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Component of the self-help package tested in ≥4 direct trials of this book; no standalone psychometrics — a fit screen, not an instrument.
### [PERF:COMORBID-TRIAGE] Route what the workbook will not carry (Boxes 2.1, 2.2, 2.4, 2.5)
**Move:** Before starting, walk the symptom checklists and route: five or more depressive symptoms present daily for over two weeks → a mood-specific resource or a clinician; any thought that life is not worth living → stop the frame and use crisis contacts immediately; body weight below BMI 17.5 or regular vomiting → clinician; rituals running over an hour a day with impairment → ritual-specific treatment in parallel. Sequencing logic (Fairburn's house of cards): where perfectionism is the supporting card, working on it lets the dependent problems fall away — but only where it really is the supporting card.
**APPLY WHEN:** before the programme starts, and again whenever mood, eating or ritual load escalates mid-programme.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to attach a label rather than to route; or skipped because the checklists feel discouraging — this is the workbook's own entry gate.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Author clinical guidance; the treat-perfectionism-first logic is supported by the book's own trials showing transfer to anxiety, mood and eating symptoms. Crisis routing is normative, not measured [PG26:CRISIS-STOP].
### [PERF:CYCLE-MAP] Personal maintenance diagram (Worksheet 5.1)
**Move:** Build your own version of the maintenance cycle through eight prompts: whether self-worth is contingent on achievement; the standards themselves; the rules that serve them; an all-or-nothing reading of a recent near-miss; the self-criticism that follows, in its actual wording; the counterproductive behaviours and their triggers; where anxiety, low mood and stress feed in; and what happens when you DO meet a standard — how briefly the satisfaction lasts, how it gets discounted, how the bar moves. Draw the arrows, including the loop from success back to a raised standard.
**APPLY WHEN:** at the start, before any change technique; again whenever motivation drops and the thread of why this persists is lost.
**AVOID WHEN:** the diagram becomes a perfectionist artefact — hunting for the correct arrows; a rough, example-rich sketch outperforms a tidy one and is meant to be revised.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** First step of the protocol in every trial format; case formulation is method-level standard CBT, never isolated as a component.
### [PERF:AREA-MONITOR] Area map plus one week of live self-monitoring (Worksheets 7.1.1, 7.1.2)
**Move:** Circle which of the 19 listed life areas apply (eating, shape, weight, social performance, checking, ordering, organisation, cleanliness, appearance, hygiene, artistic, musical, sporting, academic, work, intimate relationships, parenting, health and fitness, entertaining) and write the typical thought and typical behaviour for each. Then log for one week in four columns, at the moment it happens: area and situation; the thought with a 0–100% belief rating; the behaviour; the feelings rated 0–100%. Keep the sheet to hand, set a daily review alarm, then look for repeating areas and the thought → behaviour → bad-feeling chain.
**APPLY WHEN:** always, before any change technique, and again later as the change measure; especially when perfectionism is assumed to live in one domain only.
**AVOID WHEN:** recorded from memory days later (markedly weaker), rewritten to look good, or made exhaustive; if fear of what the records will show blocks starting, that fear is the first belief to test, not a reason to skip.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core component of the trial-tested package; self-monitoring has method-level support across anxiety, mood and eating problems, and recording itself reduces the behaviour.

## B. Motivation and ambivalence
### [PERF:MOTIV-AUDIT] Rulers, four-cell balance and the one-year projection (Worksheets 6.1, 6.2, 6.3)
**Move:** Rate separately, 0–10, how important changing perfectionism is and how confident you are that you can. For each, ask why the number is not zero (surfaces existing motivation) and what would move it up. Then fill all four cells — advantages and disadvantages of changing, advantages and disadvantages of NOT changing — writing the perceived benefits of perfectionism out in full instead of arguing them away. Close by projecting one year ahead twice, with the perfectionism in place and without it, across roughly a dozen life areas, and naming both what most scares you about reducing it and the most compelling reason to.
**APPLY WHEN:** at the start; when motivation stalls mid-programme; when the person says they want to change but keeps not starting; when the fear is that dropping perfectionism means dropping standards.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to talk yourself into a higher number; the "advantages of not changing" cell left blank (hidden ambivalence collapses the plan later); the projection turning into open-ended rumination; if importance stays very low, the book itself says this may be the wrong time to start.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Motivational components of the trial-tested package (merged from Worksheet 6.1 rulers and Worksheets 6.2–6.3 decisional balance); rulers and decisional balance have method-level support outside this book, neither trialled standalone here.

## C. Testing the standard: facts, other people, experiments
### [PERF:FACTCHECK] Check the fact under the rule, then measure your own effort curve (Section 7.2)
**Move:** Identify the factual claim a rule rests on and check it against reputable sources — universities, respected national bodies, not scaremongering or opinion sites — then bring the corrected fact back against the rule. Because there is no universal formula for how much effort is right, build a personal one empirically: log effort invested, outcome obtained and task importance, and derive task-specific "enough" levels by trial and error. Anchors: the effort–performance relation is an inverted U (Yerkes–Dodson, 1908) and most perfectionists sit past its peak, where effort can be cut with no performance loss; high perfectionism predicts lower efficiency; a harsh reaction to mistakes damages performance more than under-training does.
**APPLY WHEN:** a rule rests on a checkable empirical claim; risk is overestimated; chronic over-preparation; far more effort goes in than the result requires.
**AVOID WHEN:** facts alone are expected to change behaviour — they open the door to surveys and experiments, nothing more; or the effort log becomes a new optimisation project.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Authors' clinical practice; the mechanisms cited are method-level research (Yerkes–Dodson; efficiency studies in the Stoeber lineage; change-blindness work), not components isolated in this programme's trials.
### [PERF:SURVEY] Survey other people (Section 7.3)
**Move:** Pick one belief or standard and write 3–8 concrete questions about frequency, duration or judgement. Choose respondents strategically — people you respect for beliefs about mistakes, objectively successful people for beliefs about success. Summarise as a table or graph and extract three products: a reality check on the standard; the SPREAD of answers as evidence against black-and-white rules; and any residual doubt converted into a behavioural experiment. The same method covers the double standard — ask what standards others hold themselves to and expect the counterintuitive result that many are more lenient with themselves than with others.
**APPLY WHEN:** the standard rides on fear of others' judgement; you suspect your bar sits far above the norm; the belief is that everyone else manages this effortlessly.
**AVOID WHEN:** the standard is moral rather than comparative — surveys land flat, use [PERF:RISK-BOTH-WAYS]; the survey runs long enough to burden respondents; results are read as reassurance rather than calibration; or a range of opinion is misread as approval of your own bar. Some beliefs survey TRUE and then need acceptance, not argument.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core component of a package with ≥4 direct trials of this book; surveys are standard CBT method in social anxiety and ritual-driven problems, never isolated as a component.
### [PERF:BEHEXP] Seven-step behavioural experiment (Worksheet 7.4.1 and variants)
**Move:** The central change engine. State the belief; derive the general prediction; specify it precisely as observable behaviours plus 0–100% belief and emotion ratings; run it; record what happened; reflect; write the revised belief. Build from trivial stakes upward. Two highest-yield variants have their own sheets: deliberately sub-par performance (7.5.3 — arriving with unwashed hair, bringing a lopsided cake, leaving the office without filing, hosting with one room clean) and time-capping (7.5.4 — Suzie cut nightly case notes from four hours to one for a week with zero client complaints), where quality holds because much of the extra time was anxiety and repetition rather than work. Other stock targets: work hard, moderately and minimally on three comparable tasks; deliberately not come top; leave something half-finished; and after meeting a goal, HOLD the standard instead of raising it and compare distress and self-worth against the usual bar-raise.
**APPLY WHEN:** a belief survives fact-checking and surveys; a specific falsifiable prediction exists; the target is an ordinary non-traumatic task; hours spent far exceed what the outcome needs.
**AVOID WHEN:** predictions are left vague so nothing can be disconfirmed; the first test is high-stakes rather than graded; the test is so trivial the belief never engages; a single experiment is treated as decisive — the book prescribes many; the target opens traumatic material or is really a formal exposure programme; or the standard has a genuine safety function.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core component of a package with ≥4 direct trials of this book; behavioural experiments carry method-level trial support across CBT. The claim that clients get similar results with less time is the authors' clinical observation. Note the boundary: exposure hierarchies are a different operation and are therapist work [PG26:EXPOSURE-THERAPIST].
### [PERF:CONTRAST] Alternating-days safety-behaviour contrast (Box 7.4.1)
**Move:** Run the ritual as usual or more on odd days and once only on even days; rate worry 0–10 and certainty 0–10 immediately afterwards and again an hour later; compare at day seven. Jeff's data ran opposite to his prediction — on repeated-check days worry sat at 8–9 immediately and 7–8 an hour later with certainty at 1–2, while on single-check days worry was 4 falling to 1–4 and certainty held at 8–9. Generalises to perfectionism-on versus perfectionism-off weeks for training, report-writing or tidying before guests.
**APPLY WHEN:** checking, reassurance calls, mental replaying of conversations, post-event processing — any ritual believed to reduce anxiety or errors.
**AVOID WHEN:** the days are not comparable because the tasks differ; fatigue is high, which makes worries stickier and distorts the read; or rituals are frequent and impairing enough that ritual-specific treatment is the right route instead.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Applies checking research (repeated checking lowers confidence in one's own memory — method-level experimental work) inside the trial-tested package; the design itself is a worked case, not a trialled component.
### [PERF:RISK-BOTH-WAYS] Map the risk of both options when the standard is moral (Mohammed's case)
**Move:** When a high standard is defended on moral or protective grounds, stop arguing about what other people do and lay out, side by side, the risks of loosening the standard and the risks of keeping it. Test the alternative behaviourally and track outcomes other than the one you habitually monitor — for Mohammed, that his children did better with an available, non-anxious father, and that every parenting strategy carries risk including the hyper-vigilant one.
**APPLY WHEN:** the standard is framed as duty, protection or morality; surveys land flat because other people's norms are explicitly irrelevant to you.
**AVOID WHEN:** falling anxiety is treated as the proof it worked — loosening control made Mohammed more anxious short-term and the gain was decision-making under accepted risk; also not a substitute for ritual-specific treatment where compulsions are present.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Author clinical case material resting on the behavioural-experiment method, which has direct trial support within this programme; the risk-mapping framing itself is untested.
### [PERF:IMG-FUTURE] Rebuild the anticipated-catastrophe image (Section 7.7, forward-looking variant)
**Move:** When dread arrives as a picture rather than a sentence, take the anticipated catastrophe — the audience laughing at you — and deliberately rebuild it into a realistic-coping or positive version: stumbling, recovering, landing a joke, the audience laughing with you. Keep a mood rating before and after and a stop rule.
**APPLY WHEN:** pre-task dread presents as imagery; the feared scene is a future event, not a memory.
**AVOID WHEN:** the image is a memory or has a traumatic core — that is [PERF:IMG-RESCRIPT-MEM] and belongs in a session; or mood worsens across attempts, which is a stop signal, not a reason to push.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Split by operation from the book's imagery section, which mixes memory and future targets; solo work on non-traumatic images is permitted per [PG26:IMG-NONTRAUMA], while the perfectionism application rests on the authors' clinical experience only.

## D. All-or-nothing appraisal and rules
### [PERF:CONTINUUM] Continuum against all-or-nothing categories (Worksheet 7.5.2)
**Move:** Write the dichotomous thought, name the two poles, then place at least three REAL examples of your own behaviour at intermediate points and write down what the spread shows. Variant: place people you know on a good-to-bad line and observe that nobody sits at either pole.
**APPLY WHEN:** behaviour is described in polar categories — total work versus no work, perfect diet versus abandoned diet, competent versus fraud.
**AVOID WHEN:** no concrete examples are collected, since abstract lines do not move belief; or it is used as a debating tactic instead of evidence-gathering.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct trial support; dichotomous thinking's maintenance role is backed by correlational work only (Egan and colleagues), which identifies it as a factor separating healthy striving from the unhelpful form.
### [PERF:RULE-LIST] Rules into guidelines, goals into flexible goals
**Move:** List every personal rule, must and should — Fatima produced around thirty in about two minutes. Rewrite selected ones so they permit exceptions (never eat chocolate becomes aim to eat healthily, occasional chocolate is fine), then verify the relaxation is safe with an experiment. Fairburn's distinction is the operative one: a rule snaps when violated, a guideline flexes. Do the same at sentence level (Box 7.7.1): I would prefer to, PLUS an explicit and it is OK if I do not. Same operation on goals: Stephen's always be on time for site meetings became try to be on time, cannot control every eventuality, carry the client's number to call ahead. Test achievability by surveying admired people who also have balanced lives.
**APPLY WHEN:** self-imposed regulations feel like a prison; rules have spread to trivia; self-talk is saturated with should and runs the pressure → self-criticism → avoidance sequence; daily goal lists end unfinished every day.
**AVOID WHEN:** the rule has a genuine safety or ethical function; the rewrite stays verbal and untested; or survey targets are chosen for achievement alone without the balance criterion, which re-anchors the bar high.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Merged from the rules-into-guidelines section and the goal-flexibilising material (Stephen's case): programme components with direct trials of this book; the rule/guideline distinction is credited to Fairburn, and the claim that flexible phrasing raises attainment is the authors' clinical reasoning, not trial data.
### [PERF:THOUGHT-DIARY] Five-step thought diary (Worksheet 7.7.3)
**Move:** Record the activating event; the beliefs, each tagged with its thinking-style label and rated 0–100, including what this says about you as a person; the feelings rated 0–100; a disputation pass (what a friend would say, what alternatives exist, what positives are ignored, whether the thought is useful); then re-rate the feelings. Two or three per week when strong emotion hits, reusing recurring alternatives across situations. Perfectionism-typical styles to label: double standards, overgeneralising and should-statements, plus catastrophising, emotional reasoning, labelling, personalisation, mind-reading and predictive thinking.
**APPLY WHEN:** any strong negative reaction tied to being evaluated on performance; recurring, identifiable self-critical or catastrophic appraisals.
**AVOID WHEN:** done only in your head — the writing is the mechanism; or the activating event is a traumatic memory or image, which routes to [PERF:IMG-RESCRIPT-MEM]. The first attempt feeling effortful is normal and not a stop signal.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Core CBT method with broad method-level trial support (cognitive restructuring as a standalone component: MODERATE) and a programme component in this book's trials. Aimee's worked example: anxiety 95% → 60%, sadness 50% → 30% after one diary — a case, not an effect size.

## E. Attention, discounted success, integration
### [PERF:ATTN-BROADEN] Broaden attention in the moment (Worksheet 7.6.1)
**Move:** Name the area, record the negative-focus thoughts with 0–100% belief ratings, then widen attention two ways: ask what positive evidence about this performance is being left out, and push focus outward onto the situation — the content of the conversation, the surroundings, asking the other person questions. Record what happened. Mechanism: selective monitoring for flaws works like noticing your own car model everywhere after buying it, and sustained attention on a domain also inflates how important it seems.
**APPLY WHEN:** rumination on one flaw hijacks a live situation — a crease in a shirt mid-conversation, one mistake at a dinner party.
**AVOID WHEN:** applied only in retrospect, since it works in the moment; or it slides into suppressing the negative or seeking reassurance rather than adding back the rest of the picture.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct trial support at package level; attention-bias work is method-level, and the component effect here is not isolated.
### [PERF:POS-DIARY] Positive-evidence diary, including absent criticism (Worksheet 7.6.2 plus the Chapter 9 daily log)
**Move:** Keep a standing diary in three columns — area; positive evidence about the performance; and the ABSENCE of negative comment, which normally goes unrecorded. Extend with the Chapter 9 version: log at least one thing you did each day, deliberately including small things you would dismiss, spread across all life areas — Stephen's entries included walking the dog instead of working, starting an avoided job, phoning his parents while busy, staying civil with a difficult client.
**APPLY WHEN:** long-standing discounting of positives; achievements dismissed as having been too easy; a low baseline sense of doing anything well.
**AVOID WHEN:** stopped after one week — several weeks of active searching are needed before balanced noticing becomes automatic; entries pre-filtered for worthy items or restricted to work output, which reproduces the bias; or the log itself becomes a standard to hit.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct trial support at package level; the absent-negative-evidence column is the authors' clinical addition and untested separately.
### [PERF:REALITY-INTEGRATE] Write down what your personal reality actually is (Section 7.9)
**Move:** Assemble the outputs of the separate exercises — survey results, experiment outcomes, attention notes, alternative thoughts — into one written statement of what is true in your own situation: which part of the negative belief is distortion and which part is a genuine cost that has to be accepted. The authors explicitly refuse the positive-thinking frame; sometimes the reality is that a goal is not worth its cost, and the work is accepting that while blocking the all-or-nothing generalisation that the years were therefore wasted.
**APPLY WHEN:** several exercises have run on the same standard and the conclusions are scattered or contradictory.
**AVOID WHEN:** run as a substitute for the exercises — the integration has no content without prior data; or where the accepted cost is a bereavement or an ended relationship rather than a stalled project, which is not this lens's material.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Direct trial support for the programme as a whole (Egan 2014; Handley 2015; Rozental 2017; Shafran 2017); the integration step itself is author clinical practice.

## F. Procrastination, time and load
### [PERF:PROC-AUDIT] Procrastination map, log, cost challenge and undersized test (Worksheets 7.8.1–7.8.5)
**Move:** Three passes before any fix, then one test. Mark which domains you delay in and write a concrete delay behaviour for each. Log episodes: area and situation; the perfectionist prediction made AT THE MOMENT of deciding to delay, rated 0–100%; the actual delay behaviours (clock-watching, over-detailed lists, tidying the desk); feelings 0–100%. In a fifth column write exactly how each delay RAISED belief in the prediction, since the prediction never got tested. Tabulate benefits and costs of procrastinating and challenge each supposed benefit explicitly. Then test the prediction with a deliberately undersized action — Gemma, instead of cancelling supervision, brought two draft pages and discussed ideas; anxiety fell 90% → 45%, sadness 40% → 20%, and belief in never writing a good proposal 90% → 65%.
**APPLY WHEN:** chronic delay and deadline-crunch; mounting undone tasks; delay defended as anxiety management or dismissed as laziness or poor time management.
**AVOID WHEN:** logged from memory days later, since the prediction is what gets lost; the benefits column left blank out of shame; the cost table left without the challenge column, which is the active ingredient; the test sized too big, since the small start is the working part; or the whole exercise turned into another self-criticism vehicle.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Merged from the procrastination worksheet block (7.8.1–7.8.4 mapping and cost challenge, 7.8.5 undersized experiment) — programme components with direct trial support at package level; procrastination gets the book's thickest block, and its link to worse academic performance is correlational.
### [PERF:CHUNK] Difficulty-rated task ladder (Worksheet 7.8.9)
**Move:** Define the goal, break it into chunks, rate each 0–100 for difficulty on an eleven-step ladder (Simon's flat ran from thinking about the problem at 0 to cleaning the oven at 100), and start at the easy end NOW — not at the most important piece, and not once motivation arrives.
**APPLY WHEN:** the task feels monolithic; starting is the blocked step; the start keeps sliding.
**AVOID WHEN:** chunks are left large enough to re-trigger the delay, or the chunking is done so exhaustively that planning replaces doing.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct trial support at package level; graded task assignment has method-level support in CBT.
### [PERF:BALANCE-SCHEDULE] Weekly grid plus scheduled pleasant events (Worksheets 7.8.11, 7.8.12)
**Move:** Fill the hourly grid — 7 a.m. to 10 p.m. across seven days, fifteen slots a day — planning achievement time AND recovery time, then answer what it showed about the balance and what has to change. Plant several items from the roughly 65-item pleasant-activities menu into the grid as fixed entries and review the mood effect at week's end. Aimee scheduled television with her partner, dinners out, magazines and a beauty appointment.
**APPLY WHEN:** overwork, working into the night, no scheduled recovery, guilt about wasting time, relationship strain from constant busyness, flat mood.
**AVOID WHEN:** the grid fills with achievement blocks only, becomes another rigid rule to fail, or the activities themselves get rated for how well they were done; also when dismissed as too simple — the book pre-empts exactly that discount.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme components with direct trial support at package level; activity scheduling and behavioural activation carry the strongest independent method-level support of anything in this toolkit (BA as a standalone component: MODERATE).
### [PERF:PROBSOLVE] Six-step structured problem-solving (Worksheet 7.8.10)
**Move:** Define the problem objectively and specifically; brainstorm without judging; underline two or three viable options; weigh feasibility and likely effect and pick one; plan the concrete steps; carry it out; evaluate the effect and note what it taught you about intervening earlier. Aimee's version: vacuum only the two rooms guests would see, then switch to cooking as a distraction.
**APPLY WHEN:** the obstacle is a real practical constraint; also for generating chunk lists and alternatives to all-or-nothing behaviour.
**AVOID WHEN:** ideas are judged during the brainstorm, which is explicitly barred until the shortlist step; or it is used to route around testing a belief when the obstacle is predictive rather than practical.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component; structured problem-solving has method-level trial support as a general CBT technique, not isolated within this programme.
### [PERF:PORTABLE-COUNTERS] Flashcards, cue cards and a coping image (Worksheets 7.8.6–7.8.8)
**Move:** Distil your own disputations and experiment results into portable form: a card of helpful statements drawn from your own data; the compassionate statements from Worksheet 8.4 that actually shifted mood, one per card, carried and posted where you will see them; a short visible list of why not to delay; and a coping IMAGE built from a real past success to replace the dread image — James swapped picturing himself stuck at the desk late into the night checking endlessly for one hour of work with limited checking, then stopping and meeting friends, relief included.
**APPLY WHEN:** the decision point is brief and reasoning fails in the moment; the critical voice is loud enough that nothing balanced can be recalled; pre-task dread arrives as imagery.
**AVOID WHEN:** written before any experiments, since the statements must come from your own evidence rather than generic affirmations; used as reassurance-seeking or thought suppression; used as the sole intervention; or used to force push-through on genuinely misprioritised tasks or on the back of exhaustion.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme components resting on the authors' clinical experience and anecdote — the weakest evidence tier in this book's toolkit.

## G. Self-criticism and compassion
### [PERF:CRITIC-COMPASSION-DIARY] Two-pass critic then compassion diary (Worksheets 8.1, 8.4)
**Move:** Pass one, for a week: log the triggering event, the self-critical thought INCLUDING what it says about you as a person, and the feeling rated 0–100%. Mine your earlier monitoring sheets for the entries you would hesitate to say aloud to someone else, and note the recurring names you call yourself. Pass two, for a month: add two columns — what the compassionate voice says (what you would say to a friend, with belief rated 0–100%) and what the original feeling did afterwards — re-rated honestly even when it went up.
**APPLY WHEN:** self-criticism is so habitual it reads as plain fact; and then, once the voice is named, to build the alternative.
**AVOID WHEN:** an initial worsening is read as failure — the book's own printed example shows low mood rising 75% → 95% and humiliation 80% → 100% with a compassionate statement believed at only 30%, and keeps it in on purpose; the monitoring becomes another performance; mood drops sharply during the week (shorten it); or the trigger is a traumatic memory.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Direct trial of this book; Kothari 2019 secondary outcomes for this programme — fear of self-compassion d = −0.8, self-esteem d = +0.7. Note the boundary: compassion-focused work for self-criticism generally is LOW, driven by passive controls [PG26 Р1 correction].
### [PERF:VALUES-MIRROR] The same values list, turned on yourself (Worksheets 8.2, 8.3, Box 8.2)
**Move:** Circle the twelve values you actually apply to friends — acceptance, caring, compassion, courtesy, forgiveness, generosity, helpfulness, hope, fun, justice, service, respect — and add your own. Then read the identical list rewritten in the first person and name every place where you grant a friend what you refuse yourself. Pair it with the two-coaches thought experiment: imagine your own child coached by a man who says nothing on success and berates by name on failure, or by one who praises effort and treats mistakes as learning — then answer which coach you would keep, which produces better performance, and which one you are being to yourself.
**APPLY WHEN:** warmth toward others is articulable but warmth toward yourself is not; the double standard is invisible or denied; the belief that criticism is what keeps you sharp is live.
**AVOID WHEN:** the friendship list is filled in aspirationally rather than from real behaviour, since the contrast is the whole point; the inconsistency becomes fresh grounds for shame; or the coach exercise is taken as a one-off insight without the diaries — recognition alone does not shift the habit.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct trial support; Kothari 2019 fear of self-compassion d = −0.8; the coach experiment is adapted from Pollack & Otto (2009) and the self-criticism-to-less-progress link is correlational only.
### [PERF:OBSERVE-NOT-REACT] Watch the critical thought instead of arguing with it
**Move:** Eyes closed if that is comfortable, watch thoughts arrive as if projected on a screen and label them — a self-critical thought has just arrived, this is the familiar tape. Acknowledge body sensations without pushing them away and let the thought pass like a radio track you decline to turn up. The stated goal is a volume knob turned down, never off.
**APPLY WHEN:** arguing with the critical voice keeps feeding it; the loops are repetitive and recognisable.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to avoid the behavioural work; if closing the eyes brings intrusive traumatic imagery, stop and take it to a clinician rather than pushing through; and in a trauma history, worsening during practice means stop, not try harder [PG26].
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Method-level acceptance and mindfulness trials cited by the authors (fewer depressive episodes in recurrent low mood; reduced body dissatisfaction); not isolated within this programme.

## H. Widening the base of self-worth
### [PERF:HOTSPOT-DIARY] Seven-day self-evaluation diary and the if–then rule (Worksheet 9.1)
**Move:** For seven days record what made you feel particularly good or bad ABOUT YOURSELF, which life domain it belongs to, the thought, and the feeling intensity 0–100. Then write your own governing rule as an if–then statement, and note which domains never registered at all. Stephen's week showed only work and being a good community member moving his self-worth, while fitness, family and friends produced enjoyment but no effect on it.
**APPLY WHEN:** you cannot say which areas your sense of worth actually rests on.
**AVOID WHEN:** filled in from theory rather than from the week as it happened.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Programme component with direct trial support at package level; structurally borrowed from Fennell's low-self-esteem work.
### [PERF:BROADBASE] Widen the base of self-evaluation (Worksheets 6.4, 9.3, 9.4)
**Move:** For each of roughly thirteen life areas write who you want to BE and what you want to DO — identity and action, not targets. Redo it later and compare with the earlier version. Then pick four life areas DIFFERENT from your current self-evaluation domains and set six-month goals in each: what you want to change, the most important reason, the planned steps, the observable sign it is working, and the likely obstacles with countermeasures. Small steps, no musts. Principle: eggs in several baskets, so a setback in one area is buffered by the others.
**APPLY WHEN:** self-worth hangs on one or two domains; the fear is being worthless without high standards; after [PERF:HOTSPOT-DIARY] shows a narrow base.
**AVOID WHEN:** the new areas get converted into fresh achievement arenas with standards and checking attached — pastimes are for enjoyment, not mastery; or the how-I-will-know-it-is-working line is written as an all-or-nothing criterion.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Operationalises the book's core model; programme component with direct trial support, and Kothari 2019 reports self-esteem d = +0.7 for the programme as a whole, not for this component.

## I. Consolidation, escalation and therapist-tier operations
### [PERF:RELAPSE-BLUEPRINT] Toolbox, tough-going sheet and lapse protocol (Ch. 10, Worksheet 9.5)
**Move:** Re-read your completed exercises and decide chapter by chapter which tools go in your toolbox, guided by what you want to keep developing and what still needs attention, one line per retained strategy. Complete the six-part sheet: what kept the perfectionism going; every strategy learned; your own top tips; the situations that will be hardest; how you will handle them; and one very short reminder of the whole programme. For an actual setback run six steps: pre-identify triggers; catch the lapse early without fatalism; recruit extra support; reinstate the plan; problem-solve the trigger with [PERF:PROBSOLVE]; and note what you did well despite the difficulty.
**APPLY WHEN:** finishing the programme; a wobble starting; the critical voice getting louder again; reverting to old coping under stress.
**AVOID WHEN:** done as a completeness exercise where every tool is kept, since the selection is the point; written only after a lapse instead of in advance; postponed until it can be written perfectly; or the lapse is read as proof the work failed, which is the all-or-nothing target itself.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** Relapse-prevention components of a programme with direct trial support; blueprints are method-level CBT standard. Note that the normative guidance for closing a course adds dedicated sessions with a clinician [PG26:RELAPSE-4S].
### [PERF:IMG-RESCRIPT-MEM] Rescripting a distressing memory-image (Section 7.7, memory target)
**Move:** Re-enter a recurring humiliating scene as your competent adult self, intervene, give the younger self new information and comfort. Mary's example was a childhood memory of classmates mocking her stumbling while reading aloud, still firing in adult public speaking. Council output stops at naming the target and the format to bring: the theme, why it belongs in a session, what to prepare, and the question to ask.
**APPLY WHEN:** a specific past humiliation replays and blocks present performance; note the book's claim that perfectionism-related images are more frequent and sticky, and that in one Australian study imagery explained the perfectionism-to-distress link better than repetitive verbal thinking did.
**AVOID WHEN:** attempted solo — this book gives the technique a few paragraphs, prints no blank worksheet for it, and offers no containment structure; early rescripting is therapist-performed even inside therapy.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Imagery rescripting has method-level trials in other targets; its perfectionism application rests on the authors' clinical experience only. Tier is set by the operation — reactivating and reprocessing memory material (safety-protocol §3; Arntz 2025 via [PG26:IMG-NONTRAUMA]).
### [PERF:HISTORICAL-REVIEW] Historical test of the new perspective (Worksheet 9.2)
**Move:** Take the perspective that missing your own standards does not make you less worthwhile as a person, then scan six age bands from birth to 25 for experiences consistent with it, writing them down. Consolidate by writing yourself a letter in the voice of a compassionate other. In this format the council names it as session material and helps prepare the bands and the question, rather than running the review.
**APPLY WHEN:** the new perspective is understood intellectually but not believed; perfectionism feels lifelong and no memory of being valued for anything but output is available.
**AVOID WHEN:** run solo — the age bands routinely hold loss, abuse or absent care and the sheet carries no containment at all; also when the letter turns into a piece of writing to be performed well.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Standard CBT historical-test method in the Fennell and compassion-focused lineage, embedded in a programme with direct trial support; the tier is driven by what the exercise opens, not by weak evidence.
### [PERF:COST-BENEFIT-DREAM] Cost-benefit review of a long-pursued goal (Sally's case)
**Move:** For a goal that is not materialising, run an explicit cost-versus-benefit review, then separate two questions in writing: did this achieve the stated goal, and was it therefore worthless. A survey checks how ordinary cut-your-losses experiences are, and a deliberate search surfaces side-benefits the goal-focus hid — Sally's writing courses had quietly paid off in her day job.
**APPLY WHEN:** paralysis over what has already been invested; unable to start anything new until the old project succeeds; low mood tied to a stalled ambition.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to talk yourself out of a goal that is merely hard; and whenever letting go opens active mourning or identity collapse — there is no solo protocol for that, and nothing here reframes a loss as an opportunity.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** Author clinical case material on top of the programme's cost-benefit and survey methods; the tier follows the loss material it opens, and grief work has an entry condition that must be measured first [PG26:GRIEF-GATE].
### [PERF:ESCALATE] Add guidance, then add a clinician (Ch. 4 and Ch. 10)
**Move:** Two escalation steps the book prescribes for itself. First, if working alone and gains stall — especially when perfectionism moves but anxiety and mood do not — add guidance: a CBT therapist working through this book, or guided self-help with brief weekly feedback, the format that performed well in trials. Directories: babcp.com, eabct.org, abct.org, aacbt.org. Second, if perfectionism still substantially interferes with quality of life after genuine practice, that is the signal to find a skilled clinician rather than to try harder alone.
**APPLY WHEN:** unguided work reduces perfectionism but anxiety and low mood persist; exercises repeatedly stall; comorbid mood, ritual or eating problems; distress worsening.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to abandon the work at the first plateau — step one adds support and removes nothing.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** The authors' own stated limit on self-help plus their trial contrast: guided formats improved anxiety, mood and self-criticism where pure unguided self-help moved only perfectionism. Independent of this book, unguided is weaker than every guided format (HIGH) and Galloway's self-help ≈ face-to-face result (15 RCTs, N = 912) is an average, not a guarantee for an individual.
