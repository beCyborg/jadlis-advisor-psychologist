# Breaking Negative Thinking Patterns — digest

## What this book is for the lens

Thesis: recurring problems are not traits but **modes** — states of feeling/thinking/acting that reproduce the same outcome, each traceable to an unmet basic need. States can be named, mapped and switched; the change target is never the need itself but its degree and mode of expression.

Where it beats the rest of the lens: an operational mode vocabulary plus 20 numbered worksheets (structured self-observation without a clinician), explicit marking of which work is safe alone, and detection logic for the modes that emit no internal distress signal — spoilt, impulsive, overcompensatory — where only outside feedback is data.

## Core model

**Five mode families.** Child (Vulnerable, Angry/Impulsive, Happy), Dysfunctional Parent (Demanding, Guilt-inducing, Punitive), Coping (Surrender, Avoidance, Overcompensation), Healthy Adult — the intended default, with Happy Child deliberately scheduled.

**Diagnosis by disproportion, not content.** Emotion too big for the trigger *and* hard to steer; for the pleasant modes, frequency plus serious life damage. Ordinary irritation, procrastination and rest are not modes.

**Basic emotional needs** (Young, Klosko & Weishaar, 2006) are the yardstick: attachment/safety; independence, competence, identity; freedom to express needs and feelings; spontaneity, fun, play; realistic boundaries. Ch. 5's differential: both parties' needs served → Healthy Adult; only yours, or no read on yours at all → a dysfunctional mode (Table 5.1 pairs the six look-alikes).

**Opposite prescriptions by origin.** Vulnerable Child needs comfort and protection; Dysfunctional Parent needs limiting and reduction; angry/enraged modes usually sit *secondary* to a vulnerable feeling and dissolve once that need is met; impulsive/spoilt modes come from *absent* limits and need the inverse — limit tolerance, delayed gratification. Coping is weakened, never erased: it turns dysfunctional only when it blocks needs or obligations.

**Sequence.** Recognize modes and their biographical origin first, change second. Three levels: cognitive, emotional (imagery is the strong lever), behavioral (usually last).

**Navigation anchors.** Part I: WS1 mode overview, WS2 vulnerable child, WS3 angry/impulsive, WS4 pro/con 2×2, WS5 happy child, WS6–8 parent modes, WS9/9a surrender, WS10/10a avoidance, WS11 overcompensation, WS12 healthy adult. Part II: Ch. 6 healing vulnerable child (Ex. 6.1–6.3), Ch. 7 anger/impulsivity (Ex. 7.1–7.3), Ch. 8 happy child (Ex. 8.1), Ch. 9 limiting parent modes (Ex. 9.1/9.3, WS13–15), Ch. 10 coping change (Ex. 10.1–10.3, WS16–17), Ch. 11 healthy adult (Ex. 11.1, Box 11.1, WS18–19).

## Moves

### [ST:PRO-CON-GRID] Decisional-balance 2×2 for a mode that feels good
**Move:** Worksheet 4 — advantages and disadvantages of the angry/impulsive/spoilt mode, split short-term vs. long-term; read the asymmetry out loud and treat the *advantage* column as the real obstacle list for change, not as something to argue away.
**APPLY WHEN:** the mode feels good in the moment and motivation to change is absent or wobbly.
**AVOID WHEN:** used as self-prosecution — a grid without the genuine short-term gains will not be believed.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** standard CBT decisional balance; no book-specific data.

### [ST:CM-WS16] Coping-mode pro/con including its functional uses
**Move:** Worksheet 16 — pro/con per coping style, deliberately listing where that coping is genuinely useful; expect short-term pros against long-term cons, and keep the con list physically visible as motivation fuel, especially for avoidance.
**APPLY WHEN:** before any attempt to reduce a coping mode.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to justify erasing the mode — the stated goal is weakening.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** decisional balance; two worked lists (Harry's avoidance, Glenn's overcompensation).

### [ST:AC-BEHEXP] Behavior experiment on one recurring trigger
**Move:** Pre-plan a different response to a single recurring trigger and run it as a game; the new behavior may be constructive or deliberately exaggerated and funny — the point is that it is chosen in advance rather than emitted.
**APPLY WHEN:** the trigger is an ordinary daily-life situation and you are calm while planning.
**AVOID WHEN:** the situation carries violence risk or a genuinely unsafe counterpart.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** behavior experiments are a well-tested CBT component; no book-specific trial.

### [ST:HA-BEHEXP19] Healthy-Adult-led situation, fully pre-specified
**Move:** Worksheet 19 — the situation the Healthy Adult should lead, the exact intended behavior, which modes have interfered before, the in-situ reminder (postcard, prior imagery), how a caring person would encourage you, and the reward; run it even if you do not feel ready. The case adds hard pre-commitments: no alcohol, home by 1 a.m., a rehearsed refusal line.
**APPLY WHEN:** a concrete, non-traumatic situation with a clear behavioral target.
**AVOID WHEN:** the target situation carries real safety risk.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** broad CBT support for behavior experiments; worksheet structure is the authors'.

### [ST:CM-REDUCE] Replace, rehearse, refuse the objection, generalize
**Move:** Exercise 10.3 — define the *positive alternative* behavior rather than just stopping the old one, rehearse it vividly in imagery, expel any Guilt-inducing/Punitive objection with an explicit refusal, run it first in a low-demand situation, then generalize, rewarding each step; capture in Worksheet 17 (situation, old behavior, why change, Healthy Adult alternative, reward).
**APPLY WHEN:** reducing surrender or avoidance.
**AVOID WHEN:** first attempts get judged by perfection — partial success counts as success.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** graded behavioral practice; worksheet from clinical experience.

### [ST:PM-COUNTER] Counter-evidence against a parent message
**Move:** Worksheet 15 — for each damaging message write personal counter-evidence, treating it as a truth test against selective perception (the mode's premise filters what you notice); recruit a close friend when you cannot generate counter-evidence alone.
**APPLY WHEN:** you half-suspect the punitive voice might be right — the authors call that doubt normal.
**AVOID WHEN:** solo, if the Punitive Mode *strengthens* under challenge — the book's stated indication for professional support, not for more effort.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** cognitive-restructuring family of methods; clinical experience.

### [ST:AC-CHAIR] Chair dialogue between the mode and the Healthy Adult
**Move:** Two seats — one argues the pros of the angry/impulsive mode, the other the Healthy Adult cons — physically switching between them; soft toys or puppets can stand in for chairs.
**APPLY WHEN:** the pro/con list is written and you are calm.
**AVOID WHEN:** still furious (the authors say it simply will not work), or the target is a Parent Mode carrying abuse content.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** adapted from schema-therapy practice; authors explicitly endorse solo use.

### [ST:CM-SAFEPLACE] Coping-free anchor carried into the hard situation
**Move:** Exercise 10.2 — in imagery enter a situation where you need no coping mode at all (choir, animals, children), saturate the safe feeling, then carry it — and optionally a person from it — into the difficult situation; register even a slight shift as a result.
**APPLY WHEN:** you can name a reliably coping-free activity.
**AVOID WHEN:** no genuinely safe anchor exists yet — build one before the transfer.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** safe-place imagery is standard practice; clinical experience.

### [ST:AC-CONTROL] Five-part anger-control set
**Move:** (a) identify idiosyncratic early warning signals, bodily (tense shoulders) and cognitive ("nobody cares about me"); (b) express anger stepwise, starting calm, escalating only if unheard; (c) micro-break — open a window, breathe, study the other person's face in detail — to re-find the goal; (d) carry a calming symbol (stone, image, song); (e) rehearse the alternative reaction in imagery. Do not postpone expressing the anger too long.
**APPLY WHEN:** recurrent escalation in ordinary interpersonal settings.
**AVOID WHEN:** it substitutes for addressing the need — suppression is not the goal, and others detect swallowed anger anyway.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** overlaps standard anger-management protocols; clinical experience.

### [ST:HA-REHEARSE] Film yourself in Healthy Adult beforehand
**Move:** Exercise 11.1 — pick a situation where you usually back down and run a mental film of yourself acting in Healthy Adult Mode in it, before it happens.
**APPLY WHEN:** a predictable social situation is coming up.
**AVOID WHEN:** the scene is a trauma scene; otherwise no restriction noted.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** mental rehearsal; clinical experience (Megan case).

### [ST:AC-IMGCALM] Rehearse the calmer confrontation
**Move:** Exercise 7.3 — imagine the provoking situation, identify what would lower the anger there (reassurance, a friend's presence), then re-run the scene staying measured while still stating your need and naming the limit that was crossed.
**APPLY WHEN:** preparing for a predictable confrontation.
**AVOID WHEN:** the imagined scene is a trauma scene.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** mental-rehearsal rationale; clinical experience.

### [ST:HC-BRIDGE] Affective bridge into what you actually enjoy
**Move:** Exercise 8.1 — relax, recall clearly happy times (childhood or recent) reliving smells, tastes, sounds, skin sensations; the reactivated positive affect then lets the mind generate today's analogous activities. Without this step prescribing fun fails: a suppressed Happy Child does not know what it likes, and asking in a low mood yields nothing.
**APPLY WHEN:** you cannot name what you enjoy.
**AVOID WHEN:** the "happy" memory sits inside a loss — grief work is not this exercise.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** affective bridge is a described phenomenon, untested as an intervention here.

### [ST:JOY-INVENTORY] Happy-mode repertoire, written down
**Move:** Worksheet 5 — list the activities, people and conditions that reliably switch the happy mode on; note when it was last active, what preceded it, and any warm-up that eases access (e.g. exercise before playful activity). Roll findings into Worksheet 1.
**APPLY WHEN:** stress/overload, the happy mode is rare, or as ongoing maintenance.
**AVOID WHEN:** it is dodging a problem that needs facing — that turns it into Avoidant Coping.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** behavioral-activation-shaped workbook exercise; no trial cited.

### [ST:HA-ACTIVITY] Healthy-Adult activity list in physical view
**Move:** Build a personal list from Box 11.1 — learn something, substantive talk with a friend, take responsibility, exercise, repair something, evening log of the day's positives, read, do something healthy, new recipe, hobby, help or teach someone, tick a to-do and reward it, write yourself a friendly postcard — and place it where you physically stumble across it.
**APPLY WHEN:** the Healthy Adult is weak or avoided.
**AVOID WHEN:** the mode is already strong — the authors say skip it then.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** behavioral-activation logic; clinical experience.

### [ST:VC-NEEDCHECK] In-the-moment need check plus self-compassion set
**Move:** Exercise 6.3 — when the vulnerable mode fires: what do I want right now, which need is unmet, does it echo a childhood memory, can I meet it better today than I could then? Then: why am I hurt, what do I need, what is currently wrong in my life (workload, a move, a loss, a relationship), and what would produce the opposite emotion (company against loneliness, joy against sadness). Act on the answer here and now.
**APPLY WHEN:** routine daily use, at moderate intensity.
**AVOID WHEN:** it turns into rumination — keep it short and end on the action.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** overlaps standard self-compassion practice; clinical experience.

### [ST:AC-PRIMSEC] Sort anger into primary vs. secondary
**Move:** Exercise 7.1 — re-enter a recent episode and sort it: pure anger/frustration versus anger carrying sadness, loneliness, rejection or unfair treatment. Test the unfairness by asking whether it would be unfair if the same person did it to anyone else. If vulnerable feelings are present, route to Ch. 6 — meeting that need often makes the rage disappear without separate anger work.
**APPLY WHEN:** recurring outbursts of unclear origin.
**AVOID WHEN:** attempted while still enraged.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** primary/secondary emotion distinction is standard in emotion-focused work.

### [ST:HABIT-FUNCTION] Ask what the habit is protecting you from
**Move:** For each disliked habit ask what you would feel if you did *not* give in to it; the feeling that surfaces names what the habit is avoiding (anxiety, emptiness, dissatisfaction).
**APPLY WHEN:** the habit feels automatic and reasonless.
**AVOID WHEN:** physiological dependence is likely — abrupt stopping of alcohol or benzodiazepines is a medical matter, not a self-experiment.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** functional-analysis logic as applied in Ch. 4.

### [ST:PROBLEM-BEHAVIOR] The pleasant-now/costly-later test
**Move:** Test a habit against the Box 2.2 rule: problematic = pleasant now, costly later; healthy counterpart = unpleasant now, paying later. Practise on the book's neutral cases — postponing the tax declaration, postponing the dentist.
**APPLY WHEN:** sorting an ordinary bad habit from mode-driven avoidance or impulsivity.
**AVOID WHEN:** applied to genuine rest and pleasure — that misreading feeds the Demanding Parent.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** general behavioral principle stated in the book.

### [ST:MODE-OVERVIEW] One running master sheet
**Move:** Keep a single master sheet (Worksheet 1) listing your Child, Parent, Coping and Healthy Adult modes, and fold every later exercise's findings back into it — it is the spine the rest of the book hangs on.
**APPLY WHEN:** from the first chapter onward, throughout all later work.
**AVOID WHEN:** it degenerates into collecting labels instead of observations — a map, not a diagnosis.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** workbook structure; clinical experience.

### [ST:SIX-PROBE] Six-channel interrogation of a vulnerable mode
**Move:** Worksheet 2 — probe an activated mode along six channels: trigger situation, feelings, thoughts, body sensation, associated memories/images, behavior toward others; then add mode-switch patterns, what you actually needed, and whether your behavior met that need.
**APPLY WHEN:** mapping a mode you can already notice, ordinary non-traumatic triggers, after the state has cooled.
**AVOID WHEN:** the memory channel opens abuse or loss material (leave that probe closed, take it to a therapist), or the state is at peak intensity.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** workbook exercise; no outcome trial.

### [ST:ANGRY-MAP] Same six channels for anger and impulsivity
**Move:** Worksheet 3 — map the angry/impulsive mode along the six channels, plus which mode precedes or follows it, what the genuine need is, and whether the behavior gets that need met.
**APPLY WHEN:** recurring anger, defiance or impulsive episodes with visible fallout.
**AVOID WHEN:** mapping mid-outburst — wait for a calm interval.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** workbook exercise; clinical experience.

### [ST:PARENT-VOICE-LOG] Write out the parent mode's actual sentences
**Move:** Worksheets 6/7/8 each open with "Messages of this mode": write the sentences as you hear them, name which parent mode it is, then map trigger, feelings, thoughts, body, behavior, which mode triggered it, what you need in it, and how it changes your sense of safety.
**APPLY WHEN:** a recurring self-critical or guilt-loading inner voice; Demanding and Guilt-inducing variants.
**AVOID WHEN:** the Punitive variant is anchored in abuse memories, or the voice tells you to hurt yourself — that belongs in therapy, not a solo worksheet (severe cases such as Annabelle need long psychotherapy).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** workbook exercise; clinical experience.

### [ST:OC-WS11] Rate the overcompensation repertoire 0–100
**Move:** Worksheet 11 — for each of the six patterns (narcissistic arrogance, paranoid control, obsessive control, attention-seeking, aggression, cheating/cunning) rate intensity 0–100, list triggering situations, name the actual need there, then answer whether the behavior met it. That last column is the working lever: the need-versus-fulfilment gap.
**APPLY WHEN:** mapping a coping repertoire before attempting change.
**AVOID WHEN:** used as an accusation list against someone else.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience; no psychometrics for the ratings.

### [ST:HA-WS12] Map the Healthy Adult you already have
**Move:** Worksheet 12 — name the mode personally, record triggers, typical feelings and thoughts, associated memories, whether basic needs are met in it, how it changes your sense of safety; then list the activities, situations and people that reliably bring it on. Premise: you scale change from where access is already easy.
**APPLY WHEN:** building a change plan.
**AVOID WHEN:** it becomes an aim to live in this mode permanently — named unrealistic.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:PM-COGID] Identify the parent voice cognitively, not emotionally
**Move:** Exercise 9.1 — recall a situation of disproportionate pressure or rejection at a *cognitive* level only: what did you feel obliged to do, how would you have felt acting otherwise (traitor? guilty? not entitled?), what does the voice sound like and whose is it. Classify by dominant feeling: failure → Demanding, guilt → Guilt-inducing, shame/self-hatred/fear → Punitive.
**APPLY WHEN:** first mapping of the parent voice.
**AVOID WHEN:** you start sliding into the felt scene — the authors forbid getting *into* the punitive voice; if flooded, stop and talk to someone.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience, with an explicit safety carve-out in the chapter.

### [ST:PM-WS13] Inventory the messages with origin and impact rating
**Move:** Worksheet 13 "Identifying Parent Messages" — each message, its biographical origin, its impact today 0–100, and what activates it. Worked example: "doing something for yourself is egoistic", origin mother as role model, impact 85, activated whenever you allow yourself something.
**APPLY WHEN:** after cognitive identification (Ex. 9.1).
**AVOID WHEN:** no restriction noted; keep it descriptive rather than argumentative at this stage.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:PM-WS14] Keep / moderate / erase, per message
**Move:** Worksheet 14 — sort each message into Healthy Adult vs. Demanding/Guilt-inducing/Punitive, then decide per message: keep, moderate into a healthier rule, or erase. Discipline and care messages belong to the Healthy Adult and stay; demanding ones get rebalanced; punitive self-devaluing ones are erased, not softened.
**APPLY WHEN:** the Part I mode vocabulary is solid and WS13 is filled.
**AVOID WHEN:** attempted before the mode definitions are solid — the sort collapses without them.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience; three worked case tables (Aisha, Annabelle, Freddie).

### [ST:PM-SYMBOL] Physical limit-setting reminders
**Move:** Place a symbolic object where you work — small stop sign, stone, shell, figure — as a limit-setting cue; write yourself a postcard asserting your right to change and let supportive people write their versions; put photos of people who would answer back on your desktop.
**APPLY WHEN:** everyday reinforcement between the formal exercises.
**AVOID WHEN:** no restriction noted; it supplements the written work, never replaces it.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:MODE-NAME] Give each mode a personal nickname
**Move:** Name your modes personally — "little Lisa" for the vulnerable side, "pigheaded Tom" for the angry one — and use the name out loud when the state shows up; the label creates the distance the state itself dissolves.
**APPLY WHEN:** you can feel a state but cannot label it in the moment.
**AVOID WHEN:** the nickname turns into mockery — a contemptuous label recruits the Punitive Parent instead of distancing from the mode.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** standard schema-therapy device; clinical experience.

### [ST:SCREEN-ITEMS] Agree/disagree item sets as a first pass only
**Move:** Use the short per-mode statement sets as candidate generators — vulnerable ("I often feel completely alone", "I feel weak and helpless", "no one loves me"), overcompensatory (harsh criticism of others, fame/success fantasies, jumping defensively when criticized, overruling others, getting respect through threat). Strong agreement = candidate mode; weak agreement = a familiar feeling without clinical weight. For the overcompensatory set ask what you *did* right after a distressing episode, not how you felt.
**APPLY WHEN:** you do not yet know which modes are yours, or you are told you are dominant/arrogant but feel fine inside.
**AVOID WHEN:** treated as a test or a verdict on personality — questionnaires only hint and your own appraisal outranks them; skip during acute crisis.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** informal screening items, no psychometric validation given.

### [ST:HA-NEEDBAL] Need-balance check to identify the active mode
**Move:** When unsure which mode is running, check the need balance: are my needs met here, are the other person's, do I even have a read on mine? Then use Table 5.1 to pick the matching pair — disciplined-with-limits vs. Demanding Parent, self-criticism vs. self-hatred, enjoyment vs. impulsive overdoing, socially adequate anger vs. Angry Child outburst, situational switch-off vs. blanket Avoidant Coping, taking charge flexibly vs. rigid control.
**APPLY WHEN:** two modes seem simultaneously active (e.g. feeling rejected while knowing you are not).
**AVOID WHEN:** applied mid-conflict as a scoring device against the other party.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** conceptual rule from the book, not tested.

### [ST:WANT-MUST-TEST] Do I want to, do I have to
**Move:** Ask of each burdensome activity: do I want to do it, and do I have to do it? Neither → surrender candidate. Tax returns fail the test (must); always taking the unwanted voluntary job passes it. Correct for the known bias that people overestimate their own contribution.
**APPLY WHEN:** suspected over-compliance in relationships, family, volunteer roles, sexual life.
**AVOID WHEN:** saying no would be dangerous — safety supersedes (see ST:SAFETY-FIRST).
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** authors' heuristic; clinical experience.

### [ST:REFUSAL-SIM] Simulate the refusal and read the feeling
**Move:** Imagine concretely declining the task or asserting your preference, then read the response: strong anxiety points to the Vulnerable Child, guilt to a Dysfunctional Parent — either confirms a Compliant Surrender Mode.
**APPLY WHEN:** the behavior looks reasonable from outside and you cannot tell whether it is chosen.
**AVOID WHEN:** you are in an abusive relationship — imagined refusal there is a safety question, not a diagnostic.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:SURRENDER-SHEET] Two worked examples of surrender
**Move:** Worksheet 9 — for two personal examples: trigger situation and which mode fired, origin of the coping style, positive *and* negative consequences, and what someone else would do in the same situation. WS9a gives filled examples — over-polishing a text for a manager; absorbing all the housework.
**APPLY WHEN:** you have identified surrender and want alternatives, not just labels.
**AVOID WHEN:** the "someone else" column gets filled with an idealized person instead of a realistic peer.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** workbook exercise; clinical experience.

### [ST:AVOID-SHEET] Five-column avoidance map
**Move:** Worksheet 10 — my ways to avoid, typical situations, why I do it (the payoff), origins, and what my Healthy Adult would do; the last column is the lever. Remember the range: narrow avoidance, distraction (games, internet, nonstop work, excessive sport), stimulation (overeating, porn, gambling, risky sport), perception-dampening (alcohol, cannabis, benzodiazepines), chronic complaining that resists solutions, and setting no goals so as never to be disappointed.
**APPLY WHEN:** habits you dislike (drinking, snacking, gaming, complaining, no-showing) suspected of being emotion management.
**AVOID WHEN:** the alternative would mean confronting traumatic material or launching structured exposure solo.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** workbook exercise; clinical experience.

### [ST:CM-DETECT] Five detection questions for coping modes
**Move:** What feedback do friends and colleagues give; do different life contexts show the same pattern; how do you react to emotional distress; which Ch. 4 styles do you recognize; and — directly — ask a partner or friend which coping style they associate with you. Surrender and avoidance are relatively self-detectable; overcompensation is not, so outside data is primary there.
**APPLY WHEN:** starting coping work, especially where overcompensation is suspected.
**AVOID WHEN:** the informants are adversarial or dependent on you.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:CM-AGGR] Announce the mode, build the substitute protection first
**Move:** For aggressive overcompensation: name the mode out loud to people around you in advance, and at the first internal signal say — to them or to yourself — that anger is rising and you need a minute to breathe. Build the substitute protection *before* removing the old one: the aggression functions as protection against helplessness.
**APPLY WHEN:** escalation history with real-world consequences (withdrawal by others, a partner threatening to leave).
**AVOID WHEN:** the environment is genuinely unsafe and the aggression is protective; removing it with no replacement is explicitly warned against.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience (Carolyn case).

### [ST:FEEDBACK-DATA] Treat repeated outside feedback as data
**Move:** Take recurring external comments — "that was over the top", "you're spoilt", "your sulking is exhausting", egoistic, show-off, bossy, too loud — as data rather than attack; reconstruct the specific episode and look for a vulnerable feeling behind the dominant face. If you cannot recall an episode, ask a trusted friend to supply one. Authors' threshold: because people withdraw rather than confront, feedback that reaches you is under-reported, so more than two people saying the same thing probably means something.
**APPLY WHEN:** your own distress is low but others' is high, or the same feedback arrives from different life contexts.
**AVOID WHEN:** the source is abusive, has an interest in your compliance, is inside the conflict, or is a single hostile voice — a one-off complaint may mean nothing.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience; heuristic threshold, not a measured one.

### [ST:FRIEND-LENS] Watch the pattern in someone else
**Move:** Two directions of one trick. Outward: recall a specific episode where a friend, relative or colleague was in the mode; reconstruct their trigger, their real need, and your own reaction (compassion, helplessness, irritation) — that models what people feel around your version. Inward: ask how you would feel watching a good friend behave exactly as you do.
**APPLY WHEN:** self-observation stalls, self-justification is fluent, or you underrate the interpersonal cost of your mode.
**AVOID WHEN:** it slides into diagnosing other people to their face, or becomes a route to shame rather than clarity.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:AMBITION-DIFF] Demanding Parent vs. healthy ambition
**Move:** Box 3.2 differential — ambition is healthy if you still sleep, still rest, still see friends and are not stress-symptomatic. Suspect the mode when there is sleep disturbance, heavy rumination, binge eating, exhaustion, or pleasant need-meeting activities have vanished behind "I always have to work".
**APPLY WHEN:** high standards you are reluctant to call a problem.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to pathologize a temporary genuine crunch.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical heuristic; no cut-offs or validated thresholds offered.

### [ST:OFFER-EXIT-TEST] Offer the way out and watch
**Move:** To tell a Demanding Parent Mode from simple habit, offer a concrete way out of the extra task and observe: relief and uptake means no mode; a stream of reasons why *they specifically* must do more than everyone else means the mode.
**APPLY WHEN:** assessing chronic over-committers, including yourself via a proxy offer.
**AVOID WHEN:** turned into a test people are then told they failed.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:UNREACHED-SIGN] Positive messages that do not stick
**Move:** Read "praise and reassurance visibly fail to land" as the signature of a Punitive Parent Mode in another person — pushing harder produces frustration in you and sometimes devaluation of you by them. Reassurance and argument reliably fail against these modes; that is the diagnostic, not a sign you argued badly.
**APPLY WHEN:** you keep reassuring someone with no effect.
**AVOID WHEN:** taken as licence to withdraw — withdrawal completes their vicious circle.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:HEALTHY-ADULT-WINDOW] Raise the pattern only in Healthy Adult state
**Move:** Discuss a partner's or friend's mode with them only while they are in Healthy Adult state, never during the activated mode; confronting an overcompensator mid-mode reliably produces more overcompensation.
**APPLY WHEN:** you want to raise a recurring pattern in a close relationship.
**AVOID WHEN:** safety is at issue, or the other person has no interest in the frame.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** explicit instruction in the chapter.

### [ST:AC-ORIGINS] Check the four origins of anger and impulsivity
**Move:** Exercise 7.2 — test four origins: secondary to a Vulnerable Child; denied autonomy (clothes chosen for a teenager, interference in friendships, belittling in front of others); impulsive or aggressive role models; absent limits in childhood (a form of neglect, since setting limits is a parental duty).
**APPLY WHEN:** psychoeducational framing before attempting behavior change.
**AVOID WHEN:** it turns into a family indictment instead of a pattern map.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:AC-TRIGGERMAP] Find the common denominator across triggers
**Move:** Tabulate trigger situation → your actual reaction → the frustrated need, then look for what the rows share (in the case example, everything was family-related).
**APPLY WHEN:** before designing a behavior experiment.
**AVOID WHEN:** no restriction noted; needs several logged episodes to be informative.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:SC-LIMITPLAN] Limit plan for the spoilt/obstinate mode
**Move:** Pick the life domains to restrict first, write concrete and realistically difficult steps, pre-plan a reward per milestone (case: two previously-dumped tasks done weekly). The prescription is inverse to the vulnerable side — tolerate limits, delay gratification.
**APPLY WHEN:** partner or colleague feedback converges on selfishness or non-delivery.
**AVOID WHEN:** another mode (vulnerable child, a coping mode) has priority in the queue.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:HC-MENU] Prompt sheet for playfulness
**Move:** Use the book's eliciting list as a candidate generator after the bridge exercise — falling into leaves, loud off-key singing, racing children, walking in the rain, pillow fight, blanket cave, over-bubbled bath, playing with young animals — choosing for playfulness and curiosity, trying at least one new thing.
**APPLY WHEN:** generating candidates once the affective bridge has reopened access.
**AVOID WHEN:** it becomes a duty list policed by a Demanding Parent.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:HC-DOSE] Five dosing rules for Happy Child time
**Move:** Enough time (about an hour, not a ten-minute phone call); small steps; realistic frequency (fortnightly beats never); shared with other people, since child modes are happiest shared and long solo indulgence destabilizes family life; mood-sensitive — a forced slot on a terrible day gets swapped for Healthy Adult work.
**APPLY WHEN:** high-load lives with family and job.
**AVOID WHEN:** no restriction noted; the rules guard against both under- and over-dosing.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:BALANCE-CHECK] Ratio check between Healthy Adult and Happy Child
**Move:** Periodically check the ratio: is Healthy Adult the default with scheduled happy-child time, or has one side crowded out the other? A thin Happy Child under chronic load is a stated burnout and depression risk; permanent Happy Child is not the goal.
**APPLY WHEN:** either chronic joylessness or persistent escapist play.
**AVOID WHEN:** framed as another performance standard to fail.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:HA-WS18] Rate up to three intended changes on four dimensions
**Move:** Worksheet 18 "Changes" — for at most three intended changes rate importance, your influence over it, motivation, and willingness to invest effort (each 0–100), then state what you need, what would make the goal more concrete, and who else is affected. The Healthy Adult's job here is prioritization: which change, how much energy, what reward.
**APPLY WHEN:** too many change ambitions at once.
**AVOID WHEN:** no restriction noted; be honest about the effort rating or the plan is decorative.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:HA-CONCRETE] Convert a trait goal into observable behavior
**Move:** Unpack a vague goal: what does it look like, how would I recognize it in someone else, in which specific situations do I want it, what is the gap between my current and target behavior there. The authors state that goal specificity and closeness to actual behavior raise the probability of achieving it, so "be more self-confident" must become named behavior in named situations. Include realism about what cannot be changed (the difficult boss resolves to distance or a new job), and give people time to adjust to your change.
**APPLY WHEN:** goals phrased as traits ("more self-confident", "better balance").
**AVOID WHEN:** concreteness becomes a perfection standard.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience, stated as an observed predictor — not a measured effect.

### [ST:VC-ANCHOR] Recall the healing image in daily life
**Move:** When the vulnerable mode stirs during the day, recall the already-built comforting image — or its drawing, object or song — and let it prompt one concrete social step rather than ending in the image.
**APPLY WHEN:** the healing image already exists and reliably calms.
**AVOID WHEN:** used to suppress the feeling instead of then meeting the need — images alone do not heal.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** clinical experience (Keira case).

### [ST:PACED-READ] Work the book chapter by chapter with one confidant
**Move:** Work through chapter by chapter, pausing to think each one through against your own life before moving on; without a therapist in the loop, recruit one trusted person to discuss your patterns with.
**APPLY WHEN:** self-help use with no therapist involved.
**AVOID WHEN:** active serious mental illness — the authors state the book does not substitute for psychotherapy.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** authors' didactic judgment; no trial of the self-help format cited.

### [ST:LEVEL-SORT] Sort the work into cognitive / emotional / behavioral
**Move:** For any pattern you want to change, sort the planned work into the three levels and start at cognitive-plus-emotional, treating behavior change as the follow-on — imagery is named as a far stronger lever than thinking about the same content.
**APPLY WHEN:** you keep attempting behavior change and it does not stick.
**AVOID WHEN:** an acute safety issue is present — act on behavior and safety first.
**Tier:** SELF
**Evidence:** schema-therapy model; clinical experience.

### [ST:VC-RESCRIPT] Imagery rescripting of the childhood scene
**Move:** In imagery bring your adult self — or another caring figure — into the childhood scene to comfort and protect the little self, and let the encounter run until the child's need is *actually met*; then fix the result as a durable image, drawing, symbol or song. Rule for a healing image: the little self's needs must be met in it.
**APPLY WHEN:** contact with the vulnerable mode is established and tolerable.
**AVOID WHEN:** the scene is traumatic — this is rescripting, not visualization, and belongs in therapy.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** method-level trial support in schema therapy; no data for the solo version.

### [ST:VC-BRIDGE] Affective bridge from today's feeling to its origin
**Move:** Exercise 6.1 — relax, re-enter a recent situation carrying vulnerable-child feelings, intensify the emotion, erase the current scene and let childhood images surface; then compare the child feelings with today's. Mechanism: imagery run *from a feeling* reaches the biographical origin of that feeling, which makes today's triggers intelligible.
**APPLY WHEN:** a recurring disproportionate reaction whose origin is unclear.
**AVOID WHEN:** the likely origin is abuse, violence or loss; dissociation or self-harm risk; no support available afterwards.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** schema-therapy imagery method; no RCT of this book's self-help version.

### [ST:VC-LITTLESELF] The gentler meeting with the child self
**Move:** Exercise 6.2 — a 15-minute uninterrupted imagery walk on a country lane, meeting your roughly five-to-six-year-old self, greeting it at whatever distance feels right, exchanging something, saying goodbye, then grounding via contact with chair and floor; afterwards note what the meeting felt like, what the child needed, what you wanted to give it. Variants: visit the old home or other significant figures.
**APPLY WHEN:** re-living a distressing scene feels too risky — offered as the gentler entry.
**AVOID WHEN:** childhood scenes reliably trigger flooding; visiting abusive figures is not a solo exercise.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** clinical experience within the schema-therapy imagery tradition.

### [ST:CM-BRIDGE] Affective bridge for the origin of a coping style
**Move:** Exercise 10.1 — re-enter a situation where the coping mode was active (voice, words, body), build an affective bridge into childhood images, then reflect on the link between then and now.
**APPLY WHEN:** the origin of the coping style is unclear and mapping alone has not produced it.
**AVOID WHEN:** the childhood material is traumatic.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** schema-therapy imagery method; no book-level outcome data.

### [ST:PM-HELPER] Inner helper who answers back
**Move:** Exercise 9.3 — choose an inner helper (a real person past or present, or a figure who accepts your needs), narrate the current self-blaming situation to them internally and *wait for the reply*; test it — if it is not accepting and warm, it is the Punitive Mode wearing the helper's face. Only if replies are genuinely supportive, take the further step of letting the helper enter the punitive scene in imagery.
**APPLY WHEN:** a strong punitive voice blocks the written exercises.
**AVOID WHEN:** the scene involves abuse or punishment memories; if helper replies keep turning punitive, the authors route to psychotherapy.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** clinical experience; imagery step carries the Ch. 6 rescripting caution.

### [ST:VC-LETTER] Letter to your younger self
**Move:** Write a letter to your younger self naming what was missing then and what you commit to providing now.
**APPLY WHEN:** contact with the child mode is already established and tolerable.
**AVOID WHEN:** the content would require re-entering abuse memories.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** clinical experience (Maddison case).

### [ST:VC-MEMORABILIA] Sensory route via physical mementos
**Move:** Use photos, toys, letters and smells as sensory routes into childhood feeling, then trace which present-day situations evoke that same feeling.
**APPLY WHEN:** imagery alone produces nothing.
**AVOID WHEN:** the objects are tied to abuse or bereavement.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** clinical experience.

### [ST:SAFETY-FIRST] Protection overrides pattern work in a violent relationship
**Move:** Before working on surrender in a violent relationship, switch the goal from need-expression to protection — the book's example is a shelter for battered women. Asserting needs toward a violent partner is named as likely to escalate.
**APPLY WHEN:** any current physical danger, intimidation, or a partner with a history of assault.
**AVOID WHEN:** never skip it — this overrides all pattern-change work in the book.
**Tier:** REQUIRES-THERAPIST
**Evidence:** explicit clinical warning; specialist or protective services, not a worksheet.

## What the book debunks

- **"Strong feelings mean a mode."** The criterion is disproportion *plus* uncontrollability. Irritation when hungry, tired or genuinely wronged is not an Angry Child Mode; ordinary procrastination is not a mode.
- **"Dysfunctional Parent means the parents did it."** Grandparents, siblings, teachers, coaches or classmates can install it, and parents may have been loving in most respects and damaging in one narrow area. Demanding-Parent intentions were often good — the praise/demand balance failed.
- **"Feeling fine means the mode is functional."** Overcompensation feels good from the inside, which is why it is missed; spoilt and impulsive modes compound quiet costs (debt, failed courses, relationship erosion) while subjective distress stays low. The signal arrives from others.
- **"Silence around me is consent."** Confronting an overcompensator usually produces more overcompensation, so people go quiet and withdraw — which re-confirms the underlying loneliness and re-fuels the mode.
- **"All coping is pathological."** These are defense mechanisms; every healthy person needs some. Switching off during a boss's tantrum is the healthy version. The line is blocked needs or obligations.
- **"Erase the coping mode."** Goal is weakening; every coping mode has a function, and aggressive overcompensation needs a substitute protection built before the old one goes.
- **"Just prescribe fun."** Activity prescription fails for a suppressed Happy Child — the affective bridge must reopen access first. Permanent Happy Child is explicitly rejected as a goal.
- **"Be in Healthy Adult all the time."** Named unrealistic; modes overlap and a clean single-mode reading is often unavailable.
- **Documented failure modes.** (1) Deep imagery on Parent-Mode scenes of belittlement, punishment or abuse can trigger a mood collapse the person cannot exit unaided — hence Ch. 9 reverses Ch. 6's imagery permission. (2) A strong Punitive Mode may mock or escalate against the exercises: an indication for professional help, not more effort. (3) Chair dialogues fail at peak arousal. (4) Reducing submissive behavior irritates those who benefited — the fear of criticism is initially *realistic*, and the loop breaks only if that phase is expected. (5) Dropping avoidance costs high distress up front; dropping overcompensation is worse — the person meets the underlying inferiority and may get depressed or anxious first. (6) Exposing mobbing can offend perpetrators and worsen the victim's position.
- **Misreadings to catch.** Complaining that resists solutions is avoidance in the costume of distress; so are over-engaging with details, nonstop work, excessive sport. Reassurance and argument reliably fail against Child and Punitive modes. Not every assertive or controlling person is compensating; a single situational complaint may mean nothing. Book's negative control: a stretched working mother who hires help and asks for support is stressed, not surrendering. Parent-mode relapse during ordinary life crises is expected, not failure.

## Numbers that matter

- **5** categories of basic emotional needs (Young, Klosko & Weishaar, 2006) — the yardstick for every mode judgment.
- **3** child mode types; **3** Dysfunctional Parent types; **3** coping styles; **6** angry/impulsive sub-flavours (anger, rage, defiance, impulsivity, pamperedness, lack of discipline); **6** overcompensatory sub-patterns (Box 4.3); **6** avoidant behavior categories (Box 4.2).
- **8** features of interpersonal dependency (Box 4.1, after American Psychiatric Association, 2000); **8** Healthy Adult features (Box 5.1); **6** functional/dysfunctional contrast rows (Table 5.1); **13** Healthy Adult activities (Box 11.1); **15** items on the Happy Child prompt list; **5** anger-control tactics; **4** biographical origins of anger/impulsivity (Ex. 7.2).
- **Screening item counts:** 4 vulnerable child, 4 angry/impulsive, 4 happy child, 4/3/4 demanding / guilt-inducing / punitive, 5 surrender, 4 avoidance, 5 overcompensation.
- **0–100 ratings:** WS11 intensity per overcompensatory pattern; WS13 message impact today (worked example: 85); WS18 across four dimensions (importance, influence, motivation, willingness to invest).
- **Worksheets 1–19** plus filled examples 9a and 10a; the book is marketed on 20 worksheets.
- **Dosing:** Exercise 6.2 budgets 15 undisturbed minutes; imagined child self aged about 5–6; Happy Child time ~1 hour, with 10 minutes named as too short; fortnightly beats never.
- **Thresholds:** more than two people giving similar feedback = take it seriously; people overestimate their own contribution in a relationship by roughly **50 percent** (glossary, attributed to social-psychology studies, no citation given) — so feeling you do two-thirds to three-quarters of the work often describes an even split; selective perception illustrated as noticing **2** chatting people out of **50** interested listeners.
- **Case anchors** (recognition, not protocol): Carol 34, Sophia 34 (mother died when she was 2, stepmother when she was 16), Steven 38, Marjorie lived with parents until 24, Matthew 41, Susie 21, Freddie 28, Julia 28 (borderline personality disorder), Lydia 14, Sebastian never placed better than 6th–7th nationally, Daniel humiliated at school ~20 years earlier, an overcompensation case running 20–30 years in a person aged 47.

## Blind spots of this book

- **No outcome data of its own.** No RCT of this workbook, no effect size for any exercise. Method-level support exists for imagery rescripting, chair dialogue, behavior experiments and decisional balance inside schema therapy and CBT, but nothing validates the *self-help delivery* — cite this book for how to run a technique, never as evidence that it works.
- **Screening items are not instruments.** The agree/disagree sets and 0–100 ratings have no psychometrics, cut-offs or norms; the authors downgrade them to hints. Every number here is a self-report artefact.
- **Heuristics presented as thresholds.** "More than two people", the ~50 percent contribution-overestimation figure and the goal-specificity claim are clinical impressions or uncited social-psychology folklore.
- **Partly unfalsifiable.** Modes overlap and the model explains any behavior after the fact; there is no procedure for concluding that a candidate mode is *absent*.
- **Structural and material causes are invisible.** Everything routes to childhood need histories and internalized voices; poverty, discrimination, workload, illness, medication and neurodivergence appear only as case background. Someone burning out under an objectively impossible job gets handed a Demanding Parent frame.
- **Western-clinical family assumptions.** Developmental routes, family scenarios and the "good parent" standard assume one social form; collectivist obligation norms and migration contexts are unaddressed.
- **The self-help/therapy boundary is asserted, not operationalized.** No front-end screening catches the reader who should not do Ch. 6 alone — the boundary is discovered by hitting it.
- **Trauma cases illustrate mechanism only** — the book supplies no trauma protocol, and those cases must not be read as solo procedures.
- **Nothing on maintenance, dose or duration.** No guidance on how long the work takes, how often to revisit worksheets, or what a plateau means; drop-out and adverse-effect rates for solo use are unaddressed.

## Tier boundary

Hard stops regardless of which move is running, taken from the book's own routing: a mood collapse after Parent-Mode imagery the person cannot exit; a Punitive Mode that strengthens under challenge; inner-helper replies that keep turning punitive; no access to a Healthy Adult perspective at all. Plus its carve-outs: abuse, violence or loss surfacing in any memory channel; self-harm urges or dissociation; physiological dependence (alcohol, benzodiazepines), where stopping is a medical matter; and current violence in a relationship, where protection replaces pattern work entirely.
